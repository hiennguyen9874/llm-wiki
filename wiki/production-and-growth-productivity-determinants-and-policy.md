# Production and Growth — Productivity, its Determinants, and Long-Run Growth Policy

Long-run living standards are determined by productivity — output per hour worked — which in turn depends on physical capital per worker, human capital per worker, natural resources per worker, and technological knowledge; cross-country gaps and growth rates varied dramatically (1870–2014 US $4,264→$55,860 at 1.80%/yr, Japan 2.59%, Brazil 2.61%, UK 1.42%) and are explained by differences in these determinants rather than short-run fluctuations[^mankiw-ch12]. The aggregate production function $Y=A F(L,K,H,N)$ with constant returns to scale clarifies that $Y/L = A F(1,K/L,H/L,N/L)$[^mankiw-ch12], and policy to raise $Y/L$ works through saving/investment facing diminishing returns and a catch-up effect, plus openness to foreign investment, education with externalities and brain-drain risks, health/nutrition virtuous circles, property rights and political stability, outward-oriented free trade, and support for research and development, while population growth creates both dilution of capital and a scale effect on technological progress[^mankiw-ch12][^mankiw-ch12-040].

## Trend growth versus the business cycle

**Long-run economic growth** is the sustained upward trend in an economy's output; per-capita growth is the relevant trend for higher wages and living standards.[^krugman-ch6-037] It differs from the short-run business cycle: the source cautions that models appropriate for long-run growth need not give the same policy implications in a recession. In particular, it contrasts the short-run paradox of thrift with the longer-run role of saving in supporting growth.[^krugman-ch6-037]

The chapter's Canada–Argentina comparison illustrates that similar starting conditions need not imply similar growth paths. It reports comparable per-capita incomes into the 1930s, then sustained Canadian growth after 1930 and Argentine underperformance after World War II, associating the latter with political instability and poor macroeconomic policies.[^krugman-ch6-037] This is historical illustration, not a complete account of either country's development.

## Blanchard Ch. 10 — facts, convergence, and the Solow primer

Over very long periods, trend growth dominates even severe business-cycle contractions: Blanchard's 1890–2017 U.S. log-scale chart reports real output rising about 51-fold and output per person about tenfold, with the Great Depression, 1980–82 recession, and Great Recession visibly small relative to the trend.[^blanchard-034] The associated historical account characterizes Europe from the end of the Roman Empire to about 1500 as a Malthusian era of near-zero output-per-person growth; sustained modern per-person growth is historically recent.[^blanchard-035]

Cross-country convergence is **conditional and uneven**, rather than a historical necessity. Among today's OECD members, countries starting with lower 1950 output per person generally grew faster through 2017; but selecting a club of later economic winners can exaggerate this pattern. A cohort defined by a 1950 income threshold still shows broad convergence with exceptions such as Uruguay and Venezuela.[^blanchard-035] Across 85 countries from 1960–2017, there is no comparable all-country relationship: OECD and many Asian economies show catch-up, whereas many African countries did not, often amid conflict. These are descriptive source-era patterns, not a causal explanation of regional outcomes.[^blanchard-035]

The Solow-style starting point is $Y=F(K,N)$. With constant returns to scale, $F(xK,xN)=xY$, so setting $x=1/N$ gives $Y/N=F(K/N,1)$: output per worker depends on capital per worker.[^blanchard-035] Diminishing returns mean moving along this curve via capital accumulation produces successively smaller gains. Technological progress instead shifts the curve upward, producing more output per worker at a given capital-worker ratio.[^blanchard-035][^blanchard-036] Thus a higher saving rate can raise the long-run **level** of output per person, but capital accumulation alone cannot sustain its growth rate indefinitely; sustained per-person growth requires sustained technological progress.[^blanchard-036]

### Human capital and the endogenous-growth qualification

Blanchard extends the production relation to $Y/N=f(K/N,H/N)$: physical capital and workers' skills both raise output per worker, and education and training accumulate the latter.[^blanchard-041] The text treats relative wages as a practical human-capital weighting scheme because they are assumed to reflect relative marginal products, but flags that wage differences unrelated to productivity make this measure contestable.[^blanchard-041] It reports a qualified consensus that raising physical investment or education/training can materially raise the long-run **level** of output per worker, given technological progress, without permanently raising its growth rate. Endogenous-growth models ask whether joint accumulation can alter that conclusion; the source says the issue remains unsettled rather than established.[^blanchard-041]

## Krugman/Wells Ch. 9 — measurement, compounding, and productivity

**Real GDP per capita** (real GDP divided by population) is the textbook's summary statistic for long-run economic progress: using real GDP removes price-level changes, while the per-capita adjustment distinguishes more aggregate output from a higher average material standard of living. The source cautions that it is not a policy goal in itself.[^krugman-ch9-048]

- In its Maddison-based series, US real GDP per capita rose 848% from 1900 to 2016 (just over eightfold), an average 1.9% annually. The Rule of 70 implies a doubling in about 37 years at that rate; three doublings explain the approximate eightfold rise. The rule applies only to positive growth rates.[^krugman-ch9-048]
- A growth rate is a rate of change, not the dollar change in output, and a high rate does not remove an initially lower level. The source's 1980–2018 comparison reports average annual per-capita growth of 7.8% in China, 4.1% in India, 3.7% in Ireland, 3.0% in Bangladesh, 1.6% in the United States, 1.3% in France, 0.5% in Argentina, and −0.4% in Venezuela.[^krugman-ch9-048]
- Productivity is output per worker (or sometimes per hour); sustained per-capita growth requires it to rise. A higher employment share can temporarily raise output per person, but employment growth normally tracks population growth over the long run. For the twentieth-century United States, the source attributes 1.7 percentage points of 1.9% annual per-capita growth to productivity.[^krugman-ch9-048]
- The source identifies physical capital (human-made equipment and structures), human capital (workers' education and knowledge), and technological progress (better production methods) as the immediate sources of productivity growth. It notes that economically consequential innovation can be incremental and widely adopted rather than confined to high-technology firms.[^krugman-ch9-048]
- As a descriptive case, Bangladesh's real GDP per capita tripled from 1990 to 2019 amid political and market reforms, macroeconomic stabilization, and clothing-export growth; the source also reports improved life expectancy, child mortality, and school enrollment. This juxtaposition is not causal identification and does not erase continued low wages and unsafe working conditions.[^krugman-ch9-048]

## Krugman/Wells Ch. 9 — aggregate production, growth accounting, and policy

### Separating inputs from technology

The source represents labor productivity with an **aggregate production function**: real GDP per worker depends on physical capital per worker, human capital per worker, and technology. This is an analytical, hypothetical relationship used to distinguish their respective contributions.[^krugman-ch9-049] One 2015 application to the United States, India, and China specified $y=T K^{1/3}H^{2/3}$, with human capital related to schooling years; it attributed most of China's 1979–2008 relative advance to the technology term $T$. This is a result of that study and specification, not a general causal decomposition.[^krugman-ch9-049]

- Holding human capital and technology fixed, more physical capital raises output per worker but at a diminishing rate. The source illustrates equal $20,000 capital increments producing successively smaller productivity gains ($30,000, $20,000, then $10,000). Diminishing returns mean marginal gains shrink; they do **not** mean added capital reduces productivity.[^krugman-ch9-049]
- The ceteris-paribus qualification is essential: simultaneous gains in training or technology can change the productivity schedule. Typical estimates reported by the text associate a 1% rise in capital per worker with about a 0.33% rise in output per worker, all else equal.[^krugman-ch9-049]
- Technological progress shifts the entire production function upward: it permits more output with the same quantities of capital, human capital, and labor.[^krugman-ch9-049]

**Growth accounting** assigns observed productivity growth to the input contributions implied by such a production function; the remaining residual is **total factor productivity (TFP)**, the output attainable from a given amount of factor inputs.[^krugman-ch9-049] The residual is interpreted here as technological progress, but it is an accounting remainder rather than direct measurement of every mechanism behind it. For US nonfarm business from 1948–2019, the cited BLS estimates report 2.1% annual labor-productivity growth, about 49% explained by increased physical and human capital per worker and the remainder by rising TFP.[^krugman-ch9-049]

### Technology, resources, and growth differences

The text labels the gap between conspicuous information technology and weak measured productivity growth the **productivity paradox**. Its BLS chart reports average US TFP growth of 1.9% (1948–1973), 0.4% (1974–1995), 1.6% (1996–2005), and 0.4% (2006–2018). It offers no settled explanation; retail inventory-management adoption is presented as one contributor to the 1996–2005 rebound.[^krugman-ch9-049]

### Barcode diffusion as complementary investment

The source uses bar-code scanning to illustrate that productivity-relevant innovation can be incremental and delayed by **complementarities** rather than a lack of technical value. Scanners first entered commercial use in 1974, but retailers needed scanners and information systems while manufacturers needed product-labeling equipment; each waited for the other side to invest. Only about one-third of US supermarkets adopted scanners in the first decade.[^krugman-ch9-051]

Once complementary investments spread, machine-readable labels supported inventory and sales tracking, restocking, warehousing, and labor management. The source reports estimates that bar-code adoption reduced grocery-retailing labor costs by as much as 40%, and identifies retailing as a leading contributor to US productivity growth in 1995–2005.[^krugman-ch9-051] It also attributes slower European adoption partly to land-use policies that protected small stores unable to afford the technology. These are illustrative historical claims, not an estimate of the causal effect of any contemporary retail technology.[^krugman-ch9-051]

Natural-resource abundance can raise productivity, but the source treats it as less important than human and physical capital for most modern economies; it contrasts oil-based UAE wealth with resource-poor Japan, and resource-rich but poor Nigeria. Malthus's land-constrained stagnation prediction may describe much of pre-eighteenth-century history, but subsequent technological change, capital accumulation, and new cultivable land outweighed population pressure. Finite resources and environmental absorption remain limits the text says are addressed elsewhere, not resolved by this account.[^krugman-ch9-049]

Countries with rapid growth commonly combine high investment financed by domestic or foreign saving, education expansion, and R&D and adoption of technology. The source reports China's 2019 investment share as 43% of GDP versus 21% in the United States, and its adult literacy rising from about 66% in 1980 to 93% in 2018, compared with Argentina's roughly 94% to 96%. These are descriptive comparisons, not sufficient to identify the effect of any one policy.[^krugman-ch9-049]

### Government channels in the source

The source identifies direct support for **infrastructure** (including roads, power, ports, information networks, clean water, and disease control), education, and R&D, plus indirect support for a well-regulated financial system that lets trusted deposits become productive lending. It frames these as channels through which government can promote—or obstruct—the accumulation and application of physical capital, human capital, and technology.[^krugman-ch9-049] It gives ARPANET and subsequent National Science Foundation extension as an example of public R&D contribution, while emphasizing that much R&D is privately financed.[^krugman-ch9-049]

## Krugman/Wells Ch. 9 — institutions and uneven development

**Property rights** are legal rights to dispose of valuable property; **intellectual property rights** let innovators receive the returns from innovations. The source presents credible protection as an innovation incentive: copying may sometimes be intrinsically difficult, but governments generally supply protection. A **patent** is a government-created, temporary monopoly over use or sale, balancing an innovator's incentive against the later social benefit of competition.[^krugman-ch9-050]

Stable, impartially enforced law is likewise presented as a precondition for saving and business investment: honest courts and police, predictable laws, and protection from corruption or arbitrary seizure make returns more secure. The source uses India’s reduction of burdensome business restrictions and associated bribery after 1990 as an illustrative contributor to its subsequent growth, not causal proof. It also cautions that even non-corrupt intervention can weaken productivity when wasteful subsidies, import protection, unnecessary monopoly, or insulation from competition remove incentives.[^krugman-ch9-050]

### Catch-up is conditional, not automatic

The source’s regional examples qualify a mechanical reading of convergence. South Korea’s real GDP per capita rose about 7% annually for over 30 years beginning in the early 1960s; the broader East Asian region averaged about 6% annually since 1975. The text associates this with high saving/investment, widespread basic education, technological progress, and adoption of technologies already developed in richer economies. This supports a **convergence hypothesis**—countries beginning poorer tend to grow faster and income gaps tend to narrow—but not a guarantee: the relevant conditions must also be present.[^krugman-ch9-050]

- In the text’s comparison, Latin America combined lower saving/investment and basic education with inflation, bank failures, political instability, and difficulty adopting external technology. It reports that market-opening reforms did not generally produce an East-Asian-scale surge; Chile is identified as the one sustained-rapid-growth exception in its account.[^krugman-ch9-050]
- For sub-Saharan Africa, the source links civil war, instability, corruption, insecure property, and inadequate public-health infrastructure to inhibited investment and low productivity. It records a competing view, associated with Jeffrey Sachs, that adverse geography, disease, and poor soil cause poverty and thereby instability. Neither direction is resolved by the source; these mechanisms can reinforce one another.[^krugman-ch9-050]
- Italy illustrates that earlier success does not ensure persistence: its real GDP per capita quadrupled from 1950 to 1990, then stagnated after the late 1990s and fell after 2008. The text connects slow factors-of-production growth, weak education, declining measured TFP since 2000, seniority-based management rewards, and weak competition to its performance; this is a proposed explanation, not a causal estimate.[^krugman-ch9-050]

### Practice-data checks: compounding and convergence

The source's problem data provide calculations that distinguish growth rates from relative income levels. Starting from the same $20,000 real GDP per capita, 100 years at 2.0% yields about $144,893, whereas 1.5% yields about $88,641—about 1.63 times lower—under constant annual compounding.[^krugman-ch9-052] This is a mechanical projection, not a forecast.

Its World Bank tables also illustrate that convergence is uneven: France rose from 72.6% to 80.0% of US real GDP per capita from 1960 to 2018 and Japan from 49.0% to 89.6%; the United Kingdom was approximately unchanged at 79.4%. Over the same endpoints, Argentina fell from 32.2% to 19.1% of the US level, Ghana from 6.0% to 3.3%, and South Korea rose from 5.4% to 49.0%.[^krugman-ch9-052] These ratios are calculations from the source's dollar figures; they show relative movement, not a causal explanation or complete catch-up.

## 12-2 Productivity: its role and determinants

### Why productivity is so important

- Robinson Crusoe model: stranded sailor catching fish, growing vegetables, making clothes; standard of living = productivity (quantity of goods/services per unit labor input)[^mankiw-ch12]. More fish per hour → eats more or fishes less and makes other goods.
- For nations: GDP measures both total income and total expenditure; they are equal because income is output[^mankiw-ch12]. Nation lives well only if it produces much; Americans vs Nigerians, Japanese vs Argentine growth gaps explained by productivity[^mankiw-ch12].
- Restates Ten Principles: country's standard of living depends on its ability to produce goods and services (Principle 8)[^mankiw-ch12].

### Four determinants of productivity

1. **Physical capital per worker (K/L)** — stock of equipment and structures; produced factor of production (output in the past that is now input, e.g., lathe making table legs was itself manufactured)[^mankiw-ch12]. More/better tools → faster, more accurate output (woodworker with hand tools vs specialized machinery)[^mankiw-ch12].
2. **Human capital per worker (H/L)** — knowledge and skills via education, training, experience (early childhood, grade school, high school, college, on-the-job)[^mankiw-ch12]. Less tangible but similarly raises productive ability and is a produced factor requiring teachers, libraries, student time; students are "workers" producing human capital for future use[^mankiw-ch12].
3. **Natural resources per worker (N/L)** — inputs provided by nature: land, rivers, mineral deposits; renewable (forest with replanting) vs nonrenewable (oil, millions of years to form, fixed supply)[^mankiw-ch12]. Can explain some income differences — US agricultural land historically, Kuwait/Saudi Arabia oil today — but not necessary: Japan is rich despite few natural resources via importing resources and exporting manufactures[^mankiw-ch12].
4. **Technological knowledge (A)** — society's understanding of best ways to produce; examples: farming technology allowing small farm share to feed entire country, Ford assembly line (common knowledge once observed) vs Coca-Cola recipe (proprietary long-run) vs pharmaceutical patent (temporary proprietary)[^mankiw-ch12].

- **Distinction:** Technological knowledge = quality of society's textbooks; human capital = time population has spent reading them; both matter and are related but separate[^mankiw-ch12]. Definition: *technological knowledge* is society's understanding of the best ways to produce goods and services[^mankiw-ch12].

### FYI — The production function

- $Y = A F(L,K,H,N)$ where $F$ combines inputs; $A$ reflects technology — higher $A$ → more output from same inputs[^mankiw-ch12].
- Many production functions have constant returns to scale: doubling all inputs doubles output: $xY = A F(xL, xK, xH, xN)$ for any $x>0$[^mankiw-ch12].
- Setting $x=1/L$: $Y/L = A F(1, K/L, H/L, N/L)$ — labor productivity depends on physical capital per worker, human capital per worker, natural resources per worker, and technology $A$ — mathematical summary of four determinants[^mankiw-ch12].

### Are natural resources a limit to growth?

- Debate: population >7bn (>4× a century ago) with higher living standards — will fixed nonrenewable supplies choke future growth?[^mankiw-ch12]
- Economists generally less worried because technology conserves and substitutes: better gas mileage, insulation, efficient oil rigs, recycling, ethanol for gasoline[^mankiw-ch12].
- Historical example: 70 years ago tin (food containers) and copper (telephone wire) were feared scarce, prompting calls for mandatory recycling/rationing; plastics replaced tin, fiber-optic (sand) replaced copper[^mankiw-ch12].
- Market price test: if running out, inflation-adjusted prices should rise long-run; fact is prices show substantial short-run fluctuations but are stable or falling over long spans — conservation ability outpaces depletion[^mankiw-ch12]. Prices give no reason to believe resources limit growth[^mankiw-ch12].

## 12-3 Economic growth and public policy

Goal: what can government do to raise productivity and living standards given the four determinants[^mankiw-ch12].

### Saving and investment

- Capital is produced, so society can change its amount: producing more new capital goods today → larger capital stock tomorrow → more future goods/services[^mankiw-ch12].
- Trade-off (Principle 1): resources scarce, more capital requires less current consumption-goods production; society must consume less and save more now to enjoy higher future consumption — growth from capital accumulation is not a free lunch[^mankiw-ch12].
- Financial markets coordinate saving and investment (detailed next chapter); encouraging saving/investment is one growth lever, raising long-run standard of living[^mankiw-ch12].

### Diminishing returns and the catch-up effect

- *Diminishing returns* — benefit from an extra unit of input declines as quantity of input increases[^mankiw-ch12].
- Traditional view: capital subject to diminishing returns; with workers already well-equipped, extra capital raises productivity only slightly[^mankiw-ch12].
- Figure 1 (output per worker vs capital per worker, holding other determinants constant) — curve rises but flattens as $K/L$ increases because of diminishing returns[^mankiw-ch12].
- Implication: higher saving rate → higher capital, productivity, faster GDP growth only for a while; benefits shrink, growth slows, and long-run outcome is higher *level* of productivity/income, not permanently higher *growth rate*; reaching long run can take several decades per international studies[^mankiw-ch12].
- *Catch-up effect* — countries that start off poor tend to grow more rapidly than countries that start off rich, other things equal (conditional on investment share etc.)[^mankiw-ch12].
- Logic: poor-country workers lack rudimentary tools, so small capital investments substantially raise productivity; rich-country workers already capital-rich, so extra capital has small marginal effect; data confirm: controlling for GDP share devoted to investment, poor countries grow faster[^mankiw-ch12].
- Example 1960–1990: US and South Korea devoted similar GDP share to investment, yet US ~2%/yr vs South Korea >6%/yr; explanation is catch-up — South Korea in 1960 had GDP per person <1/10 US with small initial capital stock, so benefits larger[^mankiw-ch12].
- Analogy: "Most Improved" student usually started poor; easier to improve from low base than to add to already-high performance; being Best Student still better than Most Improved[^mankiw-ch12].

### Investment from abroad

- Domestic saving not only source; foreigners can invest.
- **Foreign direct investment** — capital investment owned and operated by foreign entity (e.g., Ford builds car factory in Mexico)[^mankiw-ch12].
- **Foreign portfolio investment** — investment financed with foreign money but operated by domestic residents (e.g., American buys stock in Mexican corporation which builds factory)[^mankiw-ch12]. In both, American saving finances Mexican capital[^mankiw-ch12].
- Foreign investment expects return: increases Mexican GDP (production in Mexico) but part of income accrues to nonresidents as profit, so Mexican GNP (income of Mexicans) rises less than GDP[^mankiw-ch12]. Recall GDP = income earned within country by residents and nonresidents; GNP = income earned by residents at home and abroad[^mankiw-ch12].
- Benefits remain: raises capital stock → higher productivity and wages, and transfers state-of-the-art technologies from richer countries; many advisors recommend removing restrictions on foreign ownership to encourage inflows[^mankiw-ch12].
- World Bank (with IMF, created after WWII on lesson that economic distress breeds political turmoil and war) channels funds from advanced countries (e.g., US) as loans for roads, sewer systems, schools, and advice; US and others fund to promote global prosperity[^mankiw-ch12].

### Education

- Investment in human capital at least as important as physical capital[^mankiw-ch12].
- US: each extra year schooling raises wage ~10% historically; gap larger in less developed countries where human capital scarce[^mankiw-ch12].
- Policy: provide good schools and encourage attendance to raise living standards[^mankiw-ch12].
- Opportunity cost: students forgo wages while in school; in LDCs children drop out early despite high return because family needs labor support[^mankiw-ch12].
- **Positive externalities:** educated person generates ideas entering society's pool usable by everyone — social return exceeds private return, justifying large public subsidies to education; externality = effect of one person's actions on bystander well-being[^mankiw-ch12].
- **Brain drain:** emigration of most highly educated workers to rich countries for higher living standards; if human capital has positive externalities, stayers become poorer; dilemma — poor countries need best foreign universities but risk those students not returning, further reducing human-capital stock[^mankiw-ch12].

### Health and nutrition

- Human capital also includes health investments: healthier workers more productive; right health investments raise productivity and living standards[^mankiw-ch12].
- Robert Fogel (Nobel 1993, with slavery/railroads work) research: better nutrition important for long-run growth[^mankiw-ch12]. In Great Britain 1780, ~1 in 5 so malnourished they were incapable of manual labor; even able workers had reduced effort from insufficient calories[^mankiw-ch12].
- Height as proxy (malnutrition in gestation/early life → short stature): 1775–1975 British caloric intake +26% and average male height +3.6 inches; 1962–1995 South Korea calories +44% and male height +2 inches; genetic change too slow, so environmental/nutrition explanation[^mankiw-ch12].
- Contemporary cross-section: taller workers earn more (wages reflect productivity), especially pronounced in poorer countries where malnutrition risk higher[^mankiw-ch12].
- Fogel's Prize lecture conclusion: improved gross nutrition accounts for roughly 30% of growth of per capita income in Britain between 1790 and 1980[^mankiw-ch12].
- Today malnutrition rare in developed nations (obesity more common), but remains obstacle in developing world — UN estimates ~1/3 of sub-Saharan Africa is undernourished[^mankiw-ch12].
- Causality both ways: poor → unhealthy (cannot afford healthcare/nutrition) and unhealthy → poor (low productivity); vicious circle but also possible virtuous circle — growth improves health, which further promotes growth[^mankiw-ch12].

### 12-3f Property rights and political stability

- Another growth lever is protecting property rights and promoting political stability — goes to heart of how market economies work[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Production involves millions of individuals and firms (car dealer, manufacturer, steel company, iron ore mining company) using division of labor to use factors effectively; coordination achieved through market prices — the invisible hand balances supply and demand in thousands of markets[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Prerequisite: economy-wide respect for property rights — ability of people to exercise authority over resources they own[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Mining company will not mine ore if it expects ore to be stolen; it mines only if confident it will benefit from sale.
- Courts enforce rights: criminal justice discourages theft; civil justice ensures buyers and sellers live up to contracts[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Developed countries tend to take rights for granted; less developed countries often have poor justice systems — contracts hard to enforce, fraud goes unpunished; in extreme cases government itself infringes rights and firms must bribe officials[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Corruption impedes coordinating power of markets and discourages domestic saving and foreign investment.
- Threat is political instability: when revolutions and coups are common, doubt about future respect for rights[^mankiw-ch12-040]. If revolutionary government might confiscate capital as after communist revolutions, domestic residents have less incentive to save, invest, start businesses and foreigners have less incentive to invest; even threat of revolution depresses living standards.
- Therefore economic prosperity depends in part on political prosperity: country with efficient courts, honest officials, stable constitution enjoys higher living standard than one with poor courts, corrupt officials, frequent revolutions/coups[^mankiw-ch12-040].

### 12-3g Free trade

- Some poorest countries have tried inward-oriented policies — avoiding interaction with rest of world — with infant-industry argument that domestic firms need protection from foreign competition to thrive, plus general distrust of foreigners, leading to tariffs and trade restrictions[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Most economists today favor outward-oriented policies integrating countries into world economy[^mankiw-ch12-040]. International trade in goods and services can improve well-being; trade is a type of technology — when country exports wheat and imports textiles, it benefits as if it invented technology for turning wheat into textiles; eliminating trade restrictions produces same kind of growth as major technological advance.
- Adverse impact of inward orientation clear when considering small size of many less developed economies: Argentina's GDP is about that of Houston, Texas — if Houston council prohibited trading outside city limits, Houston would need to produce all it consumes and all its capital goods rather than importing state-of-the-art equipment, living standards would fall immediately and worsen over time; this is what happened when Argentina pursued inward policies through much of 20th century, in contrast to outward-oriented South Korea, Singapore, Taiwan which enjoyed high growth[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Trade volume also determined by geography: countries with natural seaports find trade easier; major cities like New York, San Francisco, Hong Kong are oceanside not coincidentally[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Landlocked countries find trade harder and tend to have lower incomes: countries with >80% population within 100 km of coast have average GDP per person about four times as large as countries with <20% near coast; importance of sea access helps explain why Africa, with many landlocked countries, is so poor[^mankiw-ch12-040].

### 12-3h Research and development

- Primary reason living standards higher today than a century ago is technological knowledge advance — telephone, transistor, computer, internal combustion engine among thousands of innovations[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Most advances come from private research by firms and individual inventors, but there is public interest because knowledge is a public good: once one person discovers an idea, it enters society's pool and others can freely use it[^mankiw-ch12-040]. As government provides national defense (public good), it has role in encouraging R&D.
- US government examples: a century ago sponsored farming research and advised farmers; more recently Air Force and NASA supported aerospace research making US leading maker of rockets and planes; continues with National Science Foundation and National Institutes of Health research grants and tax breaks for firms doing R&D[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Patent system encourages research: inventor of innovative product such as new drug can apply for patent; if deemed truly original, government awards exclusive right to make product for specified number of years[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Patent gives inventor property right over invention, turning idea from public good into private good; by allowing inventors to profit even only temporarily, system enhances incentive to engage in research.

### In the News — Curmudgeon versus Optimist (Timothy Aeppel, WSJ June 16 2014)

- Contrast of Northwestern colleagues Robert Gordon (73, curmudgeon, macroeconomist) who believes best days over and human progress slowing to crawl versus Joel Mokyr (67, optimist, economic historian) who imagines coming age of gene therapies, miracle seeds without fertilizers, robotic manufacturing, 3-D printing, cloud computing[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Gordon: rate of innovation not getting faster; no evidence; biggest breakthroughs behind us — electrification, antibiotics, 19th–20th century instant communication/long-distance travel spawned hundreds of industries; recent inventions like Internet won't pack same punch; cellphones just refinement of telephone; ideal kitchen 1955 not that different from today versus clothes lines to dryers leap; family history 1920s tenements to 1950s suburbia showed earlier transformation[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Work summer 1965 analyzing dazzling productivity growth ~1920 through WWII and postwar boom; except 1990s upturn, growth tepid since; idea that great inventions run out; 2000 paper computer technology less impressive than earlier inventions; 2012 paper "Is U.S. Economic Growth Over?" predicted growth less than half 2% average 1870–2007, standard of living doubling in 78 years versus 35 years 1972–2007; headwinds — aging population too few workers, declining working-age men employment, stagnant college-degree rates, jobs lost abroad, high government debt, growing inequality[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Focus on output: retired people stop producing, and innovations that clean pollution solve past growth problems shouldn't count same as output-adding breakthroughs.
- Mokyr: innovation rate accelerating; new tools open way to inventions as telescopes did for astronomy; history shows pessimists before Gordon proven wrong (1930s popular pessimism but jet engines and radar emerged); fast computing is new tool; crisis of pessimism ignores uneven progress; quality-of-life improvements matter beyond output — new medicines treating chronic pain, hip replacement letting him bike, extending life; argues past growth mismeasured because costs not accounted[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Dutch Jewish Holocaust-survivor family background in Haifa; father died young; mother tough life not optimist yet he is optimist; credits Gordon family (father Berkeley business-cycle economist) should have made Gordon optimist.
- Both agree: "One of main missions I have in life is to point out to students how lucky they are to be born in 20th century. Compared to what life was like 100 or 200 years ago, we're incredibly fortunate" (Mokyr)[^mankiw-ch12-040].

### 12-3i Population growth

- Direct effect on labor force size: large population means more workers and more total output, but also more consumers — large total output need not mean higher standard of living for typical citizen; both large and small nations found at all development levels[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Subtler interactions with other factors of production — three views:

**1. Stretching natural resources — Malthus**

- Thomas Robert Malthus (1766–1834), English minister, *An Essay on the Principle of Population as It Affects the Future Improvement of Society*: "food is necessary to the existence of man" and "the passion between the sexes is necessary and will remain nearly in its present state" → "the power of population is infinitely greater than the power in the earth to produce subsistence for man" → only check is "misery and vice"; charity/government alleviation counterproductive because poor have more children placing greater strains[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Forecast far off mark: world population increased about sixfold over past two centuries but average living standards much higher; chronic hunger/malnutrition less common; modern famines more often result of unequal income distribution or political instability than inadequate food production[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Error: human ingenuity offset population — pesticides, fertilizers, mechanized equipment, new crop varieties each farmer feeds ever greater numbers; even with more mouths, fewer farmers necessary because each farmer much more productive.

**2. Diluting the capital stock**

- High population growth reduces GDP per worker because rapid growth in workers forces capital stock to be spread more thinly — each worker equipped with less capital → lower productivity and lower GDP per worker[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Most apparent for human capital: many school-age children burden educational system → attainment tends to be low where population growth high.
- Growth rates differ widely: US and Western Europe ~1% per year recent decades and expected slower; many poor African countries ~3% per year → population doubles every 23 years, harder to provide tools and skills[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Rapid population growth not main reason LDCs poor, but some analysts believe reducing it would help raise living standards. Direct regulation: China 1980–2015 one-child per family with substantial fines for violation; in freer countries, via awareness of birth control[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Incentives (Ten Principles: people respond to incentives): bearing a child has opportunity cost; when opportunity cost rises, families choose smaller size; women with good education and desirable employment want fewer children than those with fewer opportunities outside home; policies fostering equal treatment of women may be way to reduce population growth and raise living standards[^mankiw-ch12-040].

**3. Promoting technological progress**

- More people → more scientists, inventors, engineers to contribute to advance, benefiting everyone[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Michael Kremer "Population Growth and Technological Change: One Million B.C. to 1990" (Quarterly Journal of Economics 1993): world growth rates increased with world population — growth more rapid when world population 1 billion (~1800) than when 100 million (~500 B.C.), consistent with larger population inducing more progress[^mankiw-ch12-040].
- Kremer's regional test: melting of polar icecaps end of Ice Age ~10,000 B.C. flooded land bridges and separated world into several distinct regions with no communication for thousands of years; if progress more rapid with more people, larger regions should have grown faster[^mankiw-ch12-040]. Result: in 1500 when Columbus reestablished contact, most successful was large Eurasia-Africa Old World civilizations, next Aztec and Mayan civilizations in Americas, followed by hunter-gatherers of Australia, then primitive people of Tasmania who lacked even fire-making and most stone and bone tools — supports hypothesis.

## In the News — Using Experiments to Evaluate Aid (BRAC ultra-poor graduation RCT) [^mankiw-ch12-041]

- Problem: prior aid studies frustrating — e.g., giving a family a cow and revisiting a year later confounds cow effect with bumper crop or rising property values; little systematic scientific testing of whether aid permanently changes lives[^mankiw-ch12-041]. Dean Karlan (MIT graduate student then Yale professor) and colleagues at Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab (MIT) and Innovations for Poverty Action proposed randomized controlled trials as for drugs: give some families aid, others nothing (control), follow both long-term[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Skepticism and ethics: giving nothing to controls seems unethical to some; Karlan response: finding what works vs what doesn't allows much bigger impact in five years, helping more people[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Program tested: BRAC (Bangladesh) anti-poverty approach that had served ~400,000 families living < $1.25/day; Karlan network replicated it in six developing countries, locating poorest families in thousands of communities and randomizing[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Intervention ("graduation" smorgasbord for 1–2 years): (1) productive livestock appropriate to context — goats for milk, bees for honey, guinea pigs for sale; (2) training to raise livestock; (3) food or cash so recipients do not eat the livestock; (4) savings account; (5) physical and mental health support[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Reported in *Science* (week of May 2015 NPR report by Michaeleen Doucleff): strategy worked fairly well in five of six countries — treated families earned a bit more, had more food to eat, and showed higher mental health, happiness, and female empowerment[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Durability: families continued to make a bit more money even a year after aid stopped — "stuck … big push … sustained increased income level" (Justin Sandefur, Center for Global Development) — described as exciting/unique sustainable impact, suggesting right-kind aid can lift families slightly onto a path out of extreme poverty[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Limits and caution: still very poor; income plus food consumption together up only ~5% on average vs control (Sarah Baird, George Washington University); duration beyond one-year post-aid unknown; but moving poverty at all considered important — even small extra income can affect school attendance and hope — and provides charities/governments a basic strategy that often works[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Methodological lesson for growth/poverty policy: long-run growth determinants (capital, skills, health, savings) can be tested causally via field RCTs rather than observational before-after comparisons, addressing omitted-variable bias[^mankiw-ch12-041].

## 12-4 Conclusion: The Importance of Long-Run Growth [^mankiw-ch12-041]

- Summary restatement: standard of living depends on ability to produce goods and services (Ten Principles); policymakers seeking higher living standards must raise nation's productive ability by encouraging rapid accumulation of factors of production and ensuring they are employed as effectively as possible[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Government role consensus vs controversy: at minimum support invisible hand via property rights and political stability; more controversial whether to target/subsidize specific industries deemed important for technological progress; lessons for next generation's world hinge on heeding growth fundamentals[^mankiw-ch12-041].
- Reconciles S-I with openness (Problems & Applications Q1): nation can import substantially yet high living standard requires high production because imports are paid for by exports — trade balances via comparative advantage, not autarky[^mankiw-ch12-041].
