# Macroeconomic ideas — classical long run, Keynesian activism, monetarist critique, and natural rate

Krugman and Wells present modern macroeconomic thought as an event-driven evolution: the Great Depression exposed the costs of treating short-run output as irrelevant and helped establish Keynesian demand management; postwar experience revived monetary policy and a rule-based monetarist critique; and 1970s stagflation supported the Friedman–Phelps claim that policy cannot sustain unemployment below its natural rate without accelerating inflation.[^krugman-ch17-091]

## Classical macroeconomics and the Depression

The classical price-level model treats prices as flexible and aggregate supply as vertical: a proportional money-supply increase raises the aggregate price level proportionally without changing aggregate output. The source notes that many pre-1930 economists recognized short-run output effects, but regarded them as unimportant relative to the long run.[^krugman-ch17-091]

Business cycles had been measured before the Great Depression—Wesley Mitchell founded the National Bureau of Economic Research in 1920—but no broadly accepted theory explained their causes or prescribed a response. The resulting policy disagreement included views that expansionary policy would worsen, or merely postpone, a slump.[^krugman-ch17-091]

## Keynesian revolution and policy activism

Keynes's 1936 *General Theory* shifted attention to the short run. In the textbook's modern restatement, its two central ideas are:

1. With an upward-sloping short-run aggregate-supply curve, an aggregate-demand shift changes output and employment as well as prices.
2. Changes in business confidence (Keynes's “animal spirits”) can change investment, shift aggregate demand, and generate business cycles.[^krugman-ch17-091]

This account made recessions potentially remediable within a market economy and legitimized **macroeconomic policy activism**—using fiscal and monetary policy to smooth the business cycle.[^krugman-ch17-091] The source presents U.S. World War II spending as a historical demonstration: the federal deficit reached 30% of GDP in fiscal 1943 while unemployment fell sharply, convincing many economists that sufficiently large fiscal expansion can lift a deeply depressed economy.[^krugman-ch17-091]

## Monetary-policy revival and monetarist critique

Friedman and Schwartz's 1963 *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960* linked historical business cycles to money-supply fluctuations and argued that the Federal Reserve could have prevented the Great Depression's monetary contraction by expanding the monetary base. The source says this persuaded most economists that monetary policy should be central to economic management, shifting emphasis away from fiscal policy and toward independent central banks.[^krugman-ch17-091]

**Monetarism** then argued for nondiscretionary monetary policy: steady money-supply growth would produce steady GDP growth, so the central bank should follow a rule rather than countercyclically fine-tune the economy. Monetarists accepted Keynesian short-run demand effects and advocated more expansionary policy during the Depression, but argued that recognition, implementation, and transmission lags can make discretionary fiscal *and* monetary policy destabilizing. With money supply held fixed, fiscal expansion also faces interest-rate crowding out of private investment.[^krugman-ch17-091]

The source reports that U.S. money-growth targeting in the late 1970s and early 1980s did not yield steady growth, so traditional monetarism became uncommon. Its broader claim—that monetary policy can stabilize the economy and fiscal policy has a limited role—remained influential until the 2008 crisis and its aftermath renewed doubts.[^krugman-ch17-091]

## Natural rate hypothesis and the 1970s

Friedman and Phelps independently argued in 1968 that the apparent unemployment–inflation trade-off is not permanent. Once persistent inflation is incorporated into expectations, keeping unemployment below its natural rate produces accelerating inflation rather than a durable employment gain. The policy task is therefore stabilization around the natural rate, not permanently lower unemployment through demand expansion.[^krugman-ch17-091]

The source treats 1970s high inflation alongside high unemployment as the hypothesis's successful prediction and says it subsequently gained broad acceptance, while noting a qualification: some macroeconomists doubt that the hypothesis works at very low inflation or during deflation.[^krugman-ch17-091]

## New classical critiques and the New Keynesian response

**New classical macroeconomics** revived the classical conclusion that aggregate-demand shifts change prices but not output. Its rational-expectations premise, introduced by John Muth in 1961, holds that households and firms use all available information to make decisions optimally. On this view, workers setting long-term contracts incorporate announced future monetary and fiscal policy into inflation expectations immediately.[^krugman-ch17-092]

Robert Lucas applied that logic to argue that expected monetary-policy changes cannot alter output or unemployment; only unanticipated policy can. In this stronger version of the natural-rate argument, a policy attempt to trade future inflation for lower unemployment fails immediately rather than only after expectations adjust.[^krugman-ch17-092]

The source contrasts this conclusion with **New Keynesian economics**: interacting market imperfections make many prices temporarily sticky, so expected inflation cannot immediately neutralize activist policy. It also describes **real business cycle theory** as attributing business-cycle fluctuations to changes in productivity growth. While recognizing real-business-cycle theory's contribution and warning against attributing every recession to demand, it says many of its proponents accept the need for an upward-sloping aggregate-supply curve, leaving aggregate demand a possible determinant of output.[^krugman-ch17-092]

## Political constraint on activism

A **political business cycle** occurs when politicians use macroeconomic policy for electoral ends. The source reports that voters weigh the economy shortly before an election heavily enough to create an incentive to stimulate before voting and tolerate later inflation or unemployment. It gives expansionary U.S. fiscal and monetary policy ahead of the 1972 election as an example.[^krugman-ch17-092]

The source presents central-bank independence as one safeguard and limiting discretionary fiscal action to exceptional circumstances, such as a liquidity trap, as another.[^krugman-ch17-092]

## Contradictions

- Friedman and Schwartz argued that a sufficiently expansionary Federal Reserve could have prevented the Great Depression's monetary contraction, an influential account summarized above.[^krugman-ch17-091] The later source challenges the stronger inference that the Fed caused, or clearly could have prevented, the depression: the monetary base rose while M2 fell as bank runs shifted deposits into currency and banks accumulated reserves, so further open-market operations might have sat idle at very low rates.[^krugman-ch17-092]

## Source-era debt context

The chapter opens with a source-era illustration of changing views on fiscal deficits: it reports a U.S. fiscal-2019 federal deficit of almost $1 trillion (4.7% of GDP) amid low unemployment and healthy growth, alongside 2019 arguments by Olivier Blanchard, Jason Furman, and Lawrence Summers that public debt was less pressing than often assumed. It attributes this shift in part to the 2008 crisis and the prolonged low-interest-rate environment. These are textbook-reported context and positions, not a current assessment of debt sustainability.[^krugman-ch17-091]

## Check-your-understanding applications

The source contrasts the classical emphasis on the long run—expansionary money ultimately raises the aggregate price level without aggregate-output change—with the Keynesian reading of weak business confidence (“animal spirits”) as a case for expansionary monetary and fiscal policy.[^krugman-102] It identifies policy-recognition, legislative, and implementation lags (more severe for fiscal policy), the natural-rate constraint on attempts to reduce unemployment, and electoral misuse as limits on activism.[^krugman-102]

For the Great Recession, its answers say Friedman would likely have favored more expansionary money growth while remaining wary of discretion; they qualify usual monetarist objections based on lags and crowding out because the slump was prolonged and rates and investment spending were low.[^krugman-102] They also state the new-classical claim that only unexpected money-supply changes affect short-run activity, whereas real-business-cycle theorists attribute output fluctuations largely to total-factor-productivity changes.[^krugman-102]
