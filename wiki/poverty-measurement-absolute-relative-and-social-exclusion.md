# Poverty measurement — absolute, relative, and social exclusion

Poverty resists stable absolute measurement because the basket of essentials changes with technology and social norms, while purely relative thresholds conflate poverty with lower-half inequality; Harford (via Adam Smith) treats poverty as a social condition — lacking resources to participate in society — best proxied by periodically updated absolute lines plus adjustments for living costs and spending versus income[^harford-inequality].

## Why poverty is hard to pin down

The difficulty mirrors ranking the über-rich across eras (Bill Gates with private planes versus Marcus Crassus with cellars of wine and oil): purchasing-power comparisons across time and countries require judgments about the basket[^harford-inequality]. Macro aggregates of supply, demand and sticky prices give little handle on distribution, yet the distribution shapes welfare[^harford-inequality].

## Absolute poverty lines

- **Seebohm Rowntree, York, 1899:** Quaker son of chocolate maker Joseph Rowntree; defined poverty as inability to afford basics including pease pudding with bacon on Sunday, priced from dietary requirements[^harford-inequality].
- **Mollie Orshansky, United States, 1963:** Social Security Administration researcher who had set nutritional standards at the Bureau of Human Nutrition and Home Economics in the late 1940s–1950s. She priced a "reasonable diet" assuming "the housewife will be a careful shopper, a skillful cook, and a good manager who will prepare all the family's meals at home." Adopted as official US threshold by the White House in 1969 and since uprated only for inflation[^harford-inequality]. In 2012 the line was $30.52 a day for a single person — well above the World Bank extreme-poverty standard but still modest[^harford-inequality].
- **World Bank "dollar-a-day" extreme poverty:** Brainwave of Martin Ravallion (late 1980s), who noticed several countries' lines clustered near $370 a year. Updated for inflation (what a dollar bought ~25 years ago) and adjusted for purchasing-power parity — the "dollar" in Delhi is much less than a market-exchange-rate dollar — intended to buy in India only what a dollar buys in the US: some rice or lentils and no shelter[^harford-inequality].

Problem with frozen absolute lines: they entrench historical baskets. A 1899 line uprated for inflation would still price pease pudding while ignoring electric light, indoor plumbing (luxuries in Rowntree's day), television and internet access that were unimaginable then[^harford-inequality].

## Relative poverty and its limits

**Eurostat (EU) definition:** poverty line = 60% of each nation's median income (median = person poorer than half and richer than half)[^harford-inequality].

Consequences:

- Poverty becomes permanent unless lower-half inequality changes: doubling everyone's income leaves the rate unchanged[^harford-inequality].
- Poverty rates can fall in recessions because the median falls; UK poverty fell recently for this reason — a family with unchanged income "escapes poverty" when the threshold falls[^harford-inequality].
- The measure compares the poor to middle-income households and ignores the rich; Harford suggests calling it inequality in the lower half of the distribution[^harford-inequality].

## Poverty as social exclusion

**Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776):** "A linen shirt, for example, is, strictly speaking, not a necessity of life. The Greeks and Romans lived, I suppose, very comfortably though they had no linen. But in the present times, through the greater part of Europe, a creditable day-laborer would be ashamed to appear in public without a linen shirt"[^harford-inequality].

Interpretation: poverty is not purely absolute or relative to the median, but the inability to afford what society treats as essential for participation. A television is not haute cuisine or champagne; a child lacking one may reasonably be seen as deprived because society now treats it as essential[^harford-inequality].

**Joseph Rowntree Foundation approach:** focus groups establish what is now necessary to participate — e.g., a vacation, a no-frills mobile phone, enough money for a cheap suit every two or three years. Subjective but explicitly updated to reflect social change; Harford favors such periodically rebased absolute lines over claims that any expert can permanently fix poverty's meaning[^harford-inequality].

## Practical measurement complications

- **Cost of living geography:** living costs differ (Alabama vs New York). Absolute lines should adjust, but the US line does not[^harford-inequality].
- **Income versus spending:** a middle manager unemployed three months with savings, credit and good prospects may fall below the income line yet maintain spending. Some economists prefer to measure poverty by household spending rather than weekly/monthly/yearly income[^harford-inequality].

## Levels and trends

- **United States (official Orshansky line):** 15% poor in 2011, highest since early 1990s, up from 12.3% in 2006 before recession — consistent with a sensible absolute measure rising in slumps[^harford-inequality].
- **Europe at US standards (University of York, year 2000 estimates):** Portugal 48%, Denmark 6%, France 12%, Germany 15%, UK 18% — reflecting both national income (Portugal poorer than Denmark) and distribution (France more egalitarian than UK at similar average income)[^harford-inequality].
- **Global extreme poverty (World Bank dollar-a-day):** share of developing-world population fell from 31% in 1990 to 14% in 2008, halving the 1990–2015 goal early, largely via China's growth. Real progress on this meager standard, though hundreds of millions still live at this level[^harford-inequality].
- **US historical:** Orshansky poverty fell sharply from ~22% late 1950s to 11.1% in 1973 (historic low), then stagnated despite decades of growth — the 1960s showed rapid absolute-poverty reduction was possible[^harford-inequality].

## Responses to poverty

Harford sketches three broad stances: (1) "insulate, insulate, insulate" — quarantine the poor (citing Tom Wolfe's *Bonfire of the Vanities*), which he treats as incompatible with generous immigration attitudes; (2) direct cash transfers; (3) improving opportunities — schools, crime reduction, decent jobs (help people vs help people help themselves)[^harford-inequality].

### Cash versus opportunity

Claims that cash encourages dependency, drug abuse or fecklessness are often conflated with poverty itself. Example: UK government cited 120,000 "troubled families" as wracked by "Drug addiction. Alcohol abuse. Crime" (David Cameron), while the actual criteria were poverty, disability and unemployment, not crime or drugs[^harford-inequality]. Cash does not fix early-life deprivation that blocks self-sufficiency, so investment in children matters[^harford-inequality].

### Early-years education — Perry Preschool Project

Randomized trial in mid-1960s providing excellent preschool to randomly selected deprived African American children aged 3–4 (cost today $11,000–12,000 per child)[^harford-inequality]. Versus control group:

- almost one extra year of education completed, ~45% more likely to graduate high school[^harford-inequality];
- dramatically less likely to have children out of wedlock or teen pregnancy[^harford-inequality];
- at age 40, control group >50% had spent time in prison vs 28% for Perry children (still high)[^harford-inequality];
- in middle age, Perry children earned >40% more[^harford-inequality].

Evaluated over the long term the program paid for itself many times via extra taxes and avoided prison costs. Caveat: one small pilot, specific quality, population and era; other quality-preschool studies also look very good for deprived children. Harford recommends expanding RCTs to postnatal support, school reform and policing/criminal justice — areas where RCTs are feasible unlike monetary policy[^harford-inequality].

### Conditional cash transfers

Pioneered in Latin America: cash to poor families conditional on behavior such as sending children to school or vaccination. Attraction: combines cash with desired investments and behavioral assurance; drawback: children in most dysfunctional families likely excluded though they need help most. Worth properly evaluated pilots[^harford-inequality].
