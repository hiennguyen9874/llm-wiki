# Thinking Like an Economist — Scientific Method, Economic Models, and Positive vs Normative Analysis

Economists approach the economy with scientific objectivity — devising theories, collecting data, and testing — but with limited ability to run laboratory experiments, so they rely on assumptions and simplified models (the circular-flow diagram and the production possibilities frontier) to illustrate scarcity, trade-offs and opportunity cost, distinguish micro from macro, separate positive (how the world is) from normative (how it ought to be) analysis, and advise policy in a messy democratic process where disagreement stems from different scientific judgments and different values[^mankiw-015].

## 2-1 The Economist as Scientist

Every field has its own language; economics uses supply, demand, elasticity, comparative advantage, consumer surplus and deadweight loss to offer a new way of thinking about the world[^mankiw-015].

### Scientific method: observation, theory, and more observation

The essence of science is the dispassionate development and testing of theories about how the world works — "the whole of science is nothing more than the refinement of everyday thinking" (Einstein)[^mankiw-015]. Economists, like physicists and biologists, devise theories, collect data, and analyze data to verify or refute theories[^mankiw-015].

- **Newton analogy:** apple falling → theory of gravity applying universally, later tested and qualified by Einstein, still taught for its explanatory success[^mankiw-015].
- **Economics analogy:** observer of rapid price increases → theory that high inflation arises when government prints too much money → test with international data on prices and money. Strong correlation across countries increases confidence; lack of correlation would prompt doubt[^mankiw-015].

**Challenge: experiments often impractical.** Physicists can drop objects in a lab; economists cannot manipulate national monetary policy for experimental purposes. Like astronomers and evolutionary biologists, they make do with data the world gives them and pay close attention to *natural experiments* offered by history[^mankiw-015].

- **Example:** war in the Middle East interrupting crude oil supply → oil prices skyrocket worldwide → opportunity to study effects of a key natural resource, illustrating and evaluating theories[^mankiw-015].

### Role of assumptions

Assumptions simplify the complex world to make it understandable[^mankiw-015].

- **Physics:** time for marble to fall from ten-story building calculated assuming vacuum; friction from air is negligible for marble but not for beach ball of same weight because larger surface area makes friction material — physicist chooses assumption based on question and object[^mankiw-015].
- **Economics:** to study international trade, assume world of only two countries each producing two goods — unrealistic but focuses on essence before extending to thousands of goods and many countries[^mankiw-015].
- **Time-horizon example:** analyzing change in money supply: short-run effects → assume many prices change infrequently (magazine newsstand prices change every few years), so assume prices sticky or completely fixed; long-run effects → assume prices completely flexible. Art of scientific thinking is choosing which assumptions to make for the question at hand[^mankiw-015].

### Economic models

Models omit many details to reveal what is truly important[^mankiw-015].

- **Biology teaching model:** plastic replica of human body with heart, liver, kidneys — stylized, not a real person, but shows how major organs fit together; realism sacrificed for clarity[^mankiw-015].
- **Economic models:** mostly diagrams and equations (unlike plastic manikins); assume away irrelevant details; all models simplify reality to improve understanding[^mankiw-015].

### Model 1: The Circular-Flow Diagram

> **circular-flow diagram** — a visual model showing how dollars flow through markets among households and firms[^mankiw-015].

- **Simplified agents:** two decision makers — *firms* (produce goods/services using inputs) and *households* (own factors of production and consume output)[^mankiw-015].
- **Factors of production:** inputs such as labor, land, and capital (buildings, machines)[^mankiw-015].
- **Two market types:** (1) *markets for goods and services* where households are buyers, firms sellers; (2) *markets for the factors of production* where firms are buyers, households sellers[^mankiw-015].
- **Two loops:** inner loop = flows of inputs and outputs (households sell labor/land/capital to firms → firms use them to make goods/services sold to households); outer loop = corresponding flow of dollars (households spend money on goods/services → firms use revenue to pay wages/rent → profit returns to household owners)[^mankiw-015].
- **Dollar tour:** household wallet → Starbucks for coffee → Starbucks revenue → rent/wages to landlord/worker households → back in a wallet, restarting the flow[^mankiw-015].
- **Limitations:** very simple model omitting government and international trade (e.g., part of Starbucks dollar paying taxes or buying Brazilian coffee beans); still useful for seeing how pieces fit together[^mankiw-015].

### Model 2: The Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)

> **production possibilities frontier** — graph showing combinations of output the economy can possibly produce given available factors and production technology[^mankiw-015].

**Two-good example: cars and computers**, using all factors across two industries[^mankiw-015]:

- Endpoints: all resources to cars → 1,000 cars, 0 computers; all to computers → 3,000 computers, 0 cars[^mankiw-015].
- Interior allocations: Point A = 600 cars + 2,200 computers; Point B = 700 cars + 2,000 computers (moving factors from computers to cars)[^mankiw-015].
- **Feasibility:** points on or inside frontier feasible; point C outside frontier infeasible given technology and resources[^mankiw-015].
- **Efficiency:** outcome efficient if economy gets all it can from scarce resources; points *on* frontier are efficient (more of one good requires less of the other). Point D = 300 cars + 1,000 computers is inefficient (e.g., widespread unemployment); eliminating inefficiency can raise both (D→A: 300→600 cars and 1,000→2,200 computers)[^mankiw-015].

**Trade-offs and opportunity cost:**

- Moving A→B produces 100 more cars at expense of 200 fewer computers — illustrates Ten Principles trade-off "people face trade-offs"[^mankiw-015].
- **Opportunity cost** — what is given up to get an item; here slope of PPF measures opportunity cost of a car in computers: at A, 100 cars cost 200 computers → 1 car = 2 computers[^mankiw-015].

**Bowed shape:** PPF in Figure 2 bowed outward because opportunity cost not constant but depends on production mix; resources heterogeneous[^mankiw-015].

- At point F (few cars, many computers), frontier flat → best auto workers still in computers where they are poor at it, so +1 car costs little computer loss (low opportunity cost)[^mankiw-015].
- At point E (many cars, few computers), frontier steep → best computer technicians must be pulled to make cars, causing large computer loss (high opportunity cost)[^mankiw-015].
- Economists believe this bowed shape is typical[^mankiw-015].

**Shifts and growth:**

- PPF shows trade-off at a given time; trade-off can change over time[^mankiw-015].
- Technological advance in computer industry (more computers per worker per week) expands society's set of opportunities: endpoint with 0 computers still 1,000 cars, but for any positive computers, more can be made → frontier shifts outward (Figure 3)[^mankiw-015].
- Economy can move from old point A to new point G: 650 cars + 2,300 computers versus 600 + 2,200 — both goods increase; choice depends on preferences[^mankiw-015].
- Simplifies to highlight scarcity, efficiency, trade-offs, opportunity cost, and economic growth[^mankiw-015].

### Microeconomics and macroeconomics

- **Microeconomics** — study of how households and firms make decisions and interact in specific markets (e.g., rent control in NYC, foreign competition on US auto industry, compulsory schooling on earnings)[^mankiw-015].
- **Macroeconomics** — study of economy-wide phenomena, sum of activities across all decision makers/markets (e.g., federal borrowing, economy-wide unemployment, policies to promote national living standards)[^mankiw-015].
- Fields intertwined: macroeconomic developments arise from microeconomic decisions (e.g., income tax cut effect on total production requires analyzing households' spending decisions), but they address different questions and have distinct models often taught separately; analogy to biology levels from molecules → cells → evolution[^mankiw-015].

## 2-2 The Economist as Policy Adviser

Economists act as scientists when explaining the world and as policy advisers when trying to improve it[^mankiw-015].

### Positive versus normative analysis

- **Positive statements** — descriptive, claim about how the world *is* (e.g., Portia: "Minimum-wage laws cause unemployment")[^mankiw-015].
- **Normative statements** — prescriptive, claim about how the world *ought to be* (e.g., Noah: "The government should raise the minimum wage")[^mankiw-015].
- **Validation:** positive statements can in principle be confirmed/refuted by evidence (e.g., analyzing data on minimum-wage changes vs unemployment); normative statements involve values, ethics, religion, political philosophy and cannot be judged on data alone[^mankiw-015].
- Within belief sets, positive views affect normative views (if Portia's positive claim true, she may reject Noah's prescription), but normative conclusions require value judgments beyond positive analysis[^mankiw-015].

### Economists in Washington

Truman's quip about wanting a one-armed economist because advisers say "on the one hand... on the other hand..." reflects trade-offs: policy improving efficiency may cost equality, or help future generations at expense of current ones; an economist claiming all decisions are easy is not to be trusted[^mankiw-015].

Institutional roles:

- **Council of Economic Advisers (since 1946):** three members + few dozen staff, offices near White House, advises president and writes annual *Economic Report of the President*[^mankiw-015].
- **Administrative departments:** Office of Management and Budget (spending/regulatory plans), Treasury (tax policy), Labor (worker data, labor-market policy), Justice (antitrust enforcement)[^mankiw-015].
- **Legislative and monetary:** Congressional Budget Office (independent evaluations for Congress) and Federal Reserve (hundreds of economists analyzing US/world economies for monetary policy)[^mankiw-015].

Influence beyond advising: Keynes (1935) noted ideas of economists/political philosophers, right or wrong, are more powerful than commonly understood — "Practical men... are usually the slaves of some defunct economist" — applicable today, often Keynes himself[^mankiw-015].

### Why economists' advice is not always followed

Economic policy process differs from textbook benevolent-king model where right policy is implemented without friction[^mankiw-015].

Real-world presidents, after hearing economic advice, also weigh input from communications advisers (public explanation), press advisers (media/editorial reaction), legislative affairs advisers (Congressional amendments, passage likelihood), and political advisers (group support/opposition, electoral effects, impact on other initiatives)[^mankiw-015]. In representative democracy, policy-making is messy; economists' advice is one ingredient of a complex recipe — often with good reason not advanced as recommended[^mankiw-015].

## 2-3 Why Economists Disagree

Shaw/Truman/Reagan quips note economists appear not to reach conclusions — 100 questions, 3,000 answers — but disagreement is overstated[^mankiw-015].

Two basic reasons for conflicting advice:

### 2-3a Differences in scientific judgments

Science is ongoing search; disagreements arise over validity of alternative positive theories or size of key parameters[^mankiw-015]. Analogies: centuries-old earth- vs sun-centered solar system debate; recent climatologists' global-warming debate[^mankiw-015]. Economics is young science with much to learn[^mankiw-015].

- **Example:** whether to tax income vs consumption. Consumption-tax advocates believe exempting saved income encourages saving → frees resources for capital accumulation → faster productivity/living-standards growth. Income-tax defenders believe saving responds little to tax law. Different normative positions trace to different positive views of saving responsiveness[^mankiw-015].

### 2-3b Differences in values

Policies cannot be judged on science alone[^mankiw-015].

- **Town-well example:** Peter income $150,000 taxed $15,000 (10%); Paula income $30,000 taxed $6,000 (20%) to maintain well where both take same water. Is it fair? Who pays too much/little? Does it matter Paula's low income is disability vs acting choice, or Peter's high income is inheritance vs dreary long hours? People disagree; hiring two experts would likely yield conflicting advice. Perfecting economic science would not resolve Peter vs Paula fairness[^mankiw-015].

### Why study economics and the master-economist ideal

Robert McTeer (former president, Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, commencement address reproduced in Mankiw) argues the most important lesson is Adam Smith's **invisible hand** — understanding how decentralized self-interest coordinated by markets enhances wealth without central planning, why economies work better the fewer people they have in charge, and why tampering risks "killing the goose that lays the golden eggs"[^mankiw-016]. Economics training is the study of how to anticipate **unintended consequences** and fallacies (broken window, job-counting)[^mankiw-016] — see [Bastiat's seen and unseen and macro policy fallacies](bastiat-seen-unseen-and-policy-fallacies.md) and [Graphing Review — Types, Correlation, Slope, and Causality Pitfalls](graphing-review-correlation-slope-and-causality.md).

Keynes's master-economist paradox quoted to close the chapter: the economist must be "mathematician, historian, statesman, philosopher — in some degree ... understand symbols and speak in words ... contemplate the particular in terms of the general ... study the present in the light of the past for the purposes of the future ... purposeful and disinterested in a simultaneous mood; as aloof and incorruptible as an artist, yet sometimes as near the earth as a politician" — a tall order that practice in thinking like an economist builds toward[^mankiw-016].

## Key Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| circular-flow diagram | visual model of dollars and inputs/outputs flowing between households and firms through two market types[^mankiw-015] |
| production possibilities frontier | graph of feasible output combinations given factors and technology[^mankiw-015] |
| factors of production | inputs including labor, land, capital[^mankiw-015] |
| efficiency | getting all possible from scarce resources (on PPF)[^mankiw-015] |
| opportunity cost | value of what is given up to obtain an item; measured by PPF slope[^mankiw-015] |
| positive statement | descriptive claim about how world is[^mankiw-015] |
| normative statement | prescriptive claim about how world ought to be[^mankiw-015] |
| microeconomics / macroeconomics | study of individual/market-specific decisions vs economy-wide phenomena[^mankiw-015] |
| market for goods and services / market for factors | where households buy output vs supply inputs[^mankiw-015] |
