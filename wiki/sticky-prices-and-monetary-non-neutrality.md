# Sticky prices, nominal rigidity, and why money matters

If prices adjusted with complete freedom, the amount of currency would not matter — a nominal scaling would solve a shortage — but in practice wages and prices stick, so a shortage of money can cause a real recession that printing money can sometimes cure[^harford-ch2].

## The neutrality benchmark

- With fully flexible prices, a babysitting co-op with too little scrip could simply agree that each half-hour scrip now buys an hour of babysitting; nominal values halve, real values unchanged[^harford-ch2].
- Monopoly analogy: if the bank runs out of paper money, players normally create IOUs or poker chips (new money). The logically equivalent alternative is a redenomination — $1 becomes $2, rents halve, property prices halve, each player returns half their notes, none poorer — a purely nominal change that works in logic but not in practice because prices do not adjust smoothly[^harford-ch2].

## Why prices stick: four reasons

### 1. Fairness norms

- People exhibit fairness constraints on wages and prices, studied by Daniel Kahneman (psychologist, Nobel Memorial Prize winner)[^harford-ch2].
- Vignette: photocopy shop with one employee at $18/hour for six months; local factory closure pushes market wage to $14; owner cuts wage to $14. Kahneman et al. found 83% of respondents judged this unfair or very unfair, despite the higher wage being above market[^harford-ch2].
- Consequences: owner avoids the cut for decency, awkwardness, or fear of sabotage/strike; may even forgo hiring a second worker at $14 because a gap between colleagues invites trouble, perhaps buying a machine instead; labour market supply and demand fail to clear, unemployment higher[^harford-ch2].
- Same for product prices: raising snow-shovel price $30→$40 after a storm judged unfair[^harford-ch2].
- Gadget shortages illustrate: Nintendo, Xbox 360 (mid-2000s), and Apple iPad/iPhone launches with long queues — limited first supply could be cleared by temporary price hike (e.g., $400→$600 for 1m units vs. 5m demand) then cut after Christmas, but firms avoid it because temporary spikes annoy customers and subsequent falls enrage early buyers; Apple cut original iPhone from $600 to $400 after 2.5 months in 2007, provoked outrage and $100 vouchers from Steve Jobs[^harford-ch2].
- In the co-op, members risked pariah status by demanding six hours of sitting for three hours of scrip[^harford-ch2].

### 2. Menu costs

- Changing posted prices is costly: reprinting menus and catalogs (hence the term), renegotiating contracts[^harford-ch2].
- Extreme case: Coca-Cola at 5 cents from 1886 for more than seventy years — coffee price rose eightfold over same period — because Coke was sold in nickel-only vending machines (refitting for pennies hugely expensive; alternative was doubling to 10 cents), plus laminated trays, building murals advertising 5c, soda-fountain glasses enforcing servings, and long-term fixed-price contracts; boss wrote to President Eisenhower in 1953 proposing a 7.5-cent coin[^harford-ch2].
- Typical magnitude: Daniel Levy estimated mid-1990s cost of changing one product type's price in a supermarket at 52 cents; with several hundred thousand products, >$100,000 per store, about a third of profits; for a large industrial equipment manufacturer, management time, research, sales-force communication, and renegotiation cost >20% of profits[^harford-ch2].
- These costs alone slow adjustment even without 70-year rigidity[^harford-ch2].

### 3. Coordination problems

- Each firm's price depends on others' prices and incomes; by ignoring externalities, firms adjust too slowly[^harford-ch2].
- Harford's Exxon vs. Shell illustration: both sell identical fuel to perfectly informed customers, must price to nearest cent, board meetings on 1st (Shell) and 15th (Exxon); long-run equilibrium $1 with $0.99 cost, splitting market; cost suddenly falls to $0.49 on February 22; both make 51¢ margin briefly[^harford-ch2].
- Shell on March 1 undercuts to $0.99 to double volume at 50¢ margin; Exxon on March 15 to $0.98, etc.; price takes >2 years to fall to just above cost despite many adjustment opportunities, because each cut is only 1¢ to maximize own profit, ignoring benefit to motorists' pockets and thus to other firms' sales[^harford-ch2].
- Captures how small stickiness balloons via strategic complementarity across the economy[^harford-ch2].

### 4. Money illusion

- People react to nominal (unadjusted) rather than real (inflation-adjusted) values, even when they can compute the difference[^harford-ch2].
- True story of an economics professor: furious at a nominal pay cut, but content years later with a 3% nominal raise when inflation was 6% — a 3% real cut that did not feel like a cut[^harford-ch2].
- Resulting rigidity: nominal pay cuts are rare (<0.5% of U.S. salary negotiations end with a nominal cut) while real cuts via sub-inflation raises are common[^harford-ch2].
- Harford flags money illusion as relevant for Chapter 4[^harford-ch2].

## Implications for recessions

- Sticky prices plus a shrinking economy create a vicious circle: firms hesitate to cut prices (menu costs, coordination), products remain overpriced, sales fall, firms need to cut costs but workers resist nominal wage cuts, so layoffs rise, unemployment reduces demand, forcing further cuts[^harford-ch2].
- Can be as severe as the Great Depression; government presence (regulated prices, minimum wages, politicized public-sector pay) adds further stickiness beyond free-market reasons[^harford-ch2].
- Consequence: if the money supply is too small relative to desired savings, printing a correct amount of scrip can unlock otherwise willing trades — but calibration errors (too much money) produce inflation or the opposite miscoordination, as in the co-op's second downturn and the leaf-currency defoliation in Adams's parable[^harford-ch2].
