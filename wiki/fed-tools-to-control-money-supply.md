# How the Fed controls the money supply — open-market operations, lending, reserve requirements, interest on reserves, and limits

The Fed changes the money supply either by changing the **quantity of reserves** or by changing the **reserve ratio** and thus the money multiplier[^mankiw-16-4]. Quantity-of-reserves tools are open-market bond transactions and direct lending to banks; reserve-ratio tools are reserve requirements and interest paid on reserves[^mankiw-16-4]. All four tools are powerful but control is imprecise — because fractional-reserve money creation depends on households' deposit choices and bankers' lending choices — and is further complicated by bank runs, as the 1929–1933 collapse illustrates[^mankiw-16-4].

## Krugman/Wells policy-tool framing

Krugman/Wells groups the Fed's tools as reserve requirements, the discount rate, and — most importantly — open-market operations[^krugman-ch14-078]. It explains the same multiplier channels: lowering a reserve requirement normally enables more lending, while a wider discount-rate spread over the federal funds rate makes reserve shortfalls costlier and tends to reduce lending[^krugman-ch14-078]. The source presents reserve requirements and discount-rate movements as tools used little in ordinary practice: it says the last significant reserve-requirement change was in 1992 and describes temporary discount-window lending during the 2007 crisis[^krugman-ch14-078]. Its statements that a 10% requirement was “current” and that the discount-rate spread was 0.60 percentage points in early 2020 are source-era observations, not current-policy claims.

## Influencing the quantity of reserves (16-4a)

The Fed can alter reserves by buying or selling bonds or by lending to banks, or by a combination[^mankiw-16-4].

### Open-market operations

- The Fed conducts open-market operations when it buys or sells government bonds[^mankiw-16-4].
- **To increase the money supply**, it instructs bond traders at the **New York Fed** to buy bonds from the public; dollars paid for bonds increase dollars in the economy — some held as currency (+$1 of money per $1) and some deposited in banks (+more than $1 because reserves rise and the banking system can create additional money)[^mankiw-16-4].
- **To decrease the money supply**, it sells government bonds to the public; the public pays with currency and bank deposits, directly reducing money in circulation, and withdrawals to buy the bonds leave banks with fewer reserves, causing banks to reduce lending and reversing money creation[^mankiw-16-4].
- Transactions are easy to conduct — analogous to any individual's portfolio trade, except when two individuals trade money just changes hands while amount in circulation stays constant — and the Fed can change the money supply by a small or large amount on any day without major legal or regulatory changes; therefore open-market operations are the tool used most often[^mankiw-16-4].

### Balance-sheet mechanics and scope

In the source's normal-times simplification, the Fed's assets are mainly Treasury bills and its liabilities are the **monetary base** (currency in circulation plus bank reserves)[^krugman-ch14-079]. An open-market operation trades those bills with private commercial or investment banks, not directly with the federal government[^krugman-ch14-079].

- In a Fed **purchase**, the Fed credits the seller bank's reserve account: its Treasury-bill assets and monetary-base liabilities rise by the same amount, while the bank exchanges Treasury bills for reserves. A sale reverses both changes[^krugman-ch14-079]. Figure 14-8's $100 million example makes clear that this is a balance-sheet swap, not an immediate change in commercial-bank liabilities.
- The Fed can create the reserve credit used for a purchase; it literally controls the monetary base, not the broader money supply. The broader effect depends on banks' lending and the money-multiplier process[^krugman-ch14-079].
- The source says buying government debt directly from the Treasury would amount to direct central-bank financing of a government deficit and associates that practice historically with very high inflation; this is a source framing, not a universal causal claim[^krugman-ch14-079].
- The normal balance-sheet simplification ceased to describe the crisis response well: from 2008 the Fed expanded lending to financial institutions and credit-market support, then held more long-term Treasuries and agency debt; the source depicts another large expansion during the 2020 pandemic[^krugman-ch14-079]. These are historical observations through 2020, not a statement of its current portfolio.

### Fed lending to banks

- Banks borrow from the Fed when they lack enough reserves to satisfy regulators, meet withdrawals, make new loans, or for other business reasons[^mankiw-16-4].
- **Discount window**: banks borrow and pay the **discount rate**; the loan gives the banking system more reserves than otherwise, allowing more money creation[^mankiw-16-4].
- **Discount-rate as policy**: a higher discount rate discourages borrowing, reduces reserves and the money supply; a lower discount rate encourages borrowing, increasing reserves and the money supply[^mankiw-16-4].
- **Term Auction Facility (TAF), 2007–2010**: the Fed set a quantity of funds to lend, eligible banks bid, and loans went to the highest eligible bidders with acceptable collateral and highest offered rate[^mankiw-16-4]. Unlike the discount window where the Fed sets price and banks choose quantity, the TAF set quantity and competitive bidding set price; the more funds made available, the greater reserves and the larger the money supply[^mankiw-16-4].
- **Lender-of-last-resort role beyond money-supply control**: the Fed lends to distressed institutions to support stability[^mankiw-16-4]. Examples:
  - October 19, 1987 — 22% stock-market crash left Wall Street brokerages temporarily short of funds; before the next open Fed Chair Alan Greenspan announced readiness to serve as a source of liquidity; many economists credit this with limiting repercussions[^mankiw-16-4].
  - 2008–2009 — nationwide housing-price falls and mortgage defaults strained institutions holding mortgages; the Fed provided many billions in loans to distressed financial institutions to prevent broader ramifications[^mankiw-16-4].

## Influencing the reserve ratio and money multiplier (16-4b)

Beyond quantity of reserves, the Fed influences the **reserve ratio** and thereby the **money multiplier**, either by regulating required reserves or by paying interest on reserves[^mankiw-16-4].

### Reserve requirements

- **Reserve requirements** are regulations on the minimum amount of reserves banks must hold against deposits[^mankiw-16-4].
- They influence how much money the banking system can create per dollar of reserves: an **increase** in requirements forces banks to hold more reserves and lend less per deposited dollar, raising the reserve ratio, lowering the multiplier, and decreasing money supply; a **decrease** lowers the ratio, raises the multiplier, and increases money supply[^mankiw-16-4].
- The Fed changes reserve requirements only **rarely** because it disrupts banking business — a higher requirement can leave banks short of reserves without any deposit change, forcing curtailed lending until the new level is built[^mankiw-16-4]. Moreover, the tool has become less effective because many banks now hold **excess reserves** (more than required)[^mankiw-16-4].

### Paying interest on reserves

- Traditionally banks earned no interest on reserves held at the Fed; **starting October 2008 the Fed began paying interest on reserves**[^mankiw-16-4].
- The higher the interest rate on reserves, the more reserves banks choose to hold, tending to increase the reserve ratio, lower the multiplier, and lower the money supply[^mankiw-16-4].

## Why control is imperfect (16-4c)

The four tools have powerful effects, yet control is not precise because fractional-reserve banking puts much of money creation outside direct Fed control[^mankiw-16-4]. Two problems:

1. **Households' deposit vs. currency choice**: more deposits → more bank reserves → more money creation; less deposits → fewer reserves → less creation[^mankiw-16-4]. If people lose confidence and withdraw deposits to hold currency, the banking system loses reserves and creates less money, so money supply falls without any Fed action[^mankiw-16-4].
2. **Bankers' lending vs. excess-reserve choice**: a deposited dollar creates more money only when loaned; banks may hold excess reserves instead, so the Fed cannot be sure how much the system will create[^mankiw-16-4]. If bankers become more cautious and make fewer loans, holding greater reserves, the system creates less money and money supply falls[^mankiw-16-4].

Because the amount of money depends partly on depositor and banker behavior the Fed cannot control or perfectly predict, perfect control is impossible[^mankiw-16-4]. However, if vigilant these problems need not be large — the Fed collects data on deposits and reserves weekly, quickly learns of behavioral changes, and can respond to keep money supply close to its chosen level[^mankiw-16-4].

## Bank runs and the money supply — case study

- A **bank run** occurs when depositors fear trouble and run to withdraw deposits[^mankiw-16-4].
- Under fractional-reserve banking a bank holds only a fraction of deposits in reserve and cannot satisfy all withdrawal requests at once; even a **solvent** bank (assets exceed liabilities) lacks enough cash for all depositors simultaneously and must close until loans are repaid or a **lender of last resort** such as the Fed supplies currency[^mankiw-16-4]. The United States has not seen a major run in recent history, but the United Kingdom's **Northern Rock** suffered a run in 2007 and was eventually taken over by the government; films such as *Mary Poppins* and *It's a Wonderful Life* depict runs[^mankiw-16-4].
- Runs complicate money-supply control. Example — **Great Depression**: after a wave of runs and closings in the early 1930s, households withdrew deposits to hold currency, reversing money creation as bankers facing falling reserves reduced loans, and bankers simultaneously raised reserve ratios to keep cash for future runs, lowering the multiplier and further reducing money supply[^mankiw-16-4]. From **1929 to 1933 money supply fell 28 percent without deliberate Fed contraction**; many economists point to this fall to explain the period's high unemployment and falling prices (mechanisms examined in later chapters)[^mankiw-16-4].

### Modern mitigation — FDIC deposit insurance

- Today bank runs are **not a major problem** for the U.S. banking system or the Fed because the federal government **guarantees the safety of deposits at most banks**, primarily through the **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)**[^mankiw-16-fdic].
- Depositors do not run because they are confident that even if their bank goes bankrupt, the FDIC will make good on deposits[^mankiw-16-fdic]. Krugman/Wells reports an FDIC limit of $250,000 per depositor at each insured bank; this is a source-era policy figure, not independently verified as current[^krugman-ch14-077].
- **Benefit**: a more stable banking system — most people see bank runs only in the movies[^mankiw-16-fdic].
- **Cost / moral hazard**: bankers whose deposits are guaranteed may have **too little incentive to avoid bad risks** when making loans[^mankiw-16-fdic]. Policy trades off stability against risk-taking incentives.
- The historical scale of the risk is substantial: Krugman/Wells describes three U.S. run waves from late 1930 to early 1933, after which more than one-third of banks had failed; Roosevelt's March 6, 1933 national bank holiday gave regulators a week to close unhealthy banks and certify healthy ones[^krugman-ch14-077].

### Regulatory perimeter and shadow banking

- Deposit insurance is complemented by **capital requirements** (loss-absorbing owner equity), **reserve requirements**, and the **discount window**, through which the Fed can lend against a sudden withdrawal demand and reduce a forced asset sale at fire-sale prices[^krugman-ch14-077].
- These protections do not fully extend to **shadow banking**: nondepository firms — including investment banks, insurers, hedge funds, and money-market funds — that perform banking-like functions. The source treats the 2008 panic as evidence that run-like dynamics can persist outside conventional depository banking[^krugman-ch14-077].
