# Efficiency wages and the invention of unemployment — Ford's five-dollar day

Classical supply-demand predicts unemployment is voluntary — workers could accept lower wages and vacancies would clear — but wages often do not fall in recessions and unemployment is deeply unhappy beyond lost income, contradicting the "great vacation" view[^harford-ch8]. Henry Ford's 1914 five-dollar day illustrates why: paying above the market-clearing wage can be profit-maximizing yet create involuntary unemployment through efficiency wages[^harford-ch8].

## The classical puzzle

- In the simple market, firms wanting workers raise wages, firms wanting fewer cut wages, and jobless workers accept lower pay; persistent unemployment would be a choice to wait for higher pay[^harford-ch8].
- Counter-evidence: firms sack some workers and keep others at old wages rather than cutting pay; psychological happiness research finds money modestly affects life satisfaction but having a job matters far more[^harford-ch8].

## Ford's five-dollar day: facts

- January 1914: Ford introduced $5/day minimum, more than twice prior pay, and cut the day from nine to eight hours[^harford-ch8].
- Result: thousands braved Detroit winter to queue; one day a riot led police to use a fire hose in well-below-zero temperatures — drenched men withdrew to dry then returned[^harford-ch8].
- Conditions: six-month probation and approval by Ford's paternalistic sociology department that the household was hygienic and prudent; women excluded except heads of household, officials calling pay a "profit share"[^harford-ch8].

## Not recruitment and not philanthropy

- **Not a hot labor market:** Detroit was in recession; numbers on government assistance had almost quadrupled in the prior two years; a rival told the *Detroit News* the Ford plant could only employ so many and others would still hire at the prevailing less-than-half wage[^harford-ch8].
- Ford actively discouraged out-of-town applicants and shut fee-charging consultancies sending applications — inconsistent with seeking a broader pool[^harford-ch8].
- **Not skill scarcity:** Ford had phased out skilled craftsmen (two-thirds of workforce five years earlier in workshop-like production of handcrafted cars) for a 30-fold larger semiskilled workforce doing repetitive Model T tasks; one worker joked about becoming "Nut No. 86 in the Pontiac bughouse"[^harford-ch8].
- **Not philanthropy:** the increase was only announced grandly to Detroit press, national attention surprised Ford; it was early in his career (≈ half expected profits, like "Bill Gates turning to global poverty around 1985"), using company cash as majority — not sole — owner exposed him to minority-shareholder suit (later Dodge brothers case); Ford himself called it "one of the finest cost-cutting moves we ever made"[^harford-ch8].

## The real cost problem: turnover and shirking

- 1913: >50,000 hires for a workforce of ~13,500; average tenure three months; March 1913 >7,000 left, >50% of workforce, mostly "five-day men" who failed to appear for five days and were deemed resigned[^harford-ch8].
- Underlying: long hours, tedious work, poor wages, "annoying fools" as floor bosses per internal review; easy outside options meant little fear of firing, so slacking, absenteeism, quarrels and sabotage ("throwing a wrench in the works") were common; turnover itself was quickly replaceable but symptomatic[^harford-ch8].

## Trafford Park precedent

- 1912 musings with Percival Perry, head of Ford's Trafford Park plant in Manchester — the sole English outpost — who had raised pay from £1 10s/week (enough to recruit but not to support a family, leaving workers hungry and distracted) to £3/week double the going rate, calling it "high wages and straight wages," which greatly improved productivity[^harford-ch8]. Ford copied the strategy.

## Three mechanisms of efficiency wages

1. **Better living:** workers could feed themselves and maintain stable families (with the sociology department discouraging spending extra on booze)[^harford-ch8].
2. **Reciprocity:** gratitude/obligation led to more vigorous application[^harford-ch8].
3. **Higher cost of job loss:** at twice the elsewhere wage, workers had much to lose and became "absolutely docile" per contemporary commentary, working hard and following instructions[^harford-ch8].

Results: turnover plummeted and labor productivity rose sharply; output rose and profits continued to rise despite recession[^harford-ch8].

## Mankiw's four efficiency-wage theories — why paying above equilibrium is profitable

Mankiw defines efficiency wages as above-equilibrium wages paid by firms to increase worker productivity; unemployment from them resembles that from minimum-wage laws and unions — wages above supply-demand balance → surplus labor — but differs because the constraint is voluntary and profit-maximizing, not legally or cartel-imposed: firms are better off keeping wages high even with a surplus[^mankiw-048]. Normal profit-maximizing intuition (wages are large costs, keep them low) is overturned because higher wages can raise efficiency enough to offset cost[^mankiw-048].

1. **Worker health:** Better-paid workers eat a more nutritious diet, are healthier and more productive; profitable to pay high wages for healthy workers rather than low wages for less healthy ones. Most relevant in less-developed countries where inadequate nutrition is a problem; firms fear cutting wages would hurt health/productivity. Less relevant in rich countries like the U.S. where equilibrium wages are well above adequate-diet level[^mankiw-048].
2. **Worker turnover:** Workers quit for many reasons; quit frequency depends on benefits of leaving vs staying; higher pay reduces quits. Turnover is costly — hiring and training new workers, and new hires are less productive than experienced ones. Firms with high turnover have higher production costs; paying above equilibrium to reduce turnover can be profitable[^mankiw-048]. Image 000308 not inspected[^mankiw-048].
3. **Worker quality (adverse selection):** Firms want talented workers but cannot perfectly gauge applicant quality; hiring has randomness. High wage attracts a better applicant pool and raises workforce quality. Cutting wage in a surplus would cause most competent applicants — who have better alternative opportunities — to not apply. If wage-quality effect is strong, paying above clearing is profitable[^mankiw-048].
4. **Worker effort (shirking/moral hazard):** Many jobs allow discretion over effort; firms monitor but monitoring is costly and imperfect, not all shirkers are caught immediately. High wages make workers eager to keep jobs and motivate best effort; at clearing wage, fired workers could quickly find same-wage jobs elsewhere, so less reason not to shirk. Raising wage above equilibrium provides incentive not to shirk[^mankiw-048]. Image 000309 not inspected[^mankiw-048].

Mankiw's Ford case adds assembly-line interdependence: In 1914 Ford's $5 workday was about twice the going wage, far above the balancing wage; long lines formed exceeding needed workers[^mankiw-048]. Effects matched theory — turnover fell, absenteeism fell, productivity rose so much that production costs fell despite higher wages; Ford called it "one of the finest cost-cutting moves we ever made," and an historian noted it improved discipline, loyal interest, and personal efficiency[^mankiw-048]. Why Ford and not others earlier? Assembly lines made workers highly interdependent — if one absent or slow, others cannot complete tasks — so low turnover, high effort, and high quality became more valuable, making efficiency wages a better strategy for Ford than for other businesses then[^mankiw-048]. ## Efficiency wages as structural unemployment

- **Definition:** *Efficiency wages* — higher than competitive market-clearing but commercially sensible because they secure a loyal, motivated workforce; good for employers and for insiders lucky enough to get the jobs[^harford-ch8].
- **Involuntary outsiders:** inside the factory well-paid insiders, outside lines of unemployed men being hosed in subfreezing temperatures — no difference in skills or willingness, only luck; the textbook perfect market where workers freely walk out as "five-day men" and back into any job no longer described reality[^harford-ch8].
- **Market implication:** better-paid but more productive workers means employers hire fewer people while more people want to work; supply-demand correctly predicts more seekers and fewer jobs than at the clearing wage[^harford-ch8].
- Harford calls this semi-jokingly "inventing" unemployment — not its first existence but a new important source: people who want to work but cannot find jobs because wages are deliberately held above clearing[^harford-ch8].
- Also contributes to structural unemployment alongside minimum-wage laws that set legal minima too high and unemployment benefits that raise the pay for not working[^harford-ch8].
