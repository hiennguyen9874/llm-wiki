# Macroeconomic Theory of the Open Economy — Loanable Funds, Foreign-Currency Exchange, and Policy Effects

Mankiw's Ch.19 takes real GDP (determined by factor supplies and technology) and the price level (adjusting money supply to money demand) as given and asks what determines the trade balance and exchange rate in an open economy[^mankiw-057]. Using supply and demand in two linked markets — loanable funds and foreign-currency exchange — the real interest rate balances national saving against domestic investment plus net capital outflow, and the real exchange rate balances dollars supplied for NCO against dollars demanded for NX[^mankiw-057]. Net capital outflow is the link: it is demand in the first market and supply in the second, and it falls when the domestic real interest rate rises[^mankiw-057]. The model predicts U.S. budget deficits raise the interest rate, crowd out investment, appreciate the dollar and push NX toward deficit (the 1980s twin deficits); trade restrictions appreciate the dollar but leave NX unchanged because NX = NCO = S − I; and capital flight raises the domestic interest rate, raises NCO and depreciates the currency[^mankiw-057].

## 19-1 Supply and demand for loanable funds and for foreign-currency exchange

The model highlights the forces that determine `S, I, NCO, NX` and the two relative prices `r` (present vs future goods) and `E` (domestic vs foreign goods), applying supply and demand simultaneously to two related markets[^mankiw-057].

### 19-1a The market for loanable funds

Builds on Ch.13's single loanable-funds market but with open-economy identity[^mankiw-057]:

```
S = I + NCO
Saving = Domestic investment + Net capital outflow
```

- **Supply:** national saving `S` — the domestically generated flow of resources available for capital accumulation[^mankiw-057].
- **Demand:** `I + NCO` — anyone wanting loanable funds to buy domestic capital (`I`) or a foreign asset (`NCO`)[^mankiw-057]. `NCO` can be positive (outflow — purchase overseas adds to demand) or negative (inflow — foreign resources reduce demand for domestic funds)[^mankiw-057].

**Interest-rate dependence:**

- Higher real interest rate → encourages saving → raises quantity of loanable funds supplied (supply slopes upward)[^mankiw-057].
- Higher real rate → makes borrowing for capital projects costlier → discourages `I` and reduces quantity demanded (demand slopes downward)[^mankiw-057].
- Higher U.S. real rate also makes U.S. bonds more attractive to U.S. and foreign mutual funds (U.S. vs German government-bond example) → discourages Americans from buying foreign assets and encourages foreigners to buy U.S. assets → reduces U.S. NCO[^mankiw-057].

**Figure 1 — The Market for Loanable Funds** (image 000359): upward supply from `S`, downward demand from `I+NCO`; equilibrium `r` where shortage would push rate up and surplus would push rate down; at equilibrium saving exactly balances desired domestic investment plus NCO[^mankiw-057].

### 19-1b The market for foreign-currency exchange

Second identity from Ch.18[^mankiw-057]:

```
NCO = NX
Net capital outflow = Net exports
```

Surplus `NX > 0` → foreigners buying more U.S. goods than Americans buying foreign goods → Americans must be buying foreign assets `NCO > 0`; deficit `NX < 0` → Americans financing net purchases by selling assets abroad `NCO < 0`[^mankiw-057].

- **Supply of dollars:** `NCO` — quantity of dollars supplied to buy foreign assets (e.g., U.S. mutual fund needs dollars→yen to buy Japanese government bond)[^mankiw-057].
- **Demand for dollars:** `NX` — quantity of dollars demanded to buy U.S. net exports (e.g., Japanese airline needs yen→dollars to buy Boeing plane)[^mankiw-057].
- **Price that balances:** the **real exchange rate** — relative price of domestic and foreign goods, a key determinant of NX[^mankiw-057]. Appreciation → U.S. goods more expensive → exports fall, imports rise → NX falls → quantity of dollars demanded falls[^mankiw-057].

**Figure 2 — The Market for Foreign-Currency Exchange** (image 000360): demand slopes downward (higher real rate reduces dollars demanded for NX); supply is vertical because quantity of dollars supplied for NCO does not depend on real exchange rate — NCO depends on real interest rate, taken as given in this market[^mankiw-057].

**Why supply vertical — exchange-rate offset:** a higher dollar makes foreign assets cheaper to buy but also makes future dividends/returns (in yen) buy fewer dollars when converted back; the cost and benefit effects offset, so NCO does not depend on real exchange rate in the model[^mankiw-057].

Exchange-rate adjustment mirrors any price: shortage of dollars below equilibrium pushes dollar up; surplus above equilibrium pushes it down; at equilibrium foreign demand for U.S. net exports exactly balances American supply of dollars for NCO[^mankiw-057].

**FYI: Purchasing-power parity as a special case:** PPP assumes net exports are highly responsive to small real-rate changes, so demand in Figure 2 would be horizontal at the PPP level instead of downward-sloping[^mankiw-057]. The preceding chapter's PPP is thus the horizontal-demand special case; allowing imperfect substitutability and trade costs gives the downward slope and allows the real rate to change over time as observed[^mankiw-057].

QuickQuiz (19-1): supply/demand sources — loanable funds: supply from national saving, demand from domestic investment + NCO; foreign-currency exchange: supply from NCO, demand from NX[^mankiw-057].

## 19-2 Equilibrium in the open economy

### 19-2a Net capital outflow: the link between the two markets

Recap identities `S = I + NCO` and `NCO = NX`[^mankiw-057]:

- Loanable-funds market: `S` = supply, `I+NCO` = demand, `r` balances[^mankiw-057].
- Foreign-exchange market: `NCO` = supply, `NX` = demand, real exchange rate balances[^mankiw-057].
- `NCO` links them: American buying a foreign asset must finance in U.S. loanable-funds market (demand) and must supply dollars in exchange market (supply)[^mankiw-057].
- Key determinant of `NCO` is real interest rate — high home rate → domestic assets more attractive → low NCO; Figure 3 shows negative relationship, allowing negative NCO (inflow) with zero marked explicitly[^mankiw-057].

### 19-2b Simultaneous equilibrium in two markets

**Figure 4 — The Real Equilibrium in an Open Economy** (images 000363–000364) — three panels[^mankiw-057]:

- **(a) Market for Loanable Funds:** supply `S` and demand `I+NCO` determine equilibrium real interest rate `r`[^mankiw-057].
- **(b) Net Capital Outflow:** `r` from panel (a) determines `NCO` via downward-sloping curve[^mankiw-057].
- **(c) Market for Foreign-Currency Exchange:** `NCO` from panel (b) provides vertical supply of dollars; downward demand from `NX`; equilibrium real exchange rate `E` balances dollars supplied for foreign assets against dollars demanded for net exports[^mankiw-057].

Two relative prices adjust simultaneously: `r` (goods present vs future) and `E` (domestic vs foreign goods); as they do they determine `S, I, NCO, NX`[^mankiw-057]. Any shift analysis uses the Ch.4 three-step method: which curve shifts, which direction, compare equilibria[^mankiw-057].

QuickQuiz (19-2): the two markets are loanable funds and foreign-currency exchange; the two relative prices are the real interest rate and the real exchange rate[^mankiw-057].

**FYI: Disentangling supply and demand — arbitrariness:** like apple-orchard self-consumption being either demand increase or supply decrease, the model's division is semantic[^mankiw-057]. In loanable funds `S = I + NCO` treats `NCO` as demand, but `S − NCO = I` would treat outflow as reduction in supply of domestic funds — first emphasizes domestically generated funds whether used home or abroad, second emphasizes funds available for domestic investment whether generated home or abroad[^mankiw-057]. In foreign exchange, NX as demand and NCO as supply is a convention: importing a Japanese car is modeled as decrease in dollars demanded (NX falls) rather than increase in dollars supplied; a Japanese citizen buying a U.S. bond is decrease in dollars supplied (NCO falls) rather than increase in dollars demanded — useful for policy analysis[^mankiw-057].

## 19-3 How policies and events affect an open economy

### 19-3a Government budget deficits

A budget deficit = negative public saving → reduces national saving `S` (private + public) → same logic as closed economy but with open-economy additions[^mankiw-057].

**Three-step analysis — Figure 5 (image 000366):**

1. **Which curve:** initial impact on national saving → supply of loanable funds[^mankiw-057].
2. **Direction:** deficit reduces `S` → supply shifts left `S1 → S2` in panel (a)[^mankiw-057].
3. **New equilibria:**
   - Panel (a): fewer funds → `r1 → r2` rises to balance; borrowers borrow less — movement `A → B` along demand; households/firms reduce capital purchases; as in closed economy, deficits crowd out domestic investment[^mankiw-057].
   - Panel (b): higher `r` → `NCO` falls — domestic saving earns higher return so residents buy fewer foreign assets and foreigners buy more U.S. assets; this fall is part of the reduced quantity demanded in panel (a) `A → B`[^mankiw-057].
   - Panel (c): lower `NCO` → Americans need less foreign currency → supply of dollars shifts left `S1 → S2`; reduced supply causes real exchange rate to appreciate `E1 → E2` — dollar more valuable vs foreign currencies[^mankiw-057]. Appreciation makes U.S. goods more expensive → exports fall, imports rise → `NX` falls; trade balance pushed toward deficit[^mankiw-057].

Chain: deficit → higher `r` → crowding out `I` → lower `NCO` → stronger dollar → lower `NX`[^mankiw-057].

**Twin deficits example — U.S. 1980s:** Reagan-era tax cuts without matching nondefense spending cuts produced large federal deficit; model predicts trade deficit — in fact large trade deficits emerged, nicknamed twin deficits — but not identical twins: many factors beyond fiscal policy affect trade balance[^mankiw-057].

### 19-3b Trade policy

**Definition:** government policy that directly influences quantity of goods/services imported or exported — e.g., **tariff** (tax on imported goods) or **import quota** (limit on quantity of foreign-produced good sold domestically) to support a domestic industry[^mankiw-057].

**Figure 6 — Effects of an Import Quota** (image 000367) — quota on Japanese cars advocated as way to shrink trade deficit:

1. **Which curve:** initial impact on imports → `NX = exports − imports` → affects `NX` → demand for dollars in foreign-exchange market[^mankiw-057].
2. **Direction:** quota reduces imports at any real rate → `NX` rises at any rate → increased demand for dollars `D1 → D2` in panel (c)[^mankiw-057].
3. **New equilibria:** panel (c) dollar appreciates `E1 → E2`; panels (a) and (b) — nothing happens in loanable funds or to `NCO` (real rate unchanged, so `NCO` unchanged)[^mankiw-057]. Because `NCO` unchanged, `NX` cannot change — puzzling at first since imports fell[^mankiw-057].

**Resolution:** appreciation makes domestic goods more expensive → encourages imports and discourages exports, offsetting direct `NX` increase from quota. Imports and exports both fall, but `NX = exports − imports` stays same[^mankiw-057].

General result via identity[^mankiw-057]:

```
NX = NCO = S − I
```

Trade policies do not alter `S` or `I`, so for given `S, I` the real exchange rate adjusts to keep trade balance unchanged regardless of tariff/quota[^mankiw-057]. The famous implication: *trade policies do not affect the trade balance*[^mankiw-057].

**Micro vs macro effects:** overall balance stays same but specific firms/industries/countries affected: quota helps General Motors (less foreign competition) but hurts Boeing vs Airbus (stronger dollar makes U.S. aircraft less competitive — exports fall, imports rise); U.S. net exports of cars rise but net exports of planes fall; net exports to Japan rise but to Europe fall — composition shifts, level does not[^mankiw-057]. Economists usually oppose such restrictions: free trade allows specialization and makes all residents better off; restrictions reduce gains from trade[^mankiw-057].

### 19-3c Political instability and capital flight

**Capital flight:** large and sudden movement of funds out of a country to perceived safe havens[^mankiw-057].

**Mexico 1994** — political instability including assassination of prominent leader made world markets nervous; investors sold Mexican assets for U.S. and other safe-haven assets; analyze from Mexico's perspective[^mankiw-057].

**Figure 7 — Effects of Capital Flight** (images 000368–000369):

1. **Which curves:** selling Mexican assets → raises Mexican `NCO` → affects `NCO` curve and supply of pesos in exchange market; because demand for loanable funds = `I + NCO`, also affects loanable-funds demand[^mankiw-057].
2. **Direction:** higher `NCO` → greater demand for loanable funds to finance purchases abroad → demand shifts right `D1 → D2` in panel (a); and `NCO` curve shifts right `NCO1 → NCO2` in panel (b) (higher at any `r`)[^mankiw-057].
3. **New equilibria:**
   - Panel (a): increased demand → Mexican real interest rate rises `r1 → r2`[^mankiw-057].
   - Panel (b): `NCO` rises (higher rate only partly offsets the initial shift)[^mankiw-057].
   - Panel (c): supply of pesos rises `S1 → S2` → peso depreciates `E1 → E2`, peso less valuable vs other currencies[^mankiw-057].

The truncated source stops mid-sentence in panel (b) discussion, but the depreciation raises Mexican `NX` to match higher `NCO` via `NCO = NX`[^mankiw-057].
