# Federal Reserve System and monetary policy — Fed organization, FOMC, and open-market operations

When an economy uses fiat money, an agency must regulate the system; in the United States that agency is the Federal Reserve — the Fed — a central bank that oversees banking and controls the quantity of money made available, the money supply[^mankiw-16-1]. Central banks elsewhere include the Bank of England, Bank of Japan, and European Central Bank[^mankiw-16-1]. The Fed's primary tool is the open-market operation — purchase and sale of U.S. government bonds — with a helicopter/vacuum metaphor for money creation/destruction[^mankiw-16-1].

## Historical origins — from Hamilton to Jekyll Island

America's central bank was politically contested from the start, a pattern Lowenstein argues persists — effective policy rewarded with populist scorn[^mankiw-16-jekyll].

- **1791 First charter**: At Alexander Hamilton's urging Congress chartered a national bank — the ur-Fed[^mankiw-16-jekyll]. Thomas Jefferson opposed, mistrusting banks (agriculture more virtuous) and fearing strong central government; after 20 years Jeffersonians let the charter expire — leading to **ruinous inflation**[^mankiw-16-jekyll].
- **1817 Second Bank of the United States**: chartered to provide a more uniform currency and improve public finances; successful but Andrew Jackson despised it as a tool of East Coast elites and it too was abolished[^mankiw-16-jekyll].
- **19th century without lender of last resort**: Unlike Europe, the U.S. had no lender of last resort; result was **frequent panics and credit shortages**, yet even credit-starved farmers preferred the status quo, fearing a government bank would tyrannize the people, perhaps in cahoots with Wall Street — echoing Jefferson and Jackson[^mankiw-16-jekyll].
- **1907 panic** virtually shut down the banking system and revived reform pressure, but popular mistrust remained so pronounced reformers were afraid to go public[^mankiw-16-jekyll].
- **Jekyll Island, November 1910**: Powerful Senator Nelson W. Aldrich (Rhode Island Republican) invited a secretive group masquerading as **duck hunters** aboard his private rail car from New York (light snow), then by launch to **Jekyll Island**, home of an exclusive club amid pine and palmetto groves[^mankiw-16-jekyll]. Party included reform-minded banker **Paul Warburg** (toting a hunting rifle with no interest in hunting), a member of the powerful **Morgan bank**, an **assistant U.S. Treasury secretary**, and **Frank Vanderlip**, head of the largest bank National City[^mankiw-16-jekyll]. On inquiring about the errand, Vanderlip was told: “It may be a wild-goose chase; it may be the biggest thing you and I ever did” (Warburg)[^mankiw-16-jekyll]. Over a week of sumptuous meals (wild turkey, freshly scalloped oysters) they **mapped a draft of what became the Federal Reserve Act**, wrestling with questions still alive today: how to apportion power over the economy between Washington and localities, and how the central bank should set interest rates and the money supply[^mankiw-16-jekyll].
- **Legislative path**: Congress was never told Aldrich's bill had been drafted by Wall Street moguls; his bill did not pass but became the **basis of the Federal Reserve Act that Woodrow Wilson signed in 1913** — the third permanent central-bank charter[^mankiw-16-1][^mankiw-16-jekyll]. When the trip was later revealed, extremists used the **stranger-than-fiction** conclave to claim the Fed was a bankers' plot against the American people — for conspiracy theorists the Jekyll meeting became a metaphor for the Fed itself[^mankiw-16-jekyll]. Irony: fearing irrational suspicion, Aldrich's crew resorted to a plot that **deepened paranoia**[^mankiw-16-jekyll].
- **Contemporary echo**: Lowenstein (Los Angeles Times, Nov 2, 2015, author of *America's Bank: The Epic Struggle to Create the Federal Reserve*) notes opinion surveys rank the Fed just above the IRS in unpopularity, a font of conspiracy theories that it is debauching the currency, despite having rescued the economy in 2008; dissatisfaction persists in Congress with bills to strip autonomy, subject monetary decisions to elected politicians, and explore return to gold standard[^mankiw-16-jekyll]. Yet financiers' motives were actually patriotic — Aldrich had studied European central banks and wanted expert help — and despite clandestine tactics the Fed today is **more transparent than ever** thanks to reforms by Chairman Ben S. Bernanke, and **no less necessary than in 1791**; paranoia is unjustified, as it has always been[^mankiw-16-jekyll].

## Creation and organization

- Created in 1913 after the 1907 bank-failure panic convinced Congress a central bank was needed to ensure banking-system health[^mankiw-16-1][^mankiw-16-jekyll].
- Run by the **Board of Governors**: seven members appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate, with 14-year terms — like lifetime judicial appointments to insulate from short-term politics while formulating monetary policy[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Chair** is most important among the seven: directs staff, presides over meetings, testifies regularly before Congress; appointed by president to 4-year term — as of the 8th edition press time, Janet Yellen, former economics professor appointed by President Obama in 2014[^mankiw-16-1].
- System consists of the Board in Washington, D.C., plus **12 regional Federal Reserve Banks** in major cities; regional presidents chosen by each bank's board drawn from regional banking/business community[^mankiw-16-1]. Krugman/Wells characterizes this arrangement as neither wholly governmental nor private: presidential nomination and Senate confirmation create democratic accountability, while long Board terms and indirect appointment insulate decisions from short-term political pressure[^krugman-ch14-078].
- The regional banks provide banking and supervisory services, including auditing private-sector banks; Figure 14-6 identifies the 12 district-bank cities and places Alaska and Hawaii in the San Francisco district[^krugman-ch14-078].

## Two jobs

1. **Regulate banks and ensure banking-system health** — largely regional banks' responsibility: monitor each bank's financial condition, clear checks and facilitate transactions, and act as a **bank's bank** making loans when banks need to borrow — the **lender of last resort** for those who cannot borrow elsewhere, stabilizing the system when troubled banks run short of cash[^mankiw-16-1].
2. **Control the money supply** — the more important job; decisions concerning the money supply are **monetary policy**, made by the **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)**, which meets about every six weeks in Washington to discuss the economy and consider changes[^mankiw-16-1].

## The FOMC

- Composed of the 7 governors plus 5 of the 12 regional bank presidents — all 12 attend but only 5 vote at a time, rotating over time[^mankiw-16-1].
- The New York Fed president always votes — New York is the traditional financial center and all Fed bond purchases/sales are conducted at its trading desk[^mankiw-16-1]. Krugman/Wells likewise specifies that the remaining four regional-president voting seats rotate among the other eleven presidents and that the Board chair normally chairs the FOMC[^krugman-ch14-078].

## How the Fed changes the money supply — the helicopter metaphor

- Through the FOMC the Fed can **increase or decrease the number of dollars** in the economy — metaphorically printing bills and dropping them by helicopter to increase, or using a giant vacuum to suck them from wallets to decrease[^mankiw-16-1].
- In practice it is more complex and subtle, but the metaphor captures the meaning of monetary policy[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Primary tool: open-market operations** — purchase and sale of U.S. government bonds (certificates of indebtedness of the federal government)[^mankiw-16-1].
  - **To increase the money supply**: Fed creates dollars and uses them to **buy government bonds** from the public in bond markets; after the purchase those dollars are in the public's hands — an open-market purchase increases the money supply[^mankiw-16-1].
  - **To decrease**: Fed **sells government bonds** from its portfolio to the public; dollars paid to the Fed leave the public's hands — an open-market sale decreases the money supply[^mankiw-16-1].

## Why it matters

- Changes in the money supply profoundly affect the economy, per two of Mankiw's Ten Principles from Chapter 1: prices rise when the government prints too much money, and society faces a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment[^mankiw-16-1].
- Hence the Fed's decisions are key determinants of the **long-run rate of inflation** and **short-run employment and production**; the Fed chair has been called the second most powerful person in the United States[^mankiw-16-1].
- Consistent with the book's long-run focus in the prior four chapters, the next chapter examines long-run effects of money changes; short-run effects are taken up later[^mankiw-16-1].
