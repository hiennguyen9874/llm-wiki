# Quantity theory of money, classical dichotomy, and the costs of inflation

Inflation is an economy-wide fall in the value of money (1/P), not a rise in the value of ice cream; in the long run the price level equilibrates money supplied by the Fed with money demanded for transactions, so growth in the money supply is the primary cause of inflation — the quantity theory — leaving real variables unchanged (monetary neutrality) while raising the price level, nominal wages, and nominal interest rates via the Fisher effect[^mankiw-052].

## Inflation facts that motivate the theory

- **Definition:** inflation is increase in overall level of prices; inflation rate is percentage change in CPI, GDP deflator, or other price index; deflation is falling overall prices[^mankiw-052].
- **U.S. long run:** ~3.6% per year over past 80 years → seventeenfold increase in price level; ice-cream cone 3 cents (small) / nickel (large) in grandmother's 1930s Trenton, NJ sweet shop → at least a couple dollars today[^mankiw-052].
- **Not inevitable:** 19th-century deflation — average price level 23% lower in 1896 than in 1880, major issue in 1896 presidential election as farmers' crop-price declines reduced ability to service debts[^mankiw-052].
- **Variation:** 2005–2015 avg 1.2% per year vs 1970s 7.8% per year (price level more than doubled that decade); Reagan attacked Carter's high inflation in 1980 election; Ford's 1974 WIN button — "Whip Inflation Now" — calling inflation "public enemy number one"[^mankiw-052].
- **International 2015:** U.S. 0.1%, China 1.5%, India 4.9%, Russia 15%, Venezuela 84%[^mankiw-052].
- **Hyperinflation:** extraordinarily high inflation; generally defined as >50% per month → >100-fold per year[^mankiw-052]. Zimbabwe February 2008 central bank announced 24,000% with independent estimates higher[^mankiw-052].

> **Ten Principles preview:** Prices rise when the government prints too much money — formalized as quantity theory, with lineage David Hume (18th century) and Milton Friedman ("Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon")[^mankiw-052].

## 17-1a The level of prices and the value of money

- Lesson: inflation is more about value of money than value of goods; a nickel→$1 cone rise likely reflects money becoming less valuable, not ice cream becoming more enjoyable[^mankiw-052].
- Commentators who attribute CPI rise to "coffee +20%, heating oil +30%" miss economy-wide nature concerning medium of exchange[^mankiw-052].
- Two views of P (price level via CPI/GDP deflator):
  - P = dollars needed to buy basket of goods/services[^mankiw-052].
  - 1/P = value of money measured in goods/services; quantity of goods bought with $1 = 1/P; if P = $2 per cone, $1 = 1/2 cone; if P = $3, $1 = 1/3 cone[^mankiw-052].
- Rise in price level means lower value of money; each dollar buys smaller quantity[^mankiw-052].

## 17-1b Money supply, money demand, and monetary equilibrium

**Supply:** determined by Federal Reserve together with banking system; Fed sells bonds → receives dollars → contracts supply; buys bonds → pays dollars → expands supply; bank deposits with fractional reserves amplify via money multiplier; for this chapter, quantity supplied is taken as Fed-controlled policy variable[^mankiw-052].

**Demand:** reflects how much wealth people want to hold in liquid form — influenced by credit-card reliance, ATM availability, and interest foregone vs bonds (Chapter 21) — but standout variable is average price level[^mankiw-052]. People hold money as medium of exchange; higher prices → more dollars per transaction → higher quantity demanded. Higher price level (lower value) increases quantity demanded[^mankiw-052].

**Long-run equilibrium:** money supply and demand brought into balance by overall price level (contrast short-run where interest rates play key role, studied later)[^mankiw-052]. If price level above equilibrium → people want more money than Fed created → price level must fall; if below → want less → price level rises. At equilibrium price level, quantity demanded = quantity supplied[^mankiw-052].

- **Figure 1 (description):** horizontal axis quantity of money; left vertical axis value of money 1/P, right vertical axis price level P inverted (low P top, high P bottom). Vertical supply curve (Fed-fixed), downward-sloping demand curve (low value/high price → larger quantity demanded). Equilibrium point A balances supply and demand, determining value and price level[^mankiw-052].

## 17-1c Effects of a monetary injection

- Thought experiment from equilibrium: Fed doubles money supply by printing bills and dropping from helicopters, or realistically buying government bonds in open-market operations[^mankiw-052].
- **Figure 2:** supply shifts right MS1 → MS2, equilibrium A → B; value of money falls from 1/2 to 1/4, price level rises from 2 to 4[^mankiw-052].
- Interpretation: more plentiful dollars → higher price level → each dollar less valuable[^mankiw-052].

> **quantity theory of money** — a theory asserting that the quantity of money available determines the price level and that the growth rate in quantity determines inflation rate[^mankiw-052].

## 17-1d Adjustment process (brief look)

- Immediate effect is excess supply of money at prevailing price level; people try to get rid of excess by buying goods/services or making loans (buying bonds, depositing in savings) allowing others to buy[^mankiw-052].
- Injection increases demand for goods/services, but economy's ability to supply is unchanged — determined by labor, physical/human capital, natural resources, technological knowledge (production and growth chapter) — none altered by money injection[^mankiw-052].
- Greater demand causes prices to rise; higher price level increases quantity demanded until new equilibrium B where quantity demanded = quantity supplied; overall price level adjusts to balance supply and demand[^mankiw-052].

## 17-1e Classical dichotomy and monetary neutrality

- **Two groups:**
  - Nominal variables — measured in monetary units (e.g., corn farmer's dollar income, nominal GDP)[^mankiw-052].
  - Real variables — measured in physical units (e.g., quantity of corn in bushels, real GDP not influenced by current prices)[^mankiw-052].
- Separation called **classical dichotomy** (classical = earlier thinkers)[^mankiw-052].
- **Prices:** most quoted in money → nominal, but relative price (e.g., corn $2/bushel, wheat $1/bushel → corn = 2 bushels wheat) cancels dollars → real variable[^mankiw-052].
- Applications: real wage (dollar wage adjusted for inflation) and real interest rate (nominal adjusted for inflation) are real variables measuring exchange of goods/services for labor or over time[^mankiw-052].
- **Different forces:** classical analysis says nominal variables influenced by monetary system, money largely irrelevant for real variables. Real GDP, saving, investment, real interest rates, unemployment determined by technology and factor supplies without mentioning money[^mankiw-052].
- Doubling money supply doubles price level, dollar wage, all dollar values; production, employment, real wages, real rates unchanged — irrelevance called **monetary neutrality** (money as yardstick analogy: shortening yard 36→18 inches doubles measured distances nominally but not actual distances)[^mankiw-052].
- **Realism:** not completely — short-run (within a year or two) monetary changes affect real variables; Hume doubted short-run neutrality too (studied later as short-run non-neutrality explains why Fed changes money over time). Over a decade, changes have significant nominal but negligible real effects; neutrality is good long-run description[^mankiw-052].

### Krugman/Wells: a money-level increase and the nominal interest rate

Starting at long-run macroeconomic equilibrium (potential output), an increase in the money supply initially shifts the vertical money-supply curve right and lowers the equilibrium interest rate. As the aggregate price level rises in the long run, however, money demand at every interest rate rises in the same proportion as prices. Under monetary neutrality—where the price level rises proportionally with the money supply—the money-demand shift matches the supply increase, so the interest rate returns to its original level.[^krugman-ch15-085]

Figure 15-12 depicts this horizon distinction: equilibrium moves from $E_1$ at $r_1$ to a short-run $E_2$ at lower $r_2$, then to long-run $E_3$ at the original $r_1$. The result concerns a change in the **level** of the money supply and the interest rate; it is compatible with the Fisher-effect account that a persistent change in money-growth and expected inflation changes the long-run nominal interest rate.[^krugman-ch15-085][^mankiw-052]

**International evidence is supportive but not exact.** The source's 1983–2018 country scatterplot places average annual money-supply growth and price-level growth broadly near a 45-degree line; it notes that other determinants of prices keep the relationship from being exact. This is descriptive textbook evidence consistent with long-run monetary neutrality, not proof that money is the only cause of inflation in each country.[^krugman-ch15-085]

## 17-1f Velocity and the quantity equation

- **Velocity of money (V):** speed at which typical dollar travels wallet-to-wallet; number of times per year typical bill used to pay for newly produced good/service[^mankiw-052].

$$V = (P \times Y) / M$$

where P = price level (GDP deflator), Y = real GDP (quantity of output), M = quantity of money[^mankiw-052]. Example: 100 pizzas × $10 = $1,000 nominal with $50 money → V = 20 times per year[^mankiw-052].

Rearranged **quantity equation:**

$$M \times V = P \times Y$$

Quantity of money times velocity equals price of output times amount of output; relates M to nominal value of output P×Y[^mankiw-052]. Increase in M must be reflected in P, Y, or falling V[^mankiw-052].

> **quantity equation** — M×V = P×Y[^mankiw-052].

- **Figure 3 (U.S. since 1960):** nominal GDP, M2, and velocity (ratio) scaled to 100 in 1960; money and nominal GDP >30-fold increase, velocity relatively stable (not exactly constant but not dramatically changed)[^mankiw-052].
- Hence for some purposes constant-velocity approximation is reasonable[^mankiw-052].

**Five steps to equilibrium price level and inflation rate:**

1. Velocity relatively stable over time[^mankiw-052].
2. Stable velocity → central bank changes in M cause proportionate changes in nominal output P×Y[^mankiw-052].
3. Output Y primarily determined by factor supplies (labor, physical/human capital, natural resources) and production technology; money neutral, does not affect output[^mankiw-052].
4. With Y determined by real factors, proportional changes in P×Y are reflected in price level P[^mankiw-052].
5. Therefore rapid money-supply growth → high inflation[^mankiw-052].

These five steps are essence of quantity theory[^mankiw-052].

### Evidence: money and prices during four hyperinflations

- Hyperinflations provide natural experiments; >50% per month → >100-fold per year[^mankiw-052].
- **Figure 4 — Austria, Hungary, Germany, Poland 1920s:** quantity of money and price-level index graphed on logarithmic scales (equal vertical distance = equal percentage change); Quantity of money and price level almost parallel; moderate growth then faster and faster together; when quantity stabilizes, price stabilizes — strong association consistent with quantity theory that money-supply growth is primary cause of inflation. Source: adapted from Thomas J. Sargent, "The End of Four Big Inflations," in Robert Hall, ed., Inflation (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983), pp. 41–93[^mankiw-052].

## 17-1g The inflation tax

- **Why would governments hyperinflate?** Governments use money creation to pay for spending when they have high spending, inadequate tax revenue, limited borrowing ability[^mankiw-052].
- Normally revenue via income/sales taxes and bond sales; alternative is printing money needed[^mankiw-052].
- When government raises revenue by printing, it levies **inflation tax** — not billed like other taxes but subtler: printing raises price level, dollars in wallet become less valuable, like a tax on everyone who holds money[^mankiw-052].

> **inflation tax** — the revenue the government raises by creating money[^mankiw-052].

- **Importance varies:** U.S. recent years <3% of government revenue — trivial; 1770s Continental Congress relied heavily to pay soldiers amid limited tax/borrowing ability → >100-fold price increase in continental dollars, as quantity theory predicts; almost all hyperinflations follow same pattern, ending when fiscal reforms (e.g., spending cuts) eliminate need for inflation tax[^mankiw-052].

#### FYI: Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe

- During first decade of 2000s one of history's most extreme hyperinflations; same pattern: large budget deficits → large money creation → high inflation; ended April 2009 when central bank stopped printing Zimbabwe dollar and nation used foreign currencies (U.S. dollar, South African rand) as medium[^mankiw-052].
- Magnitude via denominations: before hyperinflation Z$1 ≈ >$1, notes like $10; Jan 2008 Reserve Bank issued 10-million-Z$ note ≈ $4; year later 10-trillion-Z$ note ≈ $3; older smaller denominations became worthless — restroom sign: "TOILET PAPER ONLY NO ZIM DOLLARS NO NEWSPAPER" (image 000334)[^mankiw-052].

### Krugman/Wells: high-inflation approximation and the seigniorage spiral

The **classical model of the price level** treats real money balances ($M/P$) as always at their long-run equilibrium: a money-supply increase therefore produces a proportionate price-level increase and no lasting change in real GDP. It deliberately omits the short-run AD–AS expansion; the source argues it is a better approximation under persistently high inflation, when workers and firms adjust wages and prices quickly. Figure 16-1 depicts the omitted path from a short-run output expansion to restored potential output at the higher price level.[^krugman-ch16-087]

For a government financing a deficit by money creation, the source expresses real seigniorage as $\Delta M/P=(\Delta M/M)\times(M/P)$. High inflation makes the public reduce real money balances; if the government still needs a fixed real revenue amount, it must accelerate money growth, which raises inflation and induces a further reduction in $M/P$. This feedback can end in hyperinflation and abandonment of the inflation tax.[^krugman-ch16-087]

The chapter's Venezuela chart is descriptive rather than an independent causal estimate: its reported monthly monetary-base and consumer-price changes rise together—10.6% and 11.6% in 2016, 27.5% and 20.8% in 2017, 37.1% and 81.8% in 2018, and 42.9% and 46.4% in 2019.[^krugman-ch16-087]

## 17-1h The Fisher effect

- Monetary neutrality says money-growth increase raises inflation but not real variables; application to interest rates linking present and future via saving/investment[^mankiw-052].
- **Nominal interest rate** — bank-posted rate, how fast dollar amount rises; **Real interest rate** — corrected for inflation, how fast purchasing power rises[^mankiw-052].

$$\text{Real rate} = \text{Nominal rate} - \text{Inflation rate}$$

e.g., 7% nominal − 3% inflation = 4% real[^mankiw-052].

$$\text{Nominal rate} = \text{Real rate} + \text{Inflation rate}$$

Useful because loanable-funds supply/demand determines real rate, while money-supply growth determines inflation (quantity theory)[^mankiw-052].

- Long run where money is neutral: money-growth change should not affect real rate (real variable); nominal must adjust one-for-one to inflation. When Fed increases money-growth rate, long-run result is higher inflation and higher nominal rate. This adjustment called **Fisher effect** after Irving Fisher (1867–1947)[^mankiw-052].
- **Short vs long run:** Fisher effect maintains long-run perspective; need not hold short-run because inflation may be unanticipated; nominal is set when loan made — surprise jump not reflected, but if high inflation persists people come to expect it. Precisely, Fisher effect says nominal adjusts to **expected** inflation, which moves with actual inflation in long run but not necessarily short run[^mankiw-052].
- **Figure 5 — U.S. since 1960:** nominal rate (three-month Treasury bills) and CPI inflation closely associated; nominal rose early 1960s through 1970s as inflation rose, fell early 1980s–1990s as Fed got inflation under control; both low by historical standards in recent years. Sources: U.S. Department of Treasury; U.S. Department of Labor[^mankiw-052].

**QuickQuiz (Chapter):** If government raises money-supply growth from 5% to 50% per year — prices (and inflation) rise much faster, nominal interest rates rise via Fisher effect; government likely doing this to finance spending via inflation tax when unable to tax or borrow[^mankiw-052].

## 17-2 The costs of inflation

Even though inflation has been low past 20 years, it remains closely watched — one study finds it most mentioned economic term in U.S. newspapers (ahead of unemployment, productivity) — and thought to be serious problem[^mankiw-052]. But are the costs what they seem? Economists agree hyperinflation is terrible; costs of moderate inflation are debated[^mankiw-052].

### 17-2a A fall in purchasing power? The inflation fallacy

- Typical answer: inflation robs purchasing power because each dollar buys fewer goods — seems to lower living standards[^mankiw-052].
- **Fallacy:** when prices rise, buyers pay more but sellers — who earn incomes selling services/labor — also get more; inflation in incomes goes hand-in-hand with inflation in prices, so inflation itself does not reduce real purchasing power[^mankiw-052].
- People believe fallacy by missing monetary neutrality: worker getting 10% raise with 6% inflation → 4% real thinks cheated; real incomes determined by real variables (physical/human capital, natural resources, technology); nominal incomes determined by those plus overall price level. If Fed lowered inflation 6%→0%, nominal raise would fall 10%→4% but real would not rise faster[^mankiw-052].
- If nominal incomes keep pace, why is inflation a problem? Economists have identified several costs where persistent money growth adversely affects real variables[^mankiw-052]. The level of prices is not itself the problem: a proportional currency redenomination changes listed prices, debts, and wages together, leaving real wages and real incomes unchanged. The rate at which prices change is distinct; the source's 1960–2020 U.S. chart shows a broadly rising price level alongside a volatile inflation rate and brief 2009 deflation.[^krugman-ch8-046]

### 17-2b Shoeleather costs

- Inflation like tax on holders of money; tax itself is transfer to government not societal cost, but like other taxes it distorts incentives and creates deadweight losses as people waste resources avoiding it[^mankiw-052].
- **Avoidance:** hold less money by going to bank more often — e.g., withdraw $50 weekly instead of $200 every four weeks, keeping more in interest-bearing savings where inflation doesn't erode[^mankiw-052].
- **Shoeleather cost:** cost of reducing money holdings — time and convenience sacrificed (term from wearing out shoes on trips to bank, not literally shoes)[^mankiw-052].
- Trivial in moderate-inflation U.S., substantial under hyperinflation[^mankiw-052].
- **Bolivia example (Wall Street Journal, Aug 13 1985):** teacher Edgar Miranda paid 25 million pesos monthly must act immediately — hourly value drop, wife rushes to market for month's rice/noodles while he changes remainder to black-market dollars. One six-month period prices at 38,000% annual rate; official last year 2,000%, expected 8,000% that year (others higher), dwarfing Israel 370% and Argentina 1,100%. Day paid: $1 = 500,000 pesos → $50; days later rate 900,000 → $27[^mankiw-052].
- With high inflation, local money is poor store of value — quickly converted to goods or stable dollars. Time/effort to reduce holdings is waste; low-inflation policy would let Miranda hold pesos and put effort to productive use. Shortly after article, Bolivia substantially reduced inflation with more restrictive monetary policy[^mankiw-052].

### 17-2c Menu costs

- Most firms do not change prices daily; typical U.S. firm changes prices about once a year (survey)[^mankiw-053].
- Frequent changes are costly — **menu costs**: costs of deciding on new prices, printing new price lists and catalogs, sending them to dealers and customers, advertising new prices, and dealing with customer annoyance over price changes; term from restaurant cost of printing a new menu[^mankiw-053].
- Inflation increases menu costs that firms must bear. With low U.S. inflation, annual adjustment is appropriate for many firms; when high inflation makes costs rise rapidly, annual adjustment is impractical — during hyperinflations firms must change prices daily or more often to keep up with other prices[^mankiw-053].
- Connects to nominal rigidity: firms change prices infrequently because adjustment is costly, not costless as classical dichotomy assumes in the short run[^mankiw-053].

### 17-2d Relative-price variability and the misallocation of resources

- Suppose Eatabit Eatery prints a new menu every January and leaves prices unchanged for the year. With no inflation, relative prices (its meals vs. other prices) stay constant over the year[^mankiw-053].
- With 12% inflation per year, its relative prices automatically fall by 1% each month; prices are high in early months just after printing and low in later months. Higher inflation → greater automatic variability of relative prices because prices change only once in a while[^mankiw-053].
- Why it matters: market economies rely on relative prices to allocate scarce resources — consumers compare quality and prices to decide what to buy, determining how factors of production are allocated among industries and firms. When inflation distorts relative prices, consumer decisions are distorted and markets are less able to allocate resources to their best use[^mankiw-053].

### 17-2e Inflation-induced tax distortions

- Almost all taxes distort incentives and lead to less efficient allocation; many become worse with inflation because lawmakers fail to account for inflation when writing tax laws[^mankiw-053].
- Economists conclude inflation tends to raise the tax burden on income earned from savings[^mankiw-053].
- **Capital gains example:** Buy IBM stock for $30 in 1988, sell for $130 in 2016 → nominal gain $100 taxed as income, but price level doubled 1988–2016 so $30 in 1988 ≡ $60 in 2016 purchasing power; real gain only $70, yet tax assessed on $100. Inflation exaggerates capital gains and inadvertently increases tax burden[^mankiw-053].
- **Interest income example — Table 1:** Two economies both tax interest at 25%, real rate 4%:
  - *Economy A* (price stability: 0% inflation, 4% nominal): 25% tax reduces real rate 4% → 3% (1% tax drag)[^mankiw-053].
  - *Economy B* (8% inflation, 12% nominal via Fisher effect): tax on full 12% nominal leaves 9% after-tax nominal; minus 8% inflation = 1% after-tax real. Same 25% tax reduces real rate 4% → 1% (3% drag)[^mankiw-053].
- Because after-tax real rate drives saving incentive, saving is much less attractive with inflation (Economy B) than with stable prices (Economy A). Many other provisions interact similarly[^mankiw-053].
- Since saving provides resources for investment — key to long-run growth — inflation-raised tax burden tends to depress long-run growth (size debated, no consensus)[^mankiw-053].
- **Solution other than eliminating inflation: index the tax system** — e.g., adjust purchase price by price index and tax only real gain; tax only real interest by excluding inflation-compensation portion[^mankiw-053].
- Partial progress: income brackets where rates change are adjusted automatically each year via CPI, but capital gains and interest income remain unindexed[^mankiw-053].
- Ideal would leave real tax liability unchanged by inflation; actual code far from perfect, and fuller indexation would add complexity to already complex code[^mankiw-053].

|  | Economy A (price stability) | Economy B (inflation) |
|---|---|---|
| Real interest rate | 4% | 4% |
| Inflation rate | 0% | 8% |
| Nominal interest rate (real + inflation) | 4% | 12% |
| Reduced interest due to 25% tax (0.25×nominal) | 1% | 3% |
| After-tax nominal interest rate (0.75×nominal) | 3% | 9% |
| After-tax real interest rate (after-tax nominal − inflation) | 3% | 1% |

### 17-2f Confusion and inconvenience

- **Yardstick analogy:** If asked "This year the yard is 36 inches. How long next year?" people say keep 36 inches — changing complications are needless[^mankiw-053].
- Money as unit of account is the yardstick for quoting prices and recording debts; the Fed's job is like the Bureau of Standards — ensuring reliability of a common unit of measurement. Creating inflation by expanding money supply erodes the real value of the unit of account[^mankiw-053].
- Costs are difficult to judge but real: tax code mismeasures real incomes (as above); accountants mismeasure firms' earnings when prices rise, because dollars at different times have different real values so profit = revenue − costs becomes more complicated, making investors less able to distinguish successful from unsuccessful firms and impeding financial markets' role of allocating saving to alternative investments[^mankiw-053].

### 17-2g A special cost of unexpected inflation: arbitrary redistributions of wealth

- Costs above occur even if inflation is steady and predictable; unexpected inflation adds arbitrary redistribution unrelated to merit or need because many loans are specified in money (unit of account)[^mankiw-053].
- **Sam–Bigbank example:** Sam Student borrows $20,000 at 7% for 10 years for college → owes $40,000 after compounding. Real value depends on inflation: hyperinflation → wages and prices so high Sam repays out of pocket change (enriched at Bigbank's expense, real debt diminished); major deflation → wages and prices fall, $40,000 burden heavier than anticipated (Bigbank enriched at Sam's expense)[^mankiw-053].
- Unexpected price changes redistribute between debtors and creditors; hyperinflation enriches Sam, deflation enriches Bigbank[^mankiw-053].
- If inflation were predictable, parties could include it via Fisher effect in the nominal rate; if hard to predict it imposes unwanted risk on both[^mankiw-053].
- **Level–volatility link:** inflation is especially volatile and uncertain when average rate is high. Evidence across countries: low-average countries (e.g., Germany late 20th century) have stable inflation; high-average countries (many in Latin America) have unstable inflation; no known examples of high, stable inflation[^mankiw-053]. Pursuing high-inflation monetary policy therefore bears both costs of high expected inflation and arbitrary redistributions of unexpected inflation[^mankiw-053].

### 17-2h Inflation is bad, but deflation may be worse

- Recent U.S. history inflation is norm, but price level fell in late 19th century and early 1930s; Japan 1998–2012 experienced 4% decline[^mankiw-053].
- **Friedman rule:** Milton Friedman suggested small predictable deflation may be desirable — deflation lowers nominal interest rate via Fisher effect, and lower nominal rate reduces cost of holding money; shoeleather costs minimized by nominal rate close to zero, which requires deflation equal to real interest rate[^mankiw-053].
- **Costs that mirror inflation:** falling price level also induces menu costs and relative-price variability; rarely as steady and predictable as Friedman prescribed, more often comes as surprise redistributing wealth toward creditors and away from debtors — particularly painful because debtors often poorer[^mankiw-053].
- **Deflation as symptom:** perhaps most important, deflation often arises from broader macroeconomic difficulties — e.g., monetary contraction that reduces overall demand for goods and services (studied in later chapters), causing falling incomes and rising unemployment. Deflation is often a symptom of deeper economic problems[^mankiw-053].

### Case study: The Wizard of Oz and the free-silver debate

- From 1880 to 1896 U.S. price level fell 23%. Unanticipated, it caused major redistribution: western farmers were debtors, eastern bankers creditors; falling prices raised real value of debts, enriching banks at farmers' expense[^mankiw-053].
- Populist solution: **free coinage of silver** — U.S. on gold standard (gold quantity determined money supply and price level); advocates wanted silver + gold as money to increase money supply, push up price level, reduce real debt burden. Common slogan: "We Are Mortgaged. All but Our Votes."[^mankiw-053].
- Central to 1890s politics; 1896 Democratic nominee William Jennings Bryan's convention speech: "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns. You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." Bryan lost to Republican William McKinley; U.S. remained on gold standard[^mankiw-053].
- L. Frank Baum, midwestern journalist author of *The Wonderful Wizard of Oz* (1900), made characters represent the battle, per Hugh Rockoff *Journal of Political Economy* 1990 interpretation [^mankiw-053]:
  - Dorothy — traditional American values; Toto — Prohibitionist/Teetotaler party; Scarecrow — farmers; Tin Woodsman — industrial workers; Cowardly Lion — William Jennings Bryan; Munchkins — citizens of the East; Wicked Witch of the East — Grover Cleveland; Wicked Witch of the West — William McKinley; Wizard — Marcus Alonzo Hanna, Republican Party chairman; Oz — ounce of gold; Yellow Brick Road — gold standard; silver slippers (changed to ruby in 1939 Technicolor film to show off color) — silver's magical power[^mankiw-053].
- Populists lost the debate but eventually got desired monetary expansion: 1898 Klondike River gold discovery plus South Africa mines increased gold supplies, money supply and price level rose, and within 15 years U.S. prices returned to 1880s levels, easing farmers' debts[^mankiw-053].

## 17-3 Conclusion — causes, costs, and the long-run vs short-run

- **Primary cause** of inflation is growth in quantity of money; when central bank creates money in large quantities, value of money falls quickly; to maintain stable prices must maintain strict control over money supply[^mankiw-053].
- **Costs are more subtle:** shoeleather costs, menu costs, increased variability of relative prices, unintended changes in tax liabilities, confusion and inconvenience, and arbitrary redistributions of wealth[^mankiw-053]. Whether total large or small depends on level — huge during hyperinflation; during moderate inflation (<10% per year) size is more open to debate[^mankiw-053].
- **Incomplete analysis:** reducing money growth makes prices rise less rapidly as quantity theory suggests, yet transition to lower inflation will likely disrupt production and employment — even though monetary policy is neutral in long run, it has profound effects on real variables in short run. Later chapters examine reasons for short-run nonneutrality to deepen understanding of causes and effects of inflation[^mankiw-053].

**Mankiw QuickQuiz — six costs list:** shoeleather (reduced money holdings), menu costs (frequent price adjustment), relative-price variability/misallocation, inflation-induced tax distortions (nominal capital gains/interest), confusion/inconvenience (eroded unit of account, accounting), arbitrary redistribution between debtors and creditors when unexpected[^mankiw-053].
