# Inequality trends, measurement, and drivers

Global between-country inequality has fallen rapidly since 1990 when weighted by population (driven by China and others catching up), but rising within-country inequality has largely offset it, so overall global inequality has only recently begun a modest decline for the first time since the industrial revolution; Anglophone concentration at the top and skill-biased technological change linked to schooling quality are the most salient recent drivers[^harford-inequality].

## Measuring inequality

- **Gini coefficient:** 0% = perfect equality, 100% = one person makes all income. Illustrative CIA *World Factbook* figures (1998): Brazil 61%, US 45%, UK 34%, France 33%, Finland 27%[^harford-inequality].
- **Top income shares:** focus on top 1% or 0.1% has become analytically important because their incomes now materially move economy-wide averages[^harford-inequality]. US poorest 99% (<~$370k) saw average income grow only 5.8% between 1993 and 2011 versus 13.1% for the whole population over 18 years — the gap reflects very high pay at the top[^harford-inequality].

## Global inequality — between versus within countries

Comparing richest versus poorest countries is simplistic and sensitive to weighting[^harford-inequality]:

- **Unweighted by population** (Burundi 8m = China 1.3bn): inequality rose dramatically 1950–2000 then eased a little[^harford-inequality].
- **Population-weighted** (Branko Milanovic, World Bank): inequality fell gradually 1950–1990 then extremely quickly after 1990, as China and other large developing countries got less poor[^harford-inequality].

Both approaches average within countries and miss distribution inside them. Thought experiment: a US socialist redistribution from Bill Gates, Warren Buffett and hedge-fund multimillionaires to equal incomes would leave global between-country inequality unchanged because US average income is unchanged[^harford-inequality].

**Milanovic adjusted for within-country inequality:** global inequality rose until around the end of the 20th century and now appears to be falling — probably for the first time since the industrial revolution — but the fall is modest. Since population-weighted between-country inequality is falling rapidly, within-country inequality must be rising fairly rapidly to nearly cancel it[^harford-inequality].

**Normative framing:** between-country inequality is arguably more pernicious because it is non-meritocratic — being born in an average household in Zimbabwe or Eritrea largely dooms one to poverty regardless of talent unless emigration is possible, whereas a smart person born poor in the US or UK has a chance[^harford-inequality]. Within-country inequality is more socially corrosive and arguably easier to fix domestically[^harford-inequality].

## Three large developing economies — diverging paths

- **Brazil:** notoriously unequal but improving. Gini 61% (1998) to 52% by early 2010s — a quarter of the way to ultra-egalitarian Finland in ~15 years. Attributed to Lula da Silva (president 2003–2010): elected as revolutionary firebrand, governed as pragmatist courting international investment while redistributing commodity-boom rewards[^harford-inequality].
- **India:** Gini in high 30s both late 1990s and more recently — not yet very unequal because there is not enough income despite outliers such as Mukesh Ambani's billion-dollar 40-storey house in Mumbai. May follow China's trajectory as incomes rise[^harford-inequality].
- **China (still nominally communist):** Gini 48% (CIA *World Factbook*) and a more recent study at 61%. Given China is much poorer than the US, such inequality implies tremendous hardship for poorer families and contributes to leadership nervousness about social unrest despite fast growth[^harford-inequality].

### Why China became so unequal

1. **Reform strategy — "Let some people get rich first"** (*Rang yi bu fen ren xian fu qi lai*, Deng Xiaoping from 1978 after Mao). Experimental, geographically and sectorally uneven liberalization, especially coastal globalization-friendly industrial zones; successful experiments inevitably create winners and losers and creative destruction. Inland growth also high, just not as high or as long as coastal[^harford-inequality].
2. **Political elite capture:** almost one in ten of China's richest 1,000 sits in the ~3,000-member National People's Congress; their average net worth is four times that of the richest US Congress members, despite the US being far wealthier. If US plutocratic influence is a concern, China's looks worse[^harford-inequality].

## Rich-country inequality — the Anglophone divergence

After decades of falling or low stable inequality, Anglophone nations have seen rising within-country inequality for ~25 years, most strikingly in very top incomes[^harford-inequality].

Top 1% income-share growth rose sharply in US, UK and Canada and to a lesser extent New Zealand and Australia, while France, Germany, Netherlands, Switzerland and Japan show little if any increase — suggesting cultural/political forces rather than pure impersonal economics[^harford-inequality]. The US case shows the scale matters beyond symbolism because top pay now shapes averages[^harford-inequality].

## Explaining the rise — SBTC and schools

Journalist Timothy Noah's exhaustive review listed many candidates: race, gender, nuclear-family breakdown, immigration, technology boom, government policy, union decline, trade, ultra-wealthy, K-12 education[^harford-inequality]. Anglophone-specific union decline and tolerance for executive/Wall Street pay raise the prior question of why those shifts hit Anglophone countries harder[^harford-inequality].

**Skill-biased technological change (SBTC)** — Harford's most significant trend (with indifferent schools): 60 years ago need to shovel stuff; 30 years ago need to control mechanical shovel; now need to fix robot shovel when it breaks. Technology amplifies skilled workers while unskilled workers become liability[^harford-inequality].

Evidence via schooling:

- OECD PISA rankings: US and UK outside top 15 in science and reading and outside top 25 in math[^harford-inequality].
- Yet British and American universities consistently dominate world rankings[^harford-inequality].
- Harford's inference: world's two most unequal wealthy economies offer mediocre school education to the masses and outstanding university education to an elite — a structure that breeds inequality[^harford-inequality].

**Future trajectory:** inequality could escalate as technology accelerates, though future tech could in principle favor less-skilled workers[^harford-inequality]. This links to the zero-growth discussion of whether robots could make many people economically redundant, with all returns to capital owners and a need for institutional redesign such as an inalienable portfolio of shares in robot manufacturers at birth — still speculative, far from present, but illustrating that technology has historically both destroyed jobs (mechanized agriculture) and created new ones (web design)[^harford-inequality].

## Growth is not distribution-neutral

Krugman/Wells’ 1953–2018 US series provides a descriptive warning against treating real GDP per capita as the income of a typical household. Real GDP per capita and real median family income grew at nearly the same rate through about 1980; thereafter, GDP per capita continued to rise much faster as income shares at the top increased. The figure indexes end near 330 for GDP per capita and 225 for median income (1953 = 100). This comparison does not establish the cause of the divergence, but it shows why aggregate growth and median-income growth answer different questions.[^krugman-ch9-050]

The same source distinguishes inequality **within** countries from inequality **between** them: it describes rapid growth in China, India, and other emerging economies as creating a large global middle class while advanced-country working-class incomes grew slowly and top incomes rose sharply. It also reports widening recent regional gaps inside wealthy countries—illustrated by Massachusetts real GDP per capita rising by nearly 50% from 1997 to 2018 versus about 10% for Kentucky—and associates lagging regions with joblessness outside the measured labor force and elevated drug-overdose, suicide, and alcohol mortality. These are descriptive accounts, not evidence that growth itself caused those regional social outcomes.[^krugman-ch9-050]
