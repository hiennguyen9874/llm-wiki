# Business cycle, recession, and Japan's lost growth

The **business cycle** is the short-run alternation between economy-wide recessions and expansions. A recession (or contraction) is a widespread downturn in which output and employment fall; an expansion (or recovery) is an upturn in which they rise.[^krugman-ch6-037] Harford's practical distinction is that a recession has GDP shrinking for months, whereas a depression continues falling or stagnating for years.[^harford-ch4]

## Dating the cycle

A **business-cycle peak** is the turn from expansion to recession; a **trough** is the turn from recession to expansion.[^krugman-ch6-037] The common “two consecutive quarters of shrinking output” rule is a useful convention in many countries, not a universal definition. In the United States, the NBER's independent panel assesses multiple indicators and makes a judgment; this can leave the dates contested, as in the 2001 recession.[^krugman-ch6-037]

## Why recessions occur

Sometimes a clear shock is identifiable — war, revolution, or sudden collapse in the price of a major export (to be detailed in Chapter 6)[^harford-ch4]. Frequently, however, an economy sickens for no obvious reason; this happens all the time and remains incompletely understood[^harford-ch4].

## Japan as illustration

- Early 1970s: >20% real growth in three years — equivalent to an extra day's production in a five-day week[^harford-ch4].
- 1974: economy shrank despite prior brisk growth[^harford-ch4].
- 1970s–1980s average: ~4% per year[^harford-ch4].
- Past two decades to early 2010s: ~1% per year; had 4% continued, Japan would be almost twice as productive and rich today[^harford-ch4].

The case illustrates that economists do not fully understand how to prevent slowdowns or reversals; otherwise they would not happen[^harford-ch4].

## Economy-wide co-movement

Krugman and Wells show U.S. private-sector employment and industrial production both falling in downturns around the early 1990s, early 2000s, late 2007, and 2020. They emphasize that the contractions reached nearly every sector, supporting the treatment of recessions as economy-wide rather than industry-specific events.[^krugman-ch6-036]

## Recession depth and national divergence

The source contrasts the U.S. Great Recession with Brazil's later slump using real GDP indexed to 100 at each pre-recession peak: the U.S. reached roughly 96 after about 1.3 years and exceeded 100 by year four, whereas Brazil fell to about 93 around year three and remained below 95 at year four.[^krugman-ch6-037] It attributes Brazil's slump to falling export-good prices and debt-heavy consumers, and describes its severity as associated with policy tightening rather than measures to cushion demand. This is a source-specific case account, not a general causal estimate.[^krugman-ch6-037]

Business cycles have an international component but are not fully synchronized. The source's 2007–2020 comparison shows U.S. and euro-area unemployment both rising in 2008–2009; U.S. unemployment then declined from 2010, while the euro area had a second rise through late 2012 before declining after 2013. It attributes cross-country divergence to policy and economic-structure differences.[^krugman-ch6-037]

International linkage includes trade: a domestic recession reduces imports, which are another country's exports, and can therefore transmit a demand shock abroad.[^krugman-ch18-097] A floating rate can partly buffer an export-demand shock because depreciation makes domestic output cheaper to foreigners and imports costlier at home. This does not guarantee insulation: the source says financial contagion during the 2008 crisis overwhelmed this channel for many countries, and the 2020 contraction was globally synchronized largely through the pandemic itself.[^krugman-ch18-097]
