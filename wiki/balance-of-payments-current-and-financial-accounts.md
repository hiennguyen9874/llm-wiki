# Balance of payments — current and financial accounts, capital inflows, and accounting balance

A country's **balance of payments accounts** summarize its transactions with other countries over a given year.[^krugman-ch18-094] The **current account** records transactions that do not create future liabilities—goods and services, factor income, and international transfers—whereas the **financial account** records net sales of domestic assets to foreigners and therefore represents capital inflows under this sign convention.[^krugman-ch18-094] Accounting requires `CA + FA = 0`; measured accounts can fail to offset because of a statistical discrepancy, not because the underlying identity ceases to hold.[^krugman-ch18-094]

## Accounts and classifications

| Account or measure | Definition in the source | What it excludes or implies |
|---|---|---|
| Balance of payments | Summary of a country's international transactions for a year | Every payment has a counterpart use; total sources and uses balance.[^krugman-ch18-094] |
| Current account (CA) | Balance on goods and services plus net international transfer payments and factor income | These transactions do not themselves create future liabilities.[^krugman-ch18-094] |
| Balance on goods and services | Exports minus imports of goods **and services** during a period | It is the largest current-account component in the source's U.S. example.[^krugman-ch18-094] |
| Merchandise trade balance | Exports minus imports of **goods only** | It omits services; economists may use it because goods-trade data arrive sooner and are more accurate.[^krugman-ch18-094] |
| Financial account (FA) | Sales of domestic assets to foreigners minus purchases of foreign assets by domestic residents | Asset sales create claims on future interest and principal; a positive FA is a capital inflow.[^krugman-ch18-094] |

**Factor income** is mainly cross-border investment income—interest and profits—and also includes some labor income.[^krugman-ch18-094] **International transfers** are funds sent between residents of different countries, such as remittances.[^krugman-ch18-094]

## The accounting identity and sign convention

The source's convention gives:

```text
CA + FA = 0
CA = −FA
```

A current-account deficit therefore has an offsetting financial-account surplus: the country makes net payments abroad for current transactions and finances that position through net asset sales to foreigners.[^krugman-ch18-094] Conversely, a current-account surplus corresponds to a financial-account deficit. The source's simplified 2019 U.S. table reports a current-account deficit of $498 billion and a financial-account surplus of $357 billion; the $141 billion difference is labeled a statistical discrepancy due to imperfect measurement.[^krugman-ch18-094]

This financial-account sign convention is the counterpart of the **net capital inflow** convention: capital entering the country finances domestic investment. It is the inverse of the net-capital-outflow convention used elsewhere in the wiki.[^krugman-ch18-094]

## GDP, GNP, and the current account

GDP measures goods and services produced domestically, so its expenditure identity includes the goods-and-services balance (`X − IM`) but not cross-border factor income or transfers.[^krugman-ch18-094] GNP adds net international factor income (for example, earnings of domestic firms abroad less income paid to foreign asset owners) but does not include transfers; the source says no regularly calculated aggregate includes transfers.[^krugman-ch18-094] The source also cautions that international factor-income and transfer data are considered relatively unreliable, one reason GDP remains the standard measure for tracking economic movements.[^krugman-ch18-094]

## Private capital-flow model: stated limits

The source introduces the financial account as foreign saving available to finance domestic investment. Its loanable-funds preview treats all international capital flows as loans and temporarily ignores expected exchange-rate changes.[^krugman-ch18-094] These are simplifications: actual flows also include foreign stock and real-estate purchases and direct foreign investment, in which firms build or acquire productive assets abroad.[^krugman-ch18-094]

## Illustration: Swiss safe-haven inflows and intervention

The chapter-opening case attributes a large Swiss-franc appreciation after the 2008 financial crisis to foreign investors seeking Swiss safety; it reports a rise from roughly €0.6 per franc at the start of 2008 to about €0.9 by mid-2011.[^krugman-ch18-094] The source says the Swiss National Bank responded from early 2009 by selling francs for foreign currencies, adding $180 billion to reserves over two and a half years, and in September 2011 announced it would sell unlimited francs to keep the franc below €0.833 per franc.[^krugman-ch18-094] These are source-period case facts, illustrating how capital inflows can appreciate a currency and strain export competitiveness, not a current description of Swiss policy.[^krugman-ch18-094]

## Check-your-understanding classifications

The source classifies a newly produced airplane sold to China, and an already existing airplane when it is shipped to China, as goods exports in the current account. A sale of Boeing stock to Chinese investors, or an already existing airplane that remains in the United States, is a sale of a U.S. asset in the financial account.[^krugman-102] It also gives the 2008 U.S. housing collapse and recession as an illustration in which deeply depressed conditions lowered U.S. interest rates and capital inflows dried up.[^krugman-102]
