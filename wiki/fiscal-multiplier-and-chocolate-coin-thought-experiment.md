# Fiscal multiplier, demand vs supply constraints, and the chocolate-coin thought experiment

The fiscal spending multiplier — ΔGDP / Δgovernment spending — can be zero or negative when the economy is supply-constrained, and one or well above one when it is demand-constrained with sticky wages/prices, idle resources, and near-zero interest rates; a deliberately pointless project (Keynes's buried banknotes, Harford's stale chocolate coins buried and dug up in abandoned mines) isolates this macroeconomic logic from microeconomic cost-benefit analysis[^harford-ch8].

## Keynes's buried banknotes and Harford's chocolate coins

- Keynes, *General Theory*: filling old bottles with banknotes, burying them in disused coalmines filled with town rubbish, and leaving private enterprise to dig them up would reduce unemployment and could raise real income and capital wealth[^harford-ch8].
- Harford's parallel without printing money: a warehouse of stale chocolate coins (mid-1990s Christmas leftovers, intentionally useless so as not to stimulate sugar/cocoa/dairy/foil) buried by one small army and dug up by another — equally pointless by design, like Sisyphus meets Willy Wonka[^harford-ch8].
- Purpose: focus on the macroeconomic effect of spending itself, not the micro merits of sweeping streets, policing, building streets/houses, subway upgrades, rural broadband, early-years education, or green infrastructure — sensible alternatives that raise micro questions Keynes wanted to set aside[^harford-ch8]. He also used the banknote image to draw an analogy to gold/silver mining, which Harford notes need not detain us away from the gold standard[^harford-ch8].

## Spending multiplier defined

- If government spends $1m and GDP grows $1m → multiplier = 1; $0 growth → 0; $500k growth → 0.5; $200k shrink → –0.2; $1.6m growth → 1.6[^harford-ch8].
- Can be negative or >1[^harford-ch8].
- Council of Economic Advisers under Obama used multipliers as high as 1.6 for the 2009 stimulus: $1m borrowed and spent → $1.6m GDP growth[^harford-ch8].

## Supply-constrained (boom) case: multiplier ≈ 0

- Economy limited by equipment, infrastructure, workforce, skills, hours in day[^harford-ch8].
- Hiring for chocolate coins means workers not available for kitchens, waitressing, insurance sales; as government expands, private sector shrinks to accommodate it[^harford-ch8].
- Crowding-out channels: higher taxes → less private spending; higher wages from programme → private firms cannot hire and exit[^harford-ch8].
- Multi­plier arithmetic: $1m more government + ~$1m less private = no change in total output[^harford-ch8].
- Implication: spending must pass a normal cost-benefit test (cost = $1m of private sector foregone); chocolate burial fails, but road building or hospital staffing might pass even with multiplier zero[^harford-ch8].

## Demand-constrained (slump) case: multiplier ≥ 1

- Trigger slumps (Great Depression, 2008 financial crisis): sticky wages/prices leave people unemployed; savings hoarded in cookie jars/mattresses rather than funding investment; supply is not the limit, demand is[^harford-ch8].
- Government can hire without poaching from private sector; private sector does not shrink; $1m spent makes economy $1m bigger → multiplier = 1[^harford-ch8]. Programme is effectively free before asking what best use of the freebie is[^harford-ch8].

## Why multiplier can exceed one

### Consumption smoothing

- Tax-financed spending need not reduce private spending one-for-one: households may meet higher taxes by raiding savings or using credit cards and cut spending slowly over time, rather than cancelling spending immediately[^harford-ch8].
- Term: consumption smoothing — author saved signing bonus, then drew it down while unemployed rather than moving back in with father[^harford-ch8].
- Not all can smooth (no savings/overdraft/credit), but many do, so $1m extra government spending funded by $1m extra tax does not cut private spending by full $1m[^harford-ch8].

### Borrowing vs tax financing and Ricardian equivalence

- In theory financing by borrowing should not matter: taxpayers anticipate future higher taxes plus interest and save now to pay them → same effect as immediate tax[^harford-ch8].
- In practice citizens do not set aside the full amount for future taxes, so borrowing-financed spending tends to have a larger multiplier than tax-financed, balanced-budget spending[^harford-ch8].
- In reality governments borrow for stimulus rather than raise taxes at the time[^harford-ch8].

### Interest-rate channel muted in slump

- In normal times extra borrowing drives up interest rates, encouraging postponement of private spending[^harford-ch8].
- In a slump few want to borrow, so government can borrow without raising rates[^harford-ch8].

### Circulation through multiple transactions

- Each dollar can count multiple times toward GDP: miner Annie paid $100 → spends at Bill's restaurant → Bill buys painting from Charlie's gallery → Charlie pays Diana to fix roof, and so on[^harford-ch8]. This chain makes multipliers >1 arithmetically possible.

## Three catches that reduce or reverse the multiplier

1. **You must actually be in a slump with no pushback.** Otherwise spending drives up interest rates (financial-system pushback) and hits supply limits (workers, fuel, office space bid away from private uses) → result is inflation, not real output[^harford-ch8]. High multiplier assumes rates at zero and flat, masses of unemployed workers, idle machinery and empty buildings, non-inflationary expansion[^harford-ch8].

2. **Spending in the wrong place leaks abroad → negative multiplier.** Example: raise $1m in taxes, spend entirely on fine French wine for the government cellar; citizens cut spending at home, money boosts France and shrinks domestic economy[^harford-ch8]. Buy domestic products in this special recession case, even though free trade is normally better for cheap high-quality goods[^harford-ch8].

3. **One-off vs persistent vested interests (act in haste, repent at leisure).** Temporary programmes create lobbies: Union of Cocoa Entombers and Exhumers hiring lobbyists, MPs for mine constituencies demanding expansion, civil servants protecting jobs[^harford-ch8].

## Spending vs tax-cut stimulus

- Stimulus via government spending guarantees the money is spent; tax rebates may be partly saved or spent on imports, neither directly boosting domestic demand[^harford-ch8].
- Tax cuts are faster to implement (organising burial logistics takes months) and sales-tax cuts encourage spending now (buying power will be less later) more directly than income-tax cuts, though both face difficulty in reversing later[^harford-ch8].
- Harford notes the ideological filter: pro-spending voices overstate fiscal multipliers when recessions are mild, rates well above zero, economies small/open with flexible exchange rates; anti-spending voices understate them — during 2008–2012, with rates near zero, large economies, often fixed exchange rates and a deep recession, stimulus was on Harford's reading appropriate though not universally so[^harford-ch8].
