# Measuring well-being beyond GDP — objective adjustments and subjective surveys

Political leaders want a success metric beyond GDP and economists agree life is multidimensional, but no single replacement has emerged: enhancing GDP with objective factors (longevity, leisure, inequality) changes country rankings, while asking people about life satisfaction solves cross-country valuation problems at the cost of survey biases — leaving policymakers to choose the measure fit for the task[^mankiw-036].

## 1. The policy search beyond GDP

- **Leaders' dissatisfaction:** GDP is a monetary measure of all goods and services a country produces; British Prime Minister David Cameron (November announcement) planned national well-being measures including life satisfaction, following French President Nicolas Sarkozy's similar effort[^mankiw-036].
- **Statistical offices' view:** Paul Allin, director of the Measuring National Well-Being Project at the U.K. Office for National Statistics: "There is more to life than GDP, but it will be hard to come up with a single measure to replace it and we are not sure that a single measure is the answer. Maybe we live in a multidimensional world and we have to get used to handling a reasonable number of bits of information"[^mankiw-036].
- **Economists' assessment:** Brookings fellow Carol Graham after an American Economic Association session on a national success indicator: "It's like a new science. There's still a lot of work to be done"[^mankiw-036].
- Source article: Mark Whitehouse, "Nations Seek Success Beyond GDP," *The Wall Street Journal*, January 10, 2011, reprinted in Mankiw Ch.10[^mankiw-036].

## 2. GDP and happiness — the Easterlin paradox and its update

- **Original paradox:** For four decades economists puzzled that richer countries did not appear happier than poor countries. University of Pennsylvania economist Richard Easterlin (research from the 1970s) found no evidence of a link between GDP per person and reported happiness[^mankiw-036].
- **Update:** More data and different techniques by three Wharton economists — Daniel Sacks, Betsey Stevenson, and Justin Wolfers — found a given percentage increase in GDP per person tends to coincide with a similar increase in reported well-being, holding across countries and over time[^mankiw-036].
- **Limits for policy:** Even if correlated, GDP is not an ideal policy target: making everyone work 120 hours a week would radically boost GDP per capita without making people happier; removing pollution limits could boost GDP per hour worked without creating a desirable world[^mankiw-036].

## 3. Objective enhancement: consumption-equivalent welfare

One approach adds objective factors — inequality, leisure, life expectancy — to GDP[^mankiw-036].

- **Klenow-Jones calculation:** Stanford economists Peter Klenow and Charles Jones (paper presented at the AEA meeting) accounted for longer life expectancy, additional leisure time, and lower inequality; this makes living standards in France and Germany look almost the same as the U.S., which otherwise leads by a large margin[^mankiw-036].
- **Difficulty:** Klenow notes many countries have poor data on crucial factors such as life expectancy, making the calculation fraught[^mankiw-036].
- **Illustrative chart:** "Happily ever after — More wealth doesn't always translate into greater quality of life, when factors such as leisure and length of life are included" — source Klenow and Jones, Stanford, data for 2000 (image 000233 not inspected)[^mankiw-036].

## 4. What GDP leaves out — textbook omissions restated

Mankiw restates the four omissions that motivate complements (also compiled in [GDP as well-being measure and productivity mismeasurement — Silicon Valley critique](gdp-well-being-and-productivity-mismeasurement.md))[^mankiw-036]:

- **Home production and volunteer work:** Chef's meal sold at restaurant counts; same meal cooked for family does not (value added to raw ingredients omitted). Day-care child care counts; parental care does not. Volunteer work is uncounted[^mankiw-036].
- **Environmental quality:** Eliminating all environmental regulations lets firms produce without pollution costs, raising GDP but likely lowering well-being as air and water deterioration more than offsets gains[^mankiw-036].
- **Income distribution:** Two societies each with GDP $5 million and $50,000 per person look identical in per-capita terms — e.g., 100 people at $50,000 vs 10 at $500,000 and 90 at zero — but few would call them equivalent; GDP per person reports the average, hiding variation[^mankiw-036].
- **Leisure:** (from companion passage) working longer raises GDP while reducing welfare — the same logic as the 120-hour example[^mankiw-036].

Conclusion in text: GDP is a good measure of economic well-being for most — but not all — purposes; keep in mind what it includes and leaves out[^mankiw-036].

## 5. Subjective approach: asking people how they feel

For cross-country comparisons, Angus Deaton (Princeton) notes valuing the extremely different goods and services consumed in, say, the U.S. and Tajikistan in a comparable way can be impossible; asking people about their situation could be easier and no less accurate[^mankiw-036].

- **Existing surveys:** Many countries use surveys to assess performance, from U.S. consumer confidence to the Netherlands' Life Situation Index, which accounts for relationships and community involvement[^mankiw-036]. As part of its well-being effort the U.K. planned to add more subjective questions to household surveys[^mankiw-036].
- **Misleading signals — women's happiness:** Wolfers found U.S. women's subjective well-being appears lower than four decades ago despite improvements in wages, education and other objective measures; this does not imply reversing the feminist movement, but may reflect rising expectations or greater frankness[^mankiw-036].
- **Revealed vs stated preference:** People's true preferences are often revealed by what they do than what they say: surveys suggest people with children tend to be less happy than those without, yet people keep having children — nobody would advocate mass sterilization to improve overall well-being[^mankiw-036].
- **Wolfers' synthesis:** "What we care about in the world is not just happiness. If you measure just one part of what makes for a full life you're going to end up harming the other parts"[^mankiw-036].

For the time being, policymakers choose the measure most appropriate for the task at hand — not ideal, but the best economics has to offer[^mankiw-036].
