# Saving, Investment, and the Financial System — Financial Institutions, National Saving Identities, and the Market for Loanable Funds

The financial system matches savers (spend less than earn) to borrowers (spend more than earn) by moving scarce resources via financial markets (direct finance) and financial intermediaries (indirect finance)[^mankiw-ch13]. In a closed economy, national saving equals investment spending ($S=I$); in an open economy, domestic investment can also be financed by a net capital inflow.[^mankiw-ch13][^krugman-ch10-053] Private saving plus public saving funds investment, and the market for loanable funds balances saving supply against investment demand. In the long-run model, saving incentives lower rates and raise investment, investment incentives raise rates and saving, and government budget deficits reduce national saving, raise rates, and crowd out private investment.[^mankiw-ch13]

## 13-1 Financial institutions in the U.S. economy

The economy contains many institutions, but all serve the same goal: directing savers' resources to borrowers; they fall into two categories: financial markets and financial intermediaries[^mankiw-ch13].

### Financial markets — direct finance

**Bond market.** A bond is a certificate of indebtedness — an IOU specifying date of maturity (when principal is repaid) and interest rate paid until maturity; buyer lends to issuer and may hold to maturity or resell[^mankiw-ch13]. Millions of bonds issued by corporations and federal/state/local governments; prices and rates reported in *Wall Street Journal* etc.[^mankiw-ch13]

Three characteristics differentiate bonds[^mankiw-ch13]:

1. **Term** — length to maturity (months to 30 years; British government perpetuity never matures)[^mankiw-ch13]. Long-term riskier because holder must wait or sell at possibly reduced price; long-term typically pays higher interest[^mankiw-ch13].
2. **Credit risk** — probability borrower fails to pay (default, bankruptcy)[^mankiw-ch13]. High perceived default → higher required rate; U.S. government considered safe (low rate) vs shaky-corporation junk bonds (very high rate); agencies like Standard & Poor's rate AAA (safest) to D (in default)[^mankiw-ch13].
3. **Tax treatment** — most bond interest is taxable income; municipal bonds (state/local governments) are exempt from federal income tax, so typically pay lower rate than corporate or federal bonds[^mankiw-ch13].

**Stock market.** Stock represents ownership in a firm — a claim to profits; e.g., 1,000,000 shares → each share = 1/1,000,000 of business[^mankiw-ch13]. Sale of stock is equity finance; sale of bonds is debt finance[^mankiw-ch13].

Differences stocks vs bonds: stockholder is part owner, bondholder is creditor; if firm is very profitable stockholders enjoy profits while bondholders receive only interest; if firm struggles, bondholders are paid before stockholders get anything; stocks offer higher risk and potentially higher return[^mankiw-ch13].

After issuance, shares trade among stockholders on organized exchanges; corporation receives no money from secondary trades[^mankiw-ch13]. Most important U.S. exchanges are New York Stock Exchange and NASDAQ; each country typically has its own exchange[^mankiw-ch13].

Share prices are determined by supply and demand reflecting perceived future profitability; optimism raises demand and price, pessimism lowers it[^mankiw-ch13]. Stock indexes monitor overall level: Dow Jones Industrial Average (since 1896, now 30 major companies — e.g., General Electric, Microsoft, Coca-Cola, Boeing, Apple, Walmart) and Standard & Poor's 500 Index (500 major companies); closely watched as indicators of future economic conditions[^mankiw-ch13].

### Financial intermediaries — indirect finance

**Banks.** Most familiar intermediary; primary job is to take deposits from savers and make loans to borrowers — paying depositors interest and charging borrowers slightly higher interest; spread covers costs and profit[^mankiw-ch13]. Small grocer expanding business typically borrows from local bank, unlike Intel which can issue bonds/stock, because buyers prefer larger, familiar issuers[^mankiw-ch13].

Second role: facilitate transactions by providing medium of exchange — checks and debit cards against deposits; distinguishes banks from other financial institutions; stocks and bonds are stores of value but not as easy, cheap, and immediate as a check/debit for transactions; this monetary role is set aside until later chapters[^mankiw-ch13].

A deposit is both a depositor's liquid claim on the bank and the bank's liability. Banks therefore perform **liquidity transformation**: they offer funds redeemable on demand while committing most deposits to longer-term, illiquid loans. This mismatch can operate because ordinary deposits and withdrawals tend to offset, so only a fraction must be held as cash; it also creates run vulnerability when confidence fails.[^krugman-ch10-056] In the United States, the source reports that FDIC insurance guaranteed individual deposits up to $250,000, reducing incentives to run; this is a source-period institutional fact, not a statement of the current coverage limit.[^krugman-ch10-056]

**Mutual funds.** Institution that sells shares to public and uses proceeds to buy a portfolio (selection) of stocks, bonds, or both; shareholder accepts all risk/return of portfolio[^mankiw-ch13].

Primary advantage: allows small amounts to diversify — don't put all eggs in one basket; single stock/bond is risky (tied to one company), diversified portfolio spreads risk across hundreds of companies; mutual fund fee typically 0.25–2.0% of assets per year[^mankiw-ch13].

Second claimed advantage: access to professional money managers who monitor prospects, buying promising and selling less promising stocks[^mankiw-ch13]. Financial economists often skeptical: with thousands of managers watching, stock price usually reflects true value, so beating the market is hard; index funds (buying all stocks in an index) perform somewhat better on average than actively managed funds because of very infrequent trading and no manager salaries (low costs)[^mankiw-ch13]. Krugman and Wells likewise distinguish low-fee funds that hold a diversified portfolio from higher-fee funds claiming selection expertise.[^krugman-ch10-056]

**Pension funds** pool members' savings and invest across assets to provide retirement income; they function much like mutual funds, subject to particular tax and regulatory rules. **Life insurance companies** sell policies promising payment to beneficiaries on a policyholder's death, transferring that financial risk.[^krugman-ch10-056]

Other institutions beyond bond/stock markets, banks, and mutual funds include pension funds, credit unions, insurance companies, and even the local loan shark — differing in many ways but sharing the directing-savers-to-borrowers function[^mankiw-ch13].

### FYI — Key numbers for stock watchers

News services/Yahoo! Finance report for each stock[^mankiw-ch13]:

- **Price** — most important: last price (most recent trade), previous close, high/low past day and past year, change from previous close[^mankiw-ch13].
- **Dividend** — profit paid to stockholders per share over previous year (remainder is retained earnings for investment); dividend yield = dividend / price[^mankiw-ch13].
- **Earnings and P/E** — earnings = revenue minus costs (accounting profit); earnings per share = total earnings / shares outstanding; price-earnings ratio (P/E) = stock price / earnings per share over past year; historically ~15 is typical; high P/E suggests expensive relative to recent earnings (expect rise or overvalued); low P/E suggests cheap (expect fall or undervalued)[^mankiw-ch13].

Investment approaches split: some track numbers to time trades; others follow buy-and-hold well-run companies ignoring daily fluctuations[^mankiw-ch13].

### Quick question

What is a share of stock vs a bond? Bond = creditor IOU with maturity and interest; stock = ownership share with claim to profits; both raise funds for investment but differ in priority, risk/return, and finance type (debt vs equity)[^mankiw-ch13].

### Financial-system functions, asset design, and liquidity

A **financial asset** is a claim on the seller's future income, so it is an asset to its owner and a corresponding liability to its issuer. A **physical asset** is a tangible income-producing object. Buying either an existing physical asset or a financial asset is ordinarily called investing, but only spending that adds new physical capital is **investment spending** in the macroeconomic sense.[^krugman-ch10-055]

The financial system makes mutually beneficial lending and borrowing more likely by reducing three frictions: **transaction costs** (the expense of negotiating and executing a deal), financial risk, and illiquidity. An asset is **liquid** when it can be quickly converted to cash with little loss of value; it is illiquid otherwise.[^krugman-ch10-055]

- Individually negotiated **loans** can be tailored to a borrower but entail substantial information, negotiation, and documentation costs. Standardized **bonds** let large issuers raise funds from many investors with less repeated negotiation; credit ratings help investors assess default risk, and resale makes bonds generally more liquid than bespoke loans.[^krugman-ch10-055]
- **Loan-backed securities** pool individual loans and sell claims on the pool. Pooling can improve diversification and liquidity relative to an individual loan, but can also make underlying quality hard to assess; the source links mortgage-backed-security losses after housing-bubble defaults to the 2008 financial crisis.[^krugman-ch10-055]

### Financial development and financing patterns

The textbook treats domestic credit to the private sector as a rough indicator of financial intermediation, not a complete measure of development. It links Chile's post-1980s banking-safety reforms, increased deposits, and private-sector lending with its stronger growth relative to crisis-prone Argentina, but presents financial development as one probable factor among several rather than establishing causation.[^krugman-ch10-056] Financing systems also differ: in 2019, nonfinancial corporate bonds equaled 31.0% of GDP in the United States versus 10.8% in the euro area; the source attributes Europe's comparatively bank-based finance partly to household deposit preferences and firms' lower risk tolerance.[^krugman-ch10-056]

**Microcredit illustrates a different response to credit-access frictions.** Conventional lenders commonly require credit histories or collateral that poor prospective borrowers lack. Grameen Bank's model instead uses five-member borrower groups for peer approval and oversight; the groups have no legal repayment duty, but shared access to larger subsequent loans creates collective incentives. The source describes microcredit as increasing small-business investment but having limited average effects on poverty and family well-being, with possible excessive debt; it is therefore not a panacea, even if it can be beneficial alongside broader financial-access efforts.[^krugman-ch10-057]

## 13-2 Saving and investment in the national income accounts

Financial-system events are central to understanding the overall economy — saving and investment determine long-run GDP and living standards, and institutions coordinate them; analysis starts with accounting identities (equations true by definition)[^mankiw-ch13].

### Important identities

GDP ($Y$) is both total income and total expenditure on output[^mankiw-ch13]:

$$Y = C + I + G + NX$$

where $C$ = consumption, $I$ = investment, $G$ = government purchases, $NX$ = net exports[^mankiw-ch13].

This chapter assumes a closed economy — no international trade in goods/services nor borrowing/lending; imports and exports zero so $NX=0$; useful simplification applying perfectly to world economy[^mankiw-ch13]. Then:

$$Y = C + I + G$$
$$Y - C - G = I$$

Left side is total income after paying for consumption and government purchases, called national saving or saving ($S$)[^mankiw-ch13]:

$$S = I$$

With $T$ = taxes minus transfer payments (e.g., Social Security, welfare), national saving can be written two ways[^mankiw-ch13]:

$$S = Y - C - G$$
$$S = (Y - T - C) + (T - G)$$

where $Y-T-C$ = private saving (households' income left after taxes and consumption) and $T-G$ = public saving (government tax revenue left after spending)[^mankiw-ch13].

- If $T > G$: budget surplus — excess tax over spending, public saving positive[^mankiw-ch13].
- If $G > T$: budget deficit — excess spending over tax, public saving negative[^mankiw-ch13].

**Mechanism question:** What coordinates saving and investment decisions to satisfy $S=I$? The financial system — bond market, stock market, banks, mutual funds take nation's saving and direct it to investment[^mankiw-ch13].

### Meaning of saving and investment

Everyday vs macroeconomic usage differs[^mankiw-ch13]:

Krugman and Wells call only expenditure that adds **new physical capital** “investment spending”; buying an existing share, bond, or building is a financial investment, not investment spending, because it does not itself add to the capital stock.[^krugman-ch10-053]

- **Saving:** Larry earns more than spends and deposits unspent income in bank or buys stock/bond adds to national saving; he may think he is "investing" but macroeconomists call it saving[^mankiw-ch13].
- **Investment:** purchase of new capital — equipment or buildings; Moe borrowing to build a new house (the one household spending counted as investment not consumption) adds to national investment; Curly Corporation selling stock to build a new factory also adds to national investment[^mankiw-ch13].

$S=I$ holds for the economy as whole, not for every household/firm: Larry's saving can exceed his investment (excess deposited in bank); Moe's saving can be less than investment (borrows shortfall); banks/intermediaries make these individual differences possible by letting one person's saving finance another's investment[^mankiw-ch13].

**QuickQuiz:** Define private saving ($Y-T-C$), public saving ($T-G$), national saving ($Y-C-G$ = sum), and investment (new capital purchases); related by $S=I$ in closed economy[^mankiw-ch13].

## 13-3 The market for loanable funds

To explain how saving and investment are coordinated, assume a single financial market — the market for loanable funds — where all savers deposit saving and all borrowers take loans; loanable funds = income saved and lent rather than consumed, and amount investors borrow to fund new projects; one interest rate is both return to saving and cost of borrowing[^mankiw-ch13]. This ignores diversity of institutions for simplicity (as in Ch.2 model-building)[^mankiw-ch13]. Krugman and Wells make the same one-market simplification but label its interest rate nominal; Mankiw treats the rate in this long-run model as real. These are modelling conventions rather than a claim that actual loans have one common rate: both sources explicitly abstract from the many rates and financial instruments that exist in practice.[^mankiw-ch13][^krugman-ch10-053]

### Supply and demand

- **Supply** of loanable funds comes from people with extra income to save and lend — directly (buying a bond) or indirectly (deposit in bank which lends); saving is source of supply[^mankiw-ch13].
- **Demand** comes from households and firms wishing to borrow to make investments — families taking mortgages for new homes, firms borrowing for equipment/factories; investment is source of demand[^mankiw-ch13].

Interest rate is price of a loan[^mankiw-ch13]:

- High rate → borrowing more expensive → quantity demanded falls.
- High rate → saving more attractive → quantity supplied rises.

So demand slopes downward, supply slopes upward[^mankiw-ch13].

**Figure 1 equilibrium:** supply and demand balance at interest rate 5% and quantity $1,200 billion loanable funds supplied = demanded[^mankiw-ch13]. Adjustment is usual: if rate below equilibrium, shortage encourages lenders to raise rate (saving up, borrowing down); if above, surplus encourages lenders to compete by lowering rate — rate moves to balance[^mankiw-ch13].

**Real vs nominal:** nominal interest rate is monetary return/cost as usually reported; real interest rate = nominal − inflation (correcting for erosion of money value); real more accurately reflects return/cost; supply and demand depend on real rate; Figure 1 should be interpreted as determining real rate[^mankiw-ch13]. For rest of chapter, "interest rate" means real rate[^mankiw-ch13].

**Invisible-hand analogy:** like milk market price adjusts to balance supply/demand of farmers/drinkers, interest rate coordinates savers (suppliers) and investors (demanders)[^mankiw-ch13].

Analysis method for policies: three steps from Ch.4 — (1) does policy shift supply or demand? (2) which direction? (3) compare equilibria on supply-demand diagram[^mankiw-ch13].

### Expected inflation and nominal loanable-funds rates

Actual loan contracts state a **nominal** rate because future inflation is unknown. Holding expected inflation fixed, a nominal-rate movement is also a real-rate movement. But when expected inflation rises, borrowers accept a nominal rate higher by the expected increase to preserve their expected borrowing cost, while lenders require the same increase to preserve their expected return. Thus both loanable-funds curves shift upward one-for-one: equilibrium nominal interest rises by the change in expected inflation, while the expected real rate and equilibrium quantity are unchanged. This is the **Fisher effect** in the source's loanable-funds model.[^krugman-ch10-055]

### Present value, efficient allocation, and shifts

Present value explains the downward demand curve: for a one-period project paying $X$ next year, $PV=X/(1+r)$. A project is profitable when its expected payoff's present value exceeds its current cost; a lower interest rate raises PV, so more projects qualify and firms demand more funds.[^krugman-ch10-054] At the model's equilibrium, this rations funds to projects profitable at the equilibrium rate and to lenders willing to lend at or below it — an efficient allocation in the model's simplified sense.[^krugman-ch10-054]

The source distinguishes **shifts** from rate-induced movements: improved perceived business opportunities and increased government borrowing raise demand for loanable funds; greater private saving and net capital inflows raise supply.[^krugman-ch10-054] It describes a deficit as demand shifting right because government is itself a borrower. The equivalent national-saving framing used above treats the same deficit as a supply reduction; both predict a higher rate and reduced private investment.[^mankiw-ch13][^krugman-ch10-054]

**Cyclical qualification:** the crowding-out prediction is other-things-equal. When output is far below full employment, government spending may raise income and saving enough to accommodate borrowing without a higher interest rate; the source reports this as a view held by many economists of U.S. deficits during 2008–2013, not as an unconditional result.[^krugman-ch10-054]

### Short-run reconciliation with the money market

The loanable-funds model remains consistent with the short-run accounting identity that additional saving equals additional investment, but it does not lead the adjustment when output is below or above potential. In Krugman/Wells's account, a monetary expansion first lowers the money-market interest rate; higher investment then raises real GDP and saving through the multiplier. The added saving shifts loanable-funds supply right by exactly enough to match the added investment, bringing its equilibrium rate to the money-market rate. Once wages and other nominal prices adjust and output returns to potential, saving and loanable-funds supply return to their initial positions; the loanable-funds market determines the long-run rate and the money market adjusts through higher money demand.[^krugman-ch15-086]

This reconciliation uses the source's nominal-rate loanable-funds diagram. It complements, rather than changes, this page's long-run real-rate framework: expected inflation remains necessary to translate a long-run real rate into a nominal rate.[^mankiw-ch13][^krugman-ch10-053][^krugman-ch10-055]

### Policy 1: Saving incentives

Should the U.S. raise saving? Standard of living depends on ability to produce goods/services (Ten Principles Ch.1); saving is long-run determinant of productivity via capital accumulation — higher saving → more capital → faster GDP growth and higher future living standards[^mankiw-ch13]. But tax laws discourage saving: federal and many state income taxes include interest and dividend; example $1,000 saved at 9% for 30 years grows to $13,268 tax-free but only $5,743 at 33% tax (6% after-tax rate) — substantially reducing incentive[^mankiw-ch13].

Proposal: expand Individual Retirement Accounts sheltering saving from taxation — directly analyzes as market for loanable funds[^mankiw-ch13]:

1. Affects supply (households save more at any rate), not demand (borrowers unaffected)[^mankiw-ch13].
2. Supply increases, shifting right $S_1 → S_2$ (households consume smaller fraction, increase deposits/bonds)[^mankiw-ch13].
3. **Figure 2 result:** equilibrium interest rate falls 5% → 4%; quantity demanded rises $1,200bn → $1,600bn moving along demand curve; lower borrowing cost motivates households and firms to borrow more to finance greater investment[^mankiw-ch13]. Result: lower rates and greater investment.

Consensus: widely accepted analysis that greater saving → lower rates, greater investment; less consensus on what tax changes to enact and equity — skepticism that changes affect national saving much and that benefits accrue primarily to wealthy least in need[^mankiw-ch13].

### Policy 2: Investment incentives

Example: investment tax credit — tax advantage to firms building new factory or buying equipment (used from time to time)[^mankiw-ch13]:

1. Affects demand (firms invest more at any rate), not supply (households saving unaffected)[^mankiw-ch13].
2. Demand increases, shifting right $D_1 → D_2$[^mankiw-ch13].
3. **Figure 3 result:** interest rate rises 5% → 6%; quantity supplied rises $1,200bn → $1,400bn moving along supply curve as households save more in response[^mankiw-ch13]. Result: higher rates and greater saving.

### Policy 3: Government budget deficits and surpluses

Topic of perpetual political debate: budget deficit = government spending > tax revenue; financed by borrowing in bond market; accumulation of borrowing = government debt; surplus = tax > spending used to retire debt; balanced budget = equality[^mankiw-ch13].

Analyze starting from balanced budget then spending increase causing deficit via three steps[^mankiw-ch13] (Figure 4):

1. Shifts supply — national saving ($private + public$) is source of supply; change in government budget balance changes public saving and thus supply; demand unchanged (households/firms borrowing unaffected)[^mankiw-ch13].
2. Supply decreases, shifting left $S_1 → S_2$: public saving negative reduces national saving; government borrowing reduces loanable funds available to households and firms[^mankiw-ch13].
3. **Figure 4 result:** interest rate rises 5% → 6%; many demanders discouraged — fewer families buy homes, fewer firms build factories; quantity falls $1,200bn → $800bn moving along demand curve[^mankiw-ch13].

The fall in investment because of government borrowing is called crowding out[^mankiw-ch13].

**Core lesson:** when government reduces national saving by running deficit, interest rate rises and investment falls; because investment matters for long-run growth, deficits reduce growth rate[^mankiw-ch13].

**Semantic note:** does deficit affect supply or demand depends on definition of "loanable funds" — model defines it as resources available to fund private investment, so deficit reduces supply; if defined as resources available from private saving, deficit would increase demand; conclusion same: higher rate, crowding out of private borrowers[^mankiw-ch13].

**Tax-cut deficit:** same logic: tax cut reduces public saving $T-G$; private saving $Y-T-C$ might rise due to lower $T$, but if households consume more ($C$ rises) private saving rises less than public saving falls, so national saving $S=Y-C-G$ declines — again supply down, rate up, crowding out[^mankiw-ch13].

**Surplus opposite:** when tax > spending, government saves difference retiring debt; surplus contributes to national saving, supply increases, rate falls, investment stimulated, capital accumulation and growth accelerate[^mankiw-ch13].

## The history of U.S. government debt

Government indebtedness is measured as debt as a percentage of GDP — GDP as a rough proxy for the tax base — so a falling debt-to-GDP ratio indicates indebtedness shrinking relative to revenue-raising ability (living within means in some sense) and a rising ratio indicates indebtedness growing relative to ability to raise revenue, often interpreted as fiscally unsustainable at current spending/tax levels[^mankiw-ch13-history]. Figure 5 shows U.S. federal debt ranging from zero in 1836 to 107 percent of GDP in 1945[^mankiw-ch13-history].

Throughout history the primary cause of fluctuations has been war: defense spending rises substantially to pay for soldiers and equipment, taxes rise by much less, so a budget deficit and increasing debt result; when war ends, spending declines and the ratio starts declining[^mankiw-ch13-history]. Two reasons debt-financing war is considered appropriate: (1) tax smoothing — without debt, tax rates would rise sharply during wars causing substantial decline in economic efficiency; (2) intergenerational burden shifting is arguably fair because future generations who must pay off the debt also benefit from defense against foreign aggressors[^mankiw-ch13-history].

One large non-war increase began around 1980: President Reagan took office 1981 committed to smaller government and lower taxes but found cutting spending politically more difficult than cutting taxes, producing large deficits through his tenure and beyond — debt from 26% of GDP in 1980 to 48% in 1993[^mankiw-ch13-history]. Because deficits reduce national saving, investment, and long-run growth, this troubled many economists and policymakers; deficit reduction became the first major goal under President Clinton from 1993 and under the Republican Congress from 1995, and with the late-1990s boom bringing extra tax revenue the federal budget moved from deficit to surplus and the debt-to-GDP ratio declined significantly for several years[^mankiw-ch13-history].

That decline stopped under President George W. Bush as surplus turned back to deficit for three reasons: (1) major tax cuts promised in the 2000 campaign; (2) the 2001 recession which automatically decreased tax revenue and increased government spending; (3) increases in homeland-security spending after September 11, 2001 and subsequent wars in Iraq and Afghanistan[^mankiw-ch13-history].

Truly dramatic increases started in 2008 with the financial crisis and deep recession: the recession automatically increased the deficit and policy measures under Bush and Obama to combat it reduced revenue and raised spending further — from 2009 to 2012 the federal budget deficit averaged about 9% of GDP, levels not seen since World War II, lifting debt from 39% in 2008 to 70% in 2012; after 2012 recovery deficits shrank and increases in the ratio became smaller[^mankiw-ch13-history].

## Debt stocks, debt burdens, and default risk

A **deficit** is a flow—the excess of government spending over tax revenue during a stated period—whereas **debt** is a stock, the amount owed at a particular point in time. Persistent deficits normally add to debt because governments generally borrow to finance them; a surplus can retire debt.[^krugman-ch13-073] **Public debt** specifically excludes intra-governmental holdings: it is government debt held by individuals and institutions outside the government.[^krugman-ch13-073]

The debt–GDP ratio is the more useful first indicator of debt burden because GDP is a rough proxy for the taxable base. The ratio can fall even while nominal debt and annual deficits rise if GDP grows faster than debt; conversely, a small current deficit can coexist with high debt inherited from past policy.[^krugman-ch13-073] This arithmetic qualifies both a demand for an annual balanced budget and any inference from a single year's deficit.

The source distinguishes two long-run hazards of rising debt. At full employment, government borrowing can compete with private investment, raising interest rates and reducing long-run growth; this is the loanable-funds crowding-out mechanism above. Large interest obligations can also constrain future budgets. Borrowing to cover those obligations can form a **debt spiral**, eventually eroding lender confidence and risking default; default is associated with financial and economic disruption.[^krugman-ch13-073] Greece's 2009–2011 surge in borrowing costs is an illustrative source case after lenders learned its debt and deficits were larger than reported, not evidence of a universal debt threshold.[^krugman-ch13-073]

These risks do not imply that fiscal expansion is always inappropriate in a depressed economy. The source's synthesis is to allow countercyclical deficits during bad years while seeking offsetting surpluses in good years—approximately balancing over time.[^krugman-ch13-073]

## Implicit liabilities, gross debt, and debt-spiral conditions

Official public debt is incomplete as a fiscal-health measure when governments have large **implicit liabilities**: promises of future spending that are economically debt-like but omitted from usual debt statistics. In the U.S. source account, Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and Affordable Care Act premium subsidies create such commitments; a stable debt–GDP ratio can therefore give a misleadingly reassuring picture.[^krugman-ch13-074]

The source's projections are source-period estimates, not current forecasts: Social Security spending rises from 4.9% of GDP in 2019 to 6.2% in 2049, while major health-care-program spending rises from 5.3% to 9.3%. It attributes the Social Security increase partly to population aging—36 beneficiaries per 100 covered workers in 2018, projected 45 per 100 in 2050—but identifies health-care spending growing faster than the overall economy as the more serious long-run concern.[^krugman-ch13-074]

**Gross versus public debt.** At fiscal year-end 2019, the source reports $21.2 trillion total federal debt but $16.8 trillion owed to the public. It attributes much of the difference to debt held by trust funds, including a $2.8 trillion Social Security trust fund. Such intragovernmental bonds are not public debt, but the source says they correspond to future benefit commitments; this is why some economists prefer gross debt as a fiscal-health indicator. These are historical source figures, not current balances.[^krugman-ch13-074]

**Debt dynamics depend on growth as well as deficits.** Nominal debt need not be repaid or even fall for the debt–GDP ratio to fall: after World War II, the source reports U.S. public debt above 100% of GDP, while vigorous growth plus mild inflation reduced the ratio by more than half by 1962 even though nominal debt was again $248 billion. Conversely, persistent large deficits raise the ratio when debt grows faster than GDP.[^krugman-ch13-074]

A **debt spiral** is not mechanically implied by positive debt. Higher debt raises interest payments but also increases the amount whose ratio can be reduced by nominal GDP growth; on the source's formulation, spiraling requires the extra interest burden to exceed that "melting" effect. It identifies the comparison of the government interest rate with nominal GDP growth as central. Its U.S. historical averages were 4.41% interest versus 4.51% nominal growth (1990–2019), and 2.40% versus 3.97% (2010–2019); it presents Blanchard's inference about low spiral risk in advanced economies as a debate-specific interpretation, not a general guarantee.[^krugman-ch13-074]

## FYI — Financial crises: six elements (2008–2009 illustration)

Because Ch.13 introduces the financial system, Mankiw uses the 2008–2009 crisis (examined more fully later) to illustrate the anatomy of a financial crisis[^mankiw-ch13-history]:

1. **Large decline in some asset prices.** In 2008–2009, real estate after an earlier boom fell about 30% in a few years, largest U.S. decline since the 1930s[^mankiw-ch13-history].
2. **Widespread insolvencies at financial institutions.** Company insolvent when debts exceed assets; banks and other firms holding mortgages backed by that real estate faced mass defaults pushing several major institutions toward bankruptcy[^mankiw-ch13-history].
3. **Decline in confidence in financial institutions.** Not all bank deposits are insured; as insolvencies mounted, uninsured depositors withdrew; banks sold assets at fire-sale prices and cut new lending[^mankiw-ch13-history].
4. **Credit crunch.** With institutions in difficulty, prospective borrowers had trouble getting loans even for profitable projects — the system fails its normal function of directing savers' resources to borrowers with best investment opportunities[^mankiw-ch13-history].
5. **Economic downturn.** Inability to finance investment reduces overall demand for goods and services; national income falls and unemployment rises[^mankiw-ch13-history].
6. **Vicious circle.** Downturn reduces company profitability and asset values, returning to step 1 — financial-system problems and downturn reinforce each other[^mankiw-ch13-history].

Crises end when institutions eventually get back on their feet — perhaps with government help — and resume normal financial intermediation[^mankiw-ch13-history].

## 13-4 Conclusion — linking present and future

"Neither a borrower nor a lender be," Polonius advises in *Hamlet* — if followed, the chapter would be unnecessary; few economists agree[^mankiw-ch13-history]. Borrowing to start a business or buy a home and lending to earn interest for retirement are usually for good reason; the financial system's job is to coordinate this borrowing and lending[^mankiw-ch13-history].

In many ways financial markets are like other markets: the price of loanable funds — the interest rate — is governed by supply and demand and shifts can be analyzed as in other markets, and markets are usually a good way to organize activity (one of the Ten Principles from Ch.1) — balancing loanable-funds supply and demand helps allocate scarce resources efficiently[^mankiw-ch13-history].

In one way they are special: unlike most markets, financial markets link present and future — savers convert current income into future purchasing power, borrowers invest today to have additional capital to produce goods and services tomorrow; well-functioning markets matter not only for current generations but for future generations who inherit the benefits[^mankiw-ch13-history].
