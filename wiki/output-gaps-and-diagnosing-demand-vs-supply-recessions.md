# Output gaps, potential output, and diagnosing Keynesian versus classical recessions

The output gap — the difference between actual output and potential output — frames whether a recession is Keynesian (demand-driven, babysitting co-op) with usable spare capacity amenable to stimulus, or classical/supply-driven (prison camp) where stimulus would mainly raise inflation; potential output is a notional estimate shaped by ideology, so Harford presents four imperfect clues — trend growth, unemployment patterns, spare capacity surveys, and inflation — and argues that short-run demand shortfalls can bleed into long-run supply damage, making many recessions a short-run Keynesian and long-run classical problem at once[^harford-output-gaps].

## The output gap and potential output

- **Definition:** Output gap = actual output minus potential output — the gap between what is produced and what could potentially be produced with the right stimulus/confidence/conditions[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Framing:** A large gap suggests a Keynesian, babysitting co-op recession with spare capacity to draw on; a small gap suggests a supply-driven recession where stimulus is not called for[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Potential output is slippery and notional:** It depends on counterfactuals — if the central bank printed more money, employers were more confident, workers could teleport to vacancies, or wishes were horses — so it must be guessed[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Ideological bias:** Economists predisposed to Keynesian models tend to see slack; classically oriented economists start from actual = potential by definition — economies don't malfunction, so a slowdown must be a fall in potential itself[^harford-output-gaps].

## Four indicators of whether a gap exists

Harford stresses none is conclusive; they are best used together[^harford-output-gaps].

### 1. Trend growth

- Modern economies have a typical growth rate from demography and gradual adoption of technology and better work practices[^harford-output-gaps].
- A drop below trend suggests a gap that stays open until a compensating growth spurt makes up for lost time[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Illustrative arithmetic:** Trend = 3% p.a. If a year records 0% growth, first guess at gap = 3% (growth that should have happened but didn't). Next year 2% growth (1pp below trend) → cumulative gap = 4%. Two subsequent years at 5% would close the gap; stimulus should then be removed or it will produce inflation[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Assumption problem:** The smooth-trend method assumes all recessions are Keynesian; the classical view treats potential as just as uneven as actual, making the trend less helpful[^harford-output-gaps].
- **US crisis example:** Pre-crisis trend ~3% p.a. (already down from 4% in the 1990s). Growth stuttered then collapsed, cumulatively ~10 percentage points below pre-crisis trend depending on how the line is drawn; post-crisis growth ~2% p.a., so the gulf kept widening[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Interpretation split:** A hard-line classical would say potential collapsed by 10% as well; Harford argues a 10% fall in a handful of years is implausibly large for factories/offices to become not just unused but unusable — so a very large fall implies at least some demand component amenable to stimulus, while a smaller, more gradual drift behind trend more plausibly signals a trend shift[^harford-output-gaps].

### 2. Unemployment

- Lots of unemployed people is prima facie evidence of slack and a case for stimulus[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Classical counter:** The unemployed are for now unemployable — a shock has devalued old skills; retraining, moving, and finding new industries takes time; cutting rates/taxes or burying chocolate coins just postpones adjustment and creates 1970s-style inflation with sluggish growth[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Discriminating test:** Structural misalignment should show sectoral divergence — some sectors booming with full employment and rising wages desperate to recruit, others stagnating[^harford-output-gaps].
- **US evidence 2007–:** Unemployment rose sharply in most sectors and for most kinds of workers, which Paul Krugman — described as an evangelist for the Keynesian view — cites as evidence for a general demand problem rather than a sideways structural shock[^harford-output-gaps].

### 3. Spare capacity / capacity utilization surveys

- Ask firms directly whether they have spare capacity[^harford-output-gaps].
- **US:** Federal Reserve capacity utilization usually a little above 80%; fell to 79% in 1991, 74% in 2002, but as low as 67% in 2009 — a large amount of spare capacity pointing to a Keynesian recession[^harford-output-gaps].
- **UK contrast:** Worker productivity slumped (supply-shock signature) and Bank of England surveys found firms reporting not much spare capacity — what holds for one economy at one moment does not hold everywhere[^harford-output-gaps].

### 4. Inflation

- Persistently low inflation over an extended period suggests weak demand; buoyant inflation despite slow growth suggests supply-side causes[^harford-output-gaps].
- Caveat: Inflation is also moved by other factors such as oil prices, so no guarantee[^harford-output-gaps].

## Why diagnosis is intrinsically hard

- **Scissors analogy:** Alfred Marshall said trying to tell whether supply or demand is doing the work is like deciding which blade of scissors cuts the paper — you cannot observe one without the other[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Blurring boundary:** Unlike scissor blades, demand and supply boundaries blur: a demand contraction can itself degrade supply[^harford-output-gaps].

## When demand bleeds into supply (hysteresis)

- **Babysitting co-op illustration:** Couples frustrated by inability to get a sitter resign and install a home cinema, no longer available to babysit — a demand recession eventually damages supply potential[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Complex-economy channels:** Skilled workers emigrate; bankruptcies destroy irreplaceable institutional knowledge; machinery rusts; buildings decay; long-term unemployed lose work ethic, skills, or employer confidence[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Bankruptcy mechanics:** Workers unemployed, premises empty, suppliers with slack needing new customers — in principle instantly redeployable, in reality requiring time and large reinvestment (retraining, bulldozing and rebuilding premises) — so short-run lack of demand creates medium- to long-run dearth of supply[^harford-output-gaps].

## Short run versus long run — the false dilemma

- Harford rejects the either/or framing. Most recessions have a Keynesian element in the short run that should be addressed with stimulus (usually monetary, from the central bank, rather than extra government spending), while structural reforms to raise productive capacity are always worth considering for the long run — often no contradiction between the two, and the same policy can do both (e.g., well-chosen infrastructure: railways, road repairs, faster broadband — employing the otherwise unemployed now and expanding capacity later)[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Bad-target example:** Paying people to bury and exhume stale chocolate coins expands no capacity[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Fiscal risks:** Stimulus is hard to scale back in good times, letting debt accumulate; US and UK entered the Great Recession already indebted and structurally borrowing in good times — not what Keynesian policy demands[^harford-output-gaps].
- **David Brooks case (New York Times, May 2012, “The Structural Revolution”):** Brooks divided cyclicalists (his Keynesians) versus structuralists (his classicals), worrying cyclicalists who see government spending as the main growth determinant paper over structural problems with debt. Harford calls this nearly right but wrong: it is not either/or but short run and long run — long-run structural reform matters, but short-run stimulus is usually also warranted[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Timing trap for structural reform:** Some reforms (e.g., making it easier to sack workers — good long-run: less hiring nervousness, more chances for young unproven workers) backfire if done mid-recession — enabling more sackings depresses demand further and delays the eventual job-growth upside until after the recession[^harford-output-gaps].

## Policy timing and political inversion

- **Normative prescription:** Run the economy like a tough right-winger in a boom — trim spending, pay off debt, reduce unnecessary regulation to make markets function better — and like a bleeding-heart left-winger in a recession — keep spending, run up debt, launch big infrastructure projects[^harford-output-gaps].
- **Observed inversion:** Tendency to do the opposite: elect left-wing governments in booms to improve labor protection, launch big public projects, and run up debt; then elect right-wing governments in recessions to slash deficits, scrap investment, and bonfire labor protections, worsening the downturn[^harford-output-gaps].

## Contradictions

- **Interpretation of a given output shortfall:** Keynesian-leaning reading (e.g., Krugman on post-2007 US sectoral unemployment and large US capacity-utilization drop to 67%) sees slack and scope for stimulus vs. classical reading (potential fell with actual; unemployed are structurally unemployable; stimulus only produces inflation as in the 1970s) — Harford presents both without resolving, noting ideology and measurement limits, and treats very large gaps as more plausibly demand-driven[^harford-output-gaps].
- **US vs UK post-crisis signals:** US data (deep utilization fall, broad unemployment rise, low inflation) pointed toward demand gap; contemporaneous UK data (productivity slump, Bank of England surveys showing little spare capacity) pointed more toward supply — illustrating that indicators do not generalize across economies[^harford-output-gaps].
