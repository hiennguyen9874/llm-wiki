# Government debt dynamics, fiscal sustainability, and debt crises

A deficit is the annual flow of new borrowing; debt is the stock accumulated from past deficits. For real debt and the debt-to-GDP ratio, sustainability depends not on the deficit alone but on the primary balance, the existing debt ratio, and the difference between the real interest rate and output growth. This arithmetic supports countercyclical deficits and tax smoothing in exceptional spending episodes, but high debt can make adverse expectations self-fulfilling and, at an extreme, lead to default or inflationary money finance.[^blanchard-090][^blanchard-091][^blanchard-093][^blanchard-094]

## Budget constraint and inflation adjustment

With $B_{t-1}$ real debt at the start of year $t$, real interest rate $r$, government purchases $G_t$, and net taxes $T_t$ (taxes minus transfers), the deficit and debt change are:

$$\mathrm{deficit}_t=rB_{t-1}+G_t-T_t=B_t-B_{t-1}$$

$G_t-T_t$ is the **primary deficit**; its negative is the primary surplus. A tax cut or spending increase therefore increases debt unless an offsetting future primary surplus is run.[^blanchard-090][^blanchard-091]

The usual cash-flow measure, $iB+G-T$, uses nominal interest payments. It overstates the change in real debt by inflation times the outstanding nominal debt, $\pi B$; the inflation-adjusted measure is $(i-\pi)B+G-T=rB+G-T$. It is especially important where either inflation or debt is high. The source's 1969–2019 U.S. chart shows the official deficit generally above the inflation-adjusted measure, while preserving the broad changes in fiscal position.[^blanchard-091]

## Debt-ratio arithmetic

For debt ratio $b=B/Y$, output growth $g$, and primary deficit share $pd=(G-T)/Y$, the source gives the approximation:

$$\Delta b \approx (r-g)b_{t-1}+pd$$

Thus a larger initial debt ratio, higher real rates, lower growth, or a larger primary deficit makes the ratio rise faster. A positive real interest rate can increase the *level* of debt even with a zero primary deficit, whereas the debt ratio can fall if growth exceeds the real interest rate.[^blanchard-091]

Postwar debt reduction illustrates the distinction. Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom all reduced very high ratios over 13–30 years; the source attributes most of the fall to high growth and negative real interest rates, with primary surpluses contributing less. For the United Kingdom, the reported ratio declined from 270% in 1946 to 47% in 1975; its average 2.1%-of-GDP primary surplus cumulated to less than one-third of that 223-point decline.[^blanchard-092]

## Private saving, the cycle, and war finance

**Ricardian equivalence** is the benchmark proposition that a tax cut financed by debt has no effect on consumption: households save the entire cut because the present value of future taxes rises equally. Public dissaving is then exactly offset by private saving, leaving total saving, investment, and capital accumulation unchanged. The source treats expectations of future taxes as an important dampener but identifies finite horizons, uncertain timing, and limited foresight as reasons the exact equivalence is unlikely to hold. Its practical conclusion is that deficits generally raise demand and output in the short run but reduce capital accumulation and output in the long run.[^blanchard-093]

The **cyclically adjusted deficit** estimates the deficit under existing tax and spending rules if output were at potential. It separates automatic recession-driven revenue losses and transfer changes from a persistent fiscal imbalance. This supports a countercyclical rule of deficits in recessions offset by surpluses in booms, not a requirement that the adjusted balance always equal zero; estimating potential output makes the measure uncertain.[^blanchard-093]

Wars and other temporary spending spikes provide a separate rationale for debt finance even at full employment. Compared with immediate tax finance, deficits preserve more contemporaneous consumption but crowd out more investment, passing some burden to future generations through a lower capital stock. Spreading taxes over time can also reduce the distortion from sharply varying tax rates (**tax smoothing**). In the source's U.S. World War II account, government purchases rose from 15% to 45% of GDP between 1939 and 1944, while the federal deficit reached 22% and debt reached 110% of GDP; these are historical source-period data, not current fiscal measures.[^blanchard-093][^blanchard-094]

## High debt and adverse feedback

High debt increases exposure to a self-fulfilling debt crisis. If creditors demand a default-risk premium, the higher $r$ raises the primary surplus required to stabilize $b$; fiscal cuts needed to supply it can depress growth, raising $r-g$ further and validating the initial concern. In the source's illustration, at debt equal to 100% of GDP and growth of 2%, moving the real rate from 3% to 8% raises the stabilizing primary surplus from 1% to 6% of GDP.[^blanchard-094]

The source presents Italian and Spanish two-year bond spreads over German bonds in 2012 as an illustration: spreads rose sharply through July, then declined after the ECB's July 26 commitment to prevent euro-area breakup. This supports the mechanism as a historical textbook account; it does not establish a universal debt threshold or policy guarantee.[^blanchard-094]

If stabilization fails, a government may restructure or default, imposing a creditor haircut. That can reduce future fiscal adjustment but can damage domestic pension funds and banks or restrict future external borrowing. Alternatively, it may compel the central bank to buy government bonds with newly created money (**money finance**, or fiscal dominance).[^blanchard-094]

## Seigniorage limit

Real revenue from money creation is:

$$\mathrm{seigniorage}=\frac{\Delta H}{P}=\frac{\Delta H}{H}\frac{H}{P}$$

High money growth tends to raise inflation and reduce desired real money balances, shrinking the tax base. Financing a fixed real deficit then requires still faster money growth, potentially producing hyperinflation. Stabilization requires eliminating the fiscal need for money finance, not merely changing the currency unit.[^blanchard-094]
