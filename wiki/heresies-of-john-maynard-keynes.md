Heilbroner's chapter "The Heresies of John Maynard Keynes" opens with the 1929 stock-market mirage and the Great Depression as a lived refutation of the classical saving-investment-interest mechanism, then pairs that national collapse with a biographical portrait of Keynes as a many-sided Cambridge figure whose *Treatise on Money* (1930) diagnosed the savings-investment seesaw but could not explain persistence of slump, setting the stage for his self-declared revolutionary *General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) and its unsettling diagnosis, New Deal cure, wartime extension, Bretton Woods aftermath and contested legacy[^heilbroner-016][^heilbroner-017].

# Synthesis

America's late-1920s prosperity masked fragile inequality and leverage that collapsed in October 1929; incomes then fell by more than half and 14 million were jobless, a condition classical economics called impossible. Keynes — mathematician, speculator, Bloomsbury insider, Treasury adviser and author of *Indian Currency and Finance* (1913), *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919) and the *Treatise on Probability* — used the *Treatise on Money* to formalize the divorce of saving (Thrift) from investment (Enterprise) and the income-flow circuit, but its automatic interest-rate safety switch failed in 1929–33. The *General Theory* replaced the seesaw with an elevator that could stand still on any floor: when incomes contracted, savings dried to a trickle rather than flooding cheap money, so the economy could rest in equilibrium with mass unemployment; prosperity thus hinged on inherently undependable investment, remediable only by government spending — a logic tested by New Deal pump-priming, inverted for wartime inflation, institutionalized at Bretton Woods, and later contested as Keynesianism withered after the 1960s[^heilbroner-016][^heilbroner-017].

# The lure before the crash — Veblen to Raskob

- Veblen, most disenchanted observer, plunges in an oil stock on a friend's tip, makes a little, then sees it cited in oil scandals and go worthless — tiny chink revealing the dazzling lure that blinded America[^heilbroner-016].
- Late-1920s signs: 45 million employed, $77 billion in wages, rents, profits and interest. Hoover: "We shall soon with the help of God be within sight of the day when poverty will be banished from the nation" — shortsighted but resting on real advance in average living standards[^heilbroner-016].
- New self-image: John J. Raskob, Democratic chairman, *Ladies' Home Journal*, "Everybody Ought to Be Rich" — save $15/week, invest at 6% reinvested dividends = $80,000 plus $400/month after 20 years[^heilbroner-016].
- More alluring path spending dividends and riding price trend alone: $780 saved 1921 → $1,092 in 1922 → $4,800 in 1925 → $6,900 in 1926 → $8,800 in 1927 → $16,000 in 1928 → over $21,000 by May 1929 (×10 in 1980s values) after nearly half a generation of uninterrupted rise — barber to banker alike gambled and won[^heilbroner-016].

# October 1929 and the revelation of rot

- Last week October 1929 Niagara burst: brokers wept, tore collars, fortunes melted as spun sugar, no buyers. Jokes: Goldman Sachs share came with complimentary revolver; hotel clerk "For sleeping or jumping?"[^heilbroner-016].
- Two months wiped two years' gains, $40 billion vanished. By three years investor's $21,000 paper down 80%; $7,000 original savings worth barely $4,000[^heilbroner-016].
- Honeycomb of loans bore just so much strain. Raskob arithmetic never asked how to save $15 from average $30 pay envelope[^heilbroner-016].
- Uneven flow: 24,000 families at apex received three times the income of 6 million families at bottom; average top income 630× average bottom[^heilbroner-016].
- Hidden weaknesses: 2 million unemployed before crash, banks failing two a day for *six years*, households mortgaged and installment-bought to neck, ~300 million shares bought on margin (borrowed money)[^heilbroner-016].
- Assurances of health daily; Irving Fisher of Yale: "permanently high plateau" — stocks fell off brink one week to the day later[^heilbroner-016].

# Great Depression — income collapse and joblessness

- Spot illustrations: Muncie, Indiana ("Middletown") — every fourth factory worker jobless by end 1930; Chicago — majority of working women <25 cents/hour, quarter <10 cents; New York Bowery — 2,000 jobless in bread lines daily; residential construction −95%; 9 million savings accounts lost; 85,000 businesses failed; salaries −40%, dividends −56%, wages −60%[^heilbroner-016].
- National income: $87 billion in 1930 → $75 billion 1930 → $59 billion 1931 → $42 billion 1932 → $39 billion 1933; over half 1929 prosperity vanished, living standard back 20 years; 14 million unemployed on corners and in Hoovervilles[^heilbroner-016].
- Most depressing: no end, no turning point — 1930 "Happy Days Are Here Again," 1931 "I've Got Five Dollars," 1932 "Brother, Can You Spare a Dime?" matched stepwise income fall[^heilbroner-016].
- Unemployment as embolism: economists invoked Adam Smith, offered no diagnosis or remedy — this kind of unemployment "simply not listed among the possible ills of the system; it was absurd, unreasonable, and therefore impossible. But it was there"[^heilbroner-016].

# Keynes — the man who tackled it

Heilbroner stresses paradox: solver was no Left-Winger but an almost-dilettante, buoyant, consummately successful polymath — architect of *Capitalism Viable* as Marx (born same year Keynes born, 1883, year Marx died) was draftsman of *Capitalism Doomed*[^heilbroner-016].

## Many-sidedness

- Recondite *Treatise on Probability* mathematical probability, praised by Bertrand Russell as "impossible to praise too highly"[^heilbroner-016].
- Fortune of £500,000 via international currencies and commodities, managing it half an hour a day abed[^heilbroner-016].
- Cambridge don, yet married Diaghilev ballerina Lydia Lopokova; darling of Bloomsbury and chairman of a life insurance company; diplomat knowing mistresses/neuroses/financial prejudices of European politicians; collector of modern art before fashionable and finest private Newton writings; ran a theater and became Director of the Bank of England; knew Roosevelt and Churchill and Shaw and Picasso; bridge as speculator, solitaire as statistician; one regret: wished he had drunk more champagne[^heilbroner-016].
- Name pronounced to rhyme with "rains," traced to William de Cahagnes 1066; traditionalist liking greatness in families; father John Neville Keynes, well-known economist[^heilbroner-016].

## Formation

- Boyhood Victorian Old School brilliant: at 4½ puzzling interest, at 6 how brain worked, at 7 "thoroughly delightful companion" to father; Mr. Goodchild's preparatory school — had a "slave" trailing books for homework help and a "commercial treaty" with a disliked boy: one book a week from library for never approaching within 15 yards[^heilbroner-016].
- Eton at 14 on scholarship: superlative marks, prizes, lavender waistcoat, champagne taste, tall stooped mustache, rowing, debater; Eton enthusiast yet discernment at 17 on headmaster's Boer War speech: "It was the usual stuff. Ought to show our thankfulness; remember dignity of school; if anything done must be of best; as always before"[^heilbroner-016].
- King's College Cambridge triumph: Alfred Marshall begged full-time economics, Pigou weekly breakfast, Secretary of the Union (automatic presidency), sought by Leonard Woolf and Lytton Strachey (lover), nucleus of Bloomsbury, climbed mountains (Strachey: "multitudes of imbecile mountains"), argued small-hours, phenomenon[^heilbroner-016].
- Ambition: "I want to manage a railway or organize a Trust or at least swindle the investing public" to Strachey — found principles easy and fascinating[^heilbroner-016].
- Civil Service examinations, posed indifference, predicted top ten, placed second — lowest mark economics: "I evidently knew more about Economy than my examiners"[^heilbroner-016].
- India Office 1907, hated, one pedigreed bull shipped to Bombay, learned an ill-considered remark earned being "snubbed"; resigned after two years back to Cambridge; nevertheless wrote *Indian Currency and Finance* (1913) — admitted small masterpiece — and at 29 sat on Royal Commission on Indian currency[^heilbroner-016].
- Cambridge: editor of *Economic Journal* 33 years, marking esteem[^heilbroner-016].
- Bloomsbury ~20–30 people — Woolfs, E. M. Forster, Clive Bell, Roger Fry, Strachey — could make poet with smile or drop with frown; "really" in dozen intonations. Dreadnought Hoax — Virginia Stephen Woolf as Emperor of Abyssinia boarded guarded battleship. Keynes central — adviser, counsellor, referee — "No, no, you're absolutely wrong about that..." to Walton, Ashton etc. Nickname Pozzo after multifarious Corsican diplomat-schemer[^heilbroner-016].

## War and peace

- Treasury overseas finances WWI: need Spanish pesetas, raked small sum, Keynes reports then says "Oh no! ... I've sold them all; I'm going to break the market." And he did[^heilbroner-016].
- Key figure; Roy Harrod: men of ripe judgment said he contributed more to winning war than any other in civil life; meanwhile bought $100k of Corot, Delacroix, Forain, Gauguin, Ingres, Manet for National Gallery (plus Cezanne for self) while Big Bertha shelled Paris depressing prices; ballet — Lydia Lopokova in "The Good-Humored Ladies" with Sitwells; her English — "legs bitten by barristers"[^heilbroner-016].
- Paris Peace Conference 1919 Deputy for Chancellor on Supreme Economic Council with full decision power and Treasury representative, but second echelon, watched Wilson outmaneuvered by Clemenceau, humane ambition replaced by vindictive one; letter to mother: "utterly worn out, partly by depression at evil around me... Peace is outrageous and impossible... must bring misfortune"; dragged from sickbed to protest "murder of Vienna"[^heilbroner-016].
- Resigned in despair, wrote *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* in three days before signing, December 1919 — brilliantly written and crushing; portraits: Clemenceau "He had only one illusion—France, and one disillusion—mankind, including his own colleagues not least," Wilson "...like Odysseus, he looked wiser when seated"; core critique Council of Four ignored starving disintegrating Europe, preoccupied with crushing enemy, doing deal for a week, doing what is just and right; reparation settled as theology/politics/electoral chicane, not economic future[^heilbroner-016].
- Warning: Europeans depressed to actual starvation — already Russia, approximately Austria — "Men will not always die quietly... drives other temperaments to nervous instability of hysteria and mad despair... may overturn remnants of organization, and submerge civilization itself" — immense success, first to see unworkability and urge revision, confirmed when Dawes Plan 1924 began undoing 1919 impasse; Keynes known for extraordinary foresight[^heilbroner-016].

## Business and private life after fame

- Began speculating international markets few thousand pounds, near total loss, loan from unknown banker impressed by war work, recouped to $2 million (≈£500k) fortune; disdained inside information — Wall Street traders could make fortunes if they disregarded it — relying on balance-sheet scrutiny, encyclopedic finance, personality intuition, flair; abed study, phone orders, day free for theory — would have gotten along famously with Ricardo[^heilbroner-016].
- Bursar of King's: £30,000 → £380,000; also investment trust and life insurance company finances[^heilbroner-016].
- Wrote for *Manchester Guardian*, Cambridge classes spiced with commodity-mart personalities, bought pictures/books, tumultuous love life with Strachey, Duncan Grant etc, then married Lydia — she gave up profession but thumps from above still practicing; to friends' surprise/relief filled Cambridge wife role perfectly; gawky triangular face straight nose clipped mustache full mobile lips disappointing chin, arching brows eyes grave/icy/sparkling or "soft as bees' bottoms in blue flowers," liked sitting mandarin hands tucked in opposing sleeves, obsessed with hands — casts of his and wife's, collection plan, summing Roosevelt: "Firm and fairly strong, but not clever or with finesse... oddly familiar... Sir Edward Grey. A more solid and Americanized Sir Edward Grey" — ten minutes searching memory while discussing silver/balanced budgets/public works. Roosevelt to Felix Frankfurter: "I had a grand talk with K. and liked him immensely" — unaware of hand-judgment[^heilbroner-016].

## Writings to 1930

- By 1935 brilliance established: *Indian Currency*, *Economic Consequences*, *Treatise on Probability*; anecdote: Max Planck told Keynes he once considered economics but found it too hard; Russell told friend he considered it but found it too easy — Keynes relished contrast[^heilbroner-016].
- *Tract on Monetary Reform* (1923): inveighed against gold fetishism and abdication of conscious currency control to impersonal gold standard; gave aphorism for talking of long-run axiom: "In the long run we are all dead"[^heilbroner-016].
- *Treatise on Money* (1930): long, difficult, uneven, sometimes brilliant/baffling attempt to explain uneven economy — wavelike expansions and contractions "economic breathing" — bad 1801 good 1802 bad 1808 etc over century in England and America[^heilbroner-016].

# History of cycle theory — from nerves to sunspots to savings

- Early view: mass nervous disorder — 1867 "variations of despondency, hopefulness, excitement, disappointment, and panic" on Wall Street/Lombard Street — describes mood but not cause[^heilbroner-016].
- Outside-economy answer: W. Stanley Jevons sunspots — boom-to-boom 10.46 years matched sunspots 10.45 (Herschel 1801 discovery) → weather → rainfall → crops → business; then recalculated sunspots to 11 years, correspondence broke, theory returned to earth[^heilbroner-016].
- Area bumblingly pointed to by Malthus a century earlier — saving and "general glut": Ricardo scoffed, Mill pooh-poohed, Smith "What is prudence in... private family can scarce be folly in that of a great nation" —视为 dangerous underworld nonsense impugning thrift/immoral[^heilbroner-016].
- Contemporaries not moralizing but observing real world: early 1800s savers = investors — landlords/capitalists saving = accumulation (amassing and immediately employing in tools/buildings/land) — saving was investment[^heilbroner-016].
- Mid-19th century structure changed: improved distribution opened saving to more, business institutionalized sought capital in anonymous pocketbooks — saving and investing divorced into separate operations by separate groups — Malthus right for unforeseen reasons[^heilbroner-016].

# National income flow and the savings-investment fracture

- Prosperity measured not by gold (poverty-stricken Africa gold-rich) nor physical assets (buildings/mines/factories did not evaporate 1932) but incomes earned; total individual = national income; central characteristic is *flow* hand-to-hand — purchase transfers income, every penny derives from someone else's spending (store, corporation bonds/stock)[^heilbroner-016].
- Metaphor: taking in each other's wash — economy constantly revitalized by handing money around[^heilbroner-016].
- Mostly naturally assured: bulk spent on consumption goods with consistent regularity for eating/clothing/enjoyment[^heilbroner-016].
- Break point: savings — if tucked in mattresses/cash hoarded, return less than received, cumulative fall in incomes at each turn — depression if widespread/continued[^heilbroner-016].
- Normally not frozen: put into stocks/bonds/banks → usable by expanding business via direct purchase or bank loans → returns as wages/salaries/profits[^heilbroner-016].
- Vital non-automatic: business needs savings only if *expanding*; regular receipts cover everyday expenses; insufficient capital to build factory without savings; impetus to invest wanes when outlook poor — gluts, international alarm, nervousness about inflation — why expand with trepidation?[^heilbroner-016].
- Possibility: if savings not invested by expanding firms, incomes *must* decline — same spiral as hoarding[^heilbroner-016].
- Strangeness: no greedy landlords or avaricious capitalists — only virtuous thrifty citizens and virtuous prudent businessmen deciding on machine/plant risk; outcome determines boom/slump; if business invests less than community saves, depression adjusts[^heilbroner-016].
- Price of economic freedom: Soviet Russia or Pharaohs' Egypt — savings and investment determined from above via edict/total control for pyramids/power plants; capitalist freedom leaves both decisions to free actors — therefore they can be out of joint: too little investment to absorb savings or too little savings to support investment; bust and boom are consequences to be faced[^heilbroner-016].

# *Treatise* — Thrift and Enterprise seesaw

- *Treatise on Money* sparkling exposition of seesaw: not original — long list already pointed — but Keynes gave luster: "It has been usual to think of the accumulated wealth of the world as having been painfully built up out of that voluntary abstinence... which we call Thrift. But it should be obvious that mere abstinence is not enough by itself to build cities or drain fens.... It is Enterprise which builds and improves the world's possessions.... If Enterprise is afoot, wealth accumulates whatever may be happening to Thrift; and if Enterprise is asleep, wealth decays whatever Thrift may be doing."[^heilbroner-016].
- Yet no sooner written than figuratively torn up: failed at one central point — did not explain how economy could *remain* in prolonged depression; seesaw weighted by surplus savings should in short order right itself opposite way[^heilbroner-016].
- Reason expectation: savings and investment tied in money market where business "buys" savings; price is interest rate; flood of savings at slump bottom should cheapen — glut of shoes lowers price — and falling 10%→5% makes new factory look profitable, increasing incentive[^heilbroner-016].
- Therefore automatic safety switch seemed built in: abundant savings → cheaper borrowing → encouraged investment → contraction rebounds[^heilbroner-016].
- What failed in Great Depression: interest rate declined but nothing happened; old nostrums — pinch of local relief and large dose of hopeful waiting — patient failed to improve; neat formulation missing something else holding economy back[^heilbroner-016].

# Toward the *General Theory*

- Brewing for some time; letter to George Bernard Shaw 1935 (just reread Marx and Engels at Shaw's suggestion, little liking): "To understand *my* state of mind... you have to know that I believe myself to be writing a book on economic theory which will largely revolutionize—not, I suppose at once, but in the course of the next ten years—the way the world thinks about economic problems.... I can't expect you or anyone else to believe this at the present stage. But for myself I don't merely hope what I say—in my own mind, I'm quite sure." — as usual quite right[^heilbroner-016].
- To be bombshell, but forbidding: title *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, interior page 25 "Let Z be the aggregate supply price of the output from employing N men, the relationship between Z and N being written Z = φ (N), which can be called the Aggregate Supply Function." Deserts of algebra and abstract analysis between oases like comparing stock picking and beauty contests; little panorama of social action layman expected from Smith/Mill/Marx[^heilbroner-016].
- Yet revolutionary: stood economics on head, very much as *Wealth of Nations* and *Capital* had done[^heilbroner-017].

# The *General Theory* core — no automatic safety mechanism

- Startling conclusion: no automatic safety mechanism; economy not seesaw righting itself but elevator that could go up or down or stand still on any floor, quite capable of standing still on ground floor — depression might not cure itself, stagnant indefinitely like ship becalmed[^heilbroner-017].
- Flaw in flood-of-savings argument: at trough savings are squeezed out — community cannot save as much hard-up as prosperous — not glut but drying-up: American private saving $3.7 billion 1929 → *nothing* 1932–33, even drawing down old savings; corporations $2.6 billion after taxes/dividends at boom top → losing nearly $6 billion three years later — saving a luxury that cannot withstand hard times[^heilbroner-017].
- Consequence: no surplus → no pressure on interest to induce borrowing; no borrowing/investment → no impetus for expansion; economy remains in "equilibrium" despite unemployed men and underutilized plant — paralysis just when dynamism needed[^heilbroner-017].
- Paradox of poverty amidst plenty, idle men and idle machines: contradiction purely moral; economy satisfies *demand* (pocketbook), not *wants* (dreams); unemployed are economic zeros, might as well be on moon for market influence[^heilbroner-017].
- Once investment declined and economy shrank, social misery appeared but not *effective* misery — nation's conscience not substitute for investment; savings declined along with investment, flow turned over evenly, smaller but unaffected[^heilbroner-017].
- Tragedy without villain: no blame on thrift (private virtue) or on businessmen (would happily invest if reasonable chance); difficulty technical/mechanical fault, price is unemployment; investment willingness cannot go on indefinitely[^heilbroner-017].

# Investment as undependable drive wheel

- Industry limited by size of market it caters to at any time: railroads 1860s built only what could be used then, not 1960s trackage to nonexistent cities; Ford could have built 1950 River Rouge plant in 1910 but would have gone bankrupt — roads, gas stations, *demand* lacking; late 1990s business spending ~$1 trillion for new equipment but not $2 trillion — day might come but not yet[^heilbroner-017].
- Typical pattern: eagerness for new opportunity → caution lest enthusiasm overbuild → inactivity when market satisfied for time being[^heilbroner-017].
- If each separate investment project immediately replaced by another, no slump; but mere vastness of wants does not mean any investment will pay — economy littered with rash overexpansions; most investment needs more than sanguine expectations — new invention, better way, intriguing product — opportunities not always there; hence if no ready substitute for each casualty, contraction begins[^heilbroner-017].
- Keynes on intrinsic vulnerability: "Ancient Egypt was doubly fortunate and doubtless owed to this its fabled wealth, in that it possessed *two* activities, namely pyramid-building and the search for the precious metals, the fruits of which, since they could not serve the needs of man by being consumed, did not stale with abundance. The Middle Ages built cathedrals and sang dirges. Two pyramids, two masses for the dead are twice as good as one; but not so two railways from London to York."[^heilbroner-017].

# Three-point diagnosis

1. Economy in depression could stay there — nothing inherent to pull it out; "equilibrium" with unemployment, even massive[^heilbroner-017].
2. Prosperity depended on investment — if capital spending fell, contraction spiral; only if it rose, expansion spiral[^heilbroner-017].
3. Investment was undependable — uncertainty at core of capitalism, constantly threatened with satiety, satiety spelled decline[^heilbroner-017].

Not book of doom — held promise and cure[^heilbroner-017].

# The cure — government spending and pump-priming

- Cure began before prescription: Hundred Days flood of social legislation meant to improve tone/morale, but tonic was deliberate government spending to stimulate economy[^heilbroner-017].
- Began as makeshift work-relief — riots in Dearborn, ragged Washington march, families in incinerator buildings, scrabbling in garbage trucks — relief essential under Hoover, then leaf-raking turned constructive under Roosevelt: roads, dams, auditoriums, airfields, harbors, housing[^heilbroner-017].
- Keynes Washington 1934 (notes on Roosevelt's hands — "Firm and fairly strong, but not clever...") urged extension; private investment $15 billion 1929 → $886 *million* 1932 — 94% drop — something had to restart motor hoisting car up shaft — "priming the pump"[^heilbroner-017].
- When *General Theory* appeared 1936 it defended/explained course already applied: lack of sufficient business investment → government must take up slack[^heilbroner-017].
- Tongue partly in cheek: "If the Treasury were to fill old bottles with bank notes, bury them at suitable depths in disused coal mines which are then filled up to the surface with town rubbish, and leave it to private enterprise on well tried principles of *laissez-faire* to dig the notes up again ... there need be no more unemployment and with the help of the repercussions, the real income of the community would probably become a good deal larger than it is. It would, indeed, be more sensible to build houses and the like; but if there are practical difficulties in the way of doing this, the above would be better than nothing."[^heilbroner-017].
- To some projects no more sane, but rationale: need for stimulation so imperative almost anything better than nothing; if investment not directly stimulated, at least consumption — consumption floor, public works two-edged: sustain buying power of unemployed and lead resumption of private expansion[^heilbroner-017].
- Letter to *New York Times* 1934: "I see the problem of recovery in the following light: How soon will normal business enterprise come to the rescue? On what scale, by which expedients, and for how long is abnormal government expenditure advisable in the meantime?" — "abnormal" = helping hand to struggling system, not permanent interference[^heilbroner-017].

# Why pump-priming disappointed — ideology over economics

- Government spending $10-billion 1929–33 → $12B → $13B → $15B 1936; private investment recovered two-thirds to $10B; national income/consumption +50% after three years injections, yet ≥9 million still out of work[^heilbroner-017].
- Reason 1 — insufficient scale: to reach full employment needed at least twice actual program; WWII $103 billion brought not only full employment but inflation — peacetime that scale impossible, murmurs federal overstep, plus Federal Reserve more afraid of inflation (at bottom of depression!) discouraging bank lending[^heilbroner-017].
- Reason 2 — business hostility: government meant helping hand, interpreted as threatening gesture; New Deal antibusiness wave — business rights/property rights/role of government rudely shaken, asked to forget preeminence, cooperate with unions, accept regulations — regarded Washington as inimical, radical, dampening eagerness for large-scale investment[^heilbroner-017].
- Half-way trap: every effort for sufficient magnitude assailed as Socialist design; half measures frightened business from full effort itself — medicine cured one illness only to weaken with side effects; not economically unsound but ideologically upsetting[^heilbroner-017].
- Born of desperation not design: private business would eventually have found new avenues as always past, but impossible to wait four long years; talk of *stagnation* as chronic condition, Marx voice louder prima facie evidence, Veblen technocrats calling engineers, and chilling reminder Hitler/Mussolini knew what to do with unemployed — in welter, Keynes as moderate reassuring voice[^heilbroner-017].
- Keynes no opponent of private enterprise: "It is better that a man should tyrannize over his bank-balance than over his fellow citizens," and if government provide enough public investment, bulk of economy could/should be left to private initiative — explanation why inescapable remedy should work; price of government inaction graver than bold unorthodoxy[^heilbroner-017].

# Moral question, conservatism, and whether management is possible

- Hayek *Road to Serfdom* indictment of over-planned economy — Keynes sympathized/liked but wrote to Hayek: "I should ... conclude rather differently. I should say that what we want is not no planning, or even less planning, indeed I should say we almost certainly want more. But the planning should take place in a community in which as many people as possible, both leaders and followers, wholly share your own moral position. Moderate planning will be safe enough if those carrying it out are rightly oriented in their own minds and hearts to the moral issue. This is in fact already true of some of them. But the curse is that there is also an important section who could be said to want planning not in order to enjoy its fruits, but because morally they hold ideas exactly the opposite of yours, and wish to serve not God but the devil." — perhaps naive hope[^heilbroner-017].
- Can capitalism be managed — faucet on *and off* to supplement never displace private investment? Issue still with us, unresolved — Heilbroner postpones to coming chapter[^heilbroner-017].
- At heart conservative — long admirer of Edmund Burke and limited government; 1931 communism rejection: "How can I accept the [Communistic] doctrine which sets up as its bible, above and beyond criticism, an obsolete textbook which I know not only to be scientifically erroneous but without interest or application to the modern world? How can I adopt a creed which, preferring the mud to the fish, exalts the boorish proletariat above the bourgeoisie and the intelligentsia, who with all their faults, are the quality of life and surely carry the seeds of all human achievement?"[^heilbroner-017].
- Aim not submersion of capitalism but saving whole by sacrificing part — even "socialization" of investment vaguely meant; creation of capitalist economy largely eliminating unemployment — greatest threat to its continuance — no more thoughtful theory/diagnosis/convincing cure than mischievous-meddlers critics offered[^heilbroner-017].

# Interlude — theater, heart attack, and acid pen

- While constructing *General Theory* built Cambridge theater — financial backer, ticket taker when clerk failed, husband of leading lady Lydia in Shakespeare, restaurateur graphing food receipts vs entertainment, bar champagne at low discount to promote consumption — pleasant interlude, black in two years[^heilbroner-017].
- 1937 heart attack forced comparative idleness — continued active trading, editing *Economic Journal*, demolishing critics singly/en masse with sarcasm/brilliance/petulance: "Mr. X *refuses* to understand me" sigh[^heilbroner-017].
- Acid reply to "Einstein has actually done for Physics what Mr. Keynes believes himself to have done for Economics"[^heilbroner-017].
- Watching Munich, indignation at Left-Wingers defeatist pacifist letters to *New Statesman and Nation* (board member): "Surely it is impossible to believe that there can really be such a person as 'A Socialist'! I disbelieve in his existence," and "When it comes to a showdown, scarce four weeks have passed before they remember that they are pacifists and write defeatist letters to your columns, leaving the defence of freedom and civilization to Colonel Blimp and the Old School Tie, for whom Three Cheers."[^heilbroner-017].

# War — inverting the framework, How to Pay for the War

- Too ill for permanent government, given room in Treasury to have brains picked; wrote *How to Pay for the War* — deferred savings: portion of wage automatically invested in bonds not redeemable until after war, then when consumer buying needed certificates cashed[^heilbroner-017].
- Compulsory saving — change not in thinking but times: old problem too little investment symptom unemployment; new problem too much investment (armament) symptom inflation; *General Theory* framework useful upside down — incomes handed out more each turn instead of less; cure opposite: then bolster investment, now increase savings[^heilbroner-017].
- Mistaken as inflation lover — he favored *reflation* from depression depths, not inflation for its own sake: quoted *Economic Consequences of the Peace*: "Lenin is said to have declared that the best way to destroy the Capitalist System was to debauch the currency. By a continuing process of inflation, governments can confiscate, secretly and unobserved, an important part of the wealth of their citizens. By this method they not only confiscate, but they confiscate *arbitrarily*.... Lenin was certainly right. There is no subtler, no surer means of overturning the existing basis of society than to debauch the currency. The process engages all the hidden forces of economic law on the side of destruction, and does it in a manner which not one man in a million is able to diagnose."[^heilbroner-017].

# What makes a master-economist — and what remains of Keynes

- Keynes on Marshall ideal quoted as self-portrait: "The study of economics does not seem to require any specialized gifts of an unusually high order. Is it not, intellectually regarded, a very easy subject compared with the higher branches of philosophy or pure science? An easy subject, at which very few excel! The paradox finds its explanation, perhaps, in that the master-economist must possess a rare *combination* of gifts. He must be mathematician, historian, statesman, philosopher—in some degree. He must understand symbols and speak in words. He must contemplate the particular in terms of the general, and touch abstract and concrete in the same flight of thought. He must study the present in the light of the past for the purposes of the future. No part of man's nature or his institutions must lie entirely outside his regard. He must be purposeful and disinterested in a simultaneous mood; as aloof and incorruptible as an artist, yet sometimes as near the earth as a politician." — Marshall only approximated, lacking iconoclasm; Keynes nearer — Bloomsbury "nothing sacred" spilled into orthodoxy, engineering mind and hopeful heart[^heilbroner-017].
- Legacy curve: Keynesian economics dominated US 1940–1960s, then slipping until by 1980 Blinder "hard to find American economist under 40 who professed to be Keynesian"[^heilbroner-017].
- Causes: failure to reconcile Keynes macro massive flows driven by unpredictable "animal spirits" with Marshallian micro rational markets; resurgence of money/inflation questions; disenchantment with activist government and return to individual behavior as steering as well as driving force not outwitted by Keynesian policies[^heilbroner-017].
- Withered not died: 1980s onward no clear agreement how to perceive economy — crisis of vision, absence of analytic prescription — hiatus struck US/UK more than Europe; Scandinavia/Germany/Netherlands/France developed pragmatic fusion micro+macro: capitalism only workable system but unsatisfactory without strong government presence aware of both global competition and generous welfare/education for casualties — very pragmatic "worldly" philosophy for which US has yet to find workable counterpart; matter to return before book done[^heilbroner-017].

# Contradictions

- Orthodox that saving is virtuous accumulation (Smith prudence, Thrift builds wealth) versus Keynesian that thrift without enterprise decays wealth — Heilbroner presents as resolved by divorce of saving and investment in mid-19th century structure[^heilbroner-016].
- Classical prediction that interest rate as price of savings automatically equates Thrift and Enterprise versus observed Great Depression where rate fell and investment did not revive — central unresolved contradiction in *Treatise* motivating *General Theory*[^heilbroner-016].
- Keynes vs Hayek on planning: Hayek warns planning leads to serfdom; Keynes agrees more planning needed but safe only if planners share Hayek's moral position — tension left unresolved by Heilbroner as "still with us"[^heilbroner-017].
- Contemporary assessment tension: Keynesianism dominant 1940–1960s vs by 1980 nearly no under-40 Keynesians in US (Blinder) — withered not died, pragmatic European fusion vs US/UK crisis of vision unresolved[^heilbroner-017].
