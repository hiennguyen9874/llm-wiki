# The Influence of Monetary and Fiscal Policy on Aggregate Demand — Liquidity Preference, Interest-Rate Targets, Multiplier, and Crowding Out

Monetary and fiscal policy are the government's two short-run tools for shifting the aggregate-demand curve — monetary policy (money supply set by the central bank) and fiscal policy (government purchases and taxes set by president and Congress) — acting on desired spending when policymakers try to offset demand shocks and stabilize output and employment[^mankiw-063]. Long-run effects were treated earlier (Ch.12–13 fiscal effects on saving/investment/growth; Ch.16–17 monetary effects on price level); this chapter examines short-run shifts in AD and their use to stabilize the economy[^mankiw-063]. Opening question: if Congress raises taxes, how should the Fed respond — expand, contract, or leave money unchanged? — requires tracing AD effects[^mankiw-063].

## 21-1 How monetary policy influences aggregate demand

### Three reasons AD slopes downward — ranking

The downward slope of AD reflects wealth, interest-rate, and exchange-rate effects working simultaneously, but they are not equal[^mankiw-063]:

- **Wealth effect:** lower P raises real value of money holdings (part of wealth) → stimulates consumption[^mankiw-063]. Least important because money holdings are a small share of household wealth[^mankiw-063].
- **Interest-rate effect:** lower P reduces money needed for transactions → people try to lend excess → interest rate falls → stimulates investment[^mankiw-063]. **Most important for the U.S. economy**[^mankiw-063].
- **Exchange-rate effect:** lower P → lower interest rate → investors move funds abroad for higher return → domestic currency depreciates in foreign-exchange market → domestic goods cheaper relative to foreign → net exports rise[^mankiw-063]. Not large for the U.S. because exports/imports are a small fraction of GDP; more important for smaller open economies with higher trade shares[^mankiw-063].

To explain the slope more fully, the chapter develops the **theory of liquidity preference**[^mankiw-063].

### 21-1a Theory of liquidity preference — Keynes

Proposed by John Maynard Keynes in *The General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money* (1936): interest rate adjusts to balance supply and demand for money — an application of supply and demand[^mankiw-063].

**Nominal vs. real interest rates.** Nominal is as reported; real is corrected for inflation[^mankiw-063]. When expected inflation is stable — reasonable for short-run analysis — nominal and real differ by a constant, so changes refer to both[^mankiw-063]. The analysis holds expected inflation constant[^mankiw-063].

**Money supply:** Controlled by the Federal Reserve. Fed alters quantity of reserves primarily via open-market operations (buying bonds deposits dollars → reserves and money rise; selling bonds withdraws dollars → reserves and money fall) and also via discount rate (rate banks borrow reserves from Fed), reserve requirements, and interest on reserves[^mankiw-063]. Details are implementation; for AD analysis assume Fed controls money supply directly and quantity supplied is fixed at the chosen level, independent of interest rate → **vertical supply curve** (Figure 1)[^mankiw-063].

**Money demand:** Asset liquidity = ease of conversion into medium of exchange; money is most liquid by definition, which explains demand — people hold money despite higher-return alternatives because it can buy goods and services[^mankiw-063]. Many factors determine money demand, but theory emphasizes **interest rate as opportunity cost** of holding money: holding cash instead of interest-bearing bonds forgoes interest[^mankiw-063]. Higher interest rate → higher cost of holding money → quantity demanded falls; lower rate → quantity demanded rises → **downward-sloping demand curve** (Figure 1)[^mankiw-063].

**Equilibrium in the money market:** One **equilibrium interest rate** where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied[^mankiw-063]. If rate is above equilibrium (r1), quantity demanded M^d_1 < quantity supplied → surplus of money → holders buy bonds/deposit → issuers/banks lower offered rates → rate falls to equilibrium[^mankiw-063]. If rate is below equilibrium (r2), quantity demanded M^d_2 > quantity supplied → shortage → people cut bond holdings → issuers must offer higher rates to attract buyers → rate rises to equilibrium[^mankiw-063].

### 21-1b Downward slope of AD via liquidity preference

At higher price level, more money is exchanged per transaction → larger quantity of money demanded at any given interest rate → money demand curve shifts right[^mankiw-063].

Figure 2 mechanics: price level rise P1 → P2 shifts MD1 → MD2 right in panel (a)[^mankiw-063]. For fixed money supply, interest rate must rise r1 → r2 to balance supply and demand and discourage extra demand[^mankiw-063]. In panel (b), higher interest rate raises cost of borrowing and return to saving → fewer households borrow for houses (or buy smaller houses) → residential investment falls; fewer firms borrow for factories/equipment → business investment falls[^mankiw-063]. Quantity of goods and services demanded falls Y1 → Y2[^mankiw-063].

Three-step summary: (1) higher P raises money demand, (2) higher money demand raises interest rate, (3) higher interest rate reduces quantity demanded → negative P–Y relationship, downward-sloping AD[^mankiw-063]. Logic symmetrically reverses for a fall in price level[^mankiw-063].

### FYI — Interest rates in the long run vs. short run

Can the loanable-funds theory (earlier: interest balances supply of loanable funds = national saving and demand = desired investment) be reconciled with liquidity preference (interest balances money supply and demand)? Yes, by horizon[^mankiw-063]:

**Classical long-run determination (most economists agree describes long run):**
1. Output determined by capital/labor supplies and production technology (natural level)[^mankiw-063].
2. For any given output, interest rate adjusts to balance supply and demand for loanable funds[^mankiw-063].
3. Given output and interest rate, price level adjusts to balance supply and demand for money; money-supply changes cause proportionate price changes[^mankiw-063].

**Short-run determination (prices slow to adjust; short-run AS upward-sloping, not vertical):**
1. Price level is stuck at previously expected level, relatively unresponsive to changing conditions[^mankiw-063].
2. For any given (stuck) price level, interest rate adjusts to balance supply and demand for money[^mankiw-063].
3. Interest rate that balances money market influences quantity of goods and services demanded and thus level of output[^mankiw-063].

This reverses the order of analysis used for long run[^mankiw-063]. For long-run interest-rate questions use loanable-funds (saving propensities and investment opportunities); for short-run use liquidity preference (monetary policy)[^mankiw-063].

### 21-1c Changes in the money supply shift AD

For any given price level, quantity demanded changes shift the entire AD curve[^mankiw-063].

Figure 3: Fed **increases money supply** by buying bonds → MS1 → MS2 right in panel (a); money demand unchanged → interest rate must fall r1 → r2 to induce people to hold extra money[^mankiw-063]. Lower rate reduces borrowing cost and return to saving → households spend more on homes (residential investment), firms spend more on plants/equipment (business investment) → quantity demanded at price P̄ rises Y1 → Y2; at every price level quantity demanded rises → **entire AD curve shifts right AD1 → AD2**[^mankiw-063].

Conversely, Fed **contracts money supply** → raises interest rate → reduces quantity demanded at any price → AD shifts left[^mankiw-063].

QuickQuiz implication: a **decrease** in money supply raises the equilibrium interest rate (shortage of money at old rate) and shifts AD left[^mankiw-063].

### 21-1d The role of interest-rate targets

Discussion so far treats money supply as instrument: Fed buying bonds raises money and expands AD; selling bonds lowers money and contracts AD[^mankiw-063]. In recent years the Fed has conducted policy by **setting a target for the federal funds rate** — the interbank overnight rate — reevaluated every six weeks at FOMC meetings, rather than targeting money supply as at times in the past[^mankiw-063].

Reasons: money supply is hard to measure precisely and money demand fluctuates; with fixed money, demand fluctuations would cause interest-rate, AD, and output fluctuations; by announcing a funds-rate target the Fed essentially accommodates day-to-day money-demand shifts by adjusting money supply accordingly[^mankiw-063].

This does not fundamentally alter analysis — liquidity preference shows monetary policy can be described either in terms of money supply or interest rate[^mankiw-063]. When FOMC sets target at say 6%, bond traders are told to conduct whatever open-market operations ensure equilibrium rate is 6%: to lower target from 6% to 5% traders buy bonds, increasing money and lowering equilibrium rate as in Figure 3; to raise target they sell bonds, decreasing money and raising rate[^mankiw-063].

Lessons: expansionary monetary policy = increase money supply **or** lower interest rate; contractionary = decrease money supply **or** raise interest rate — two sides of same coin[^mankiw-063]. See [Federal funds rate, discount rate, and the Fed's money-supply target — how open-market operations hit the FOMC target](federal-funds-rate-and-money-supply-target.md) for 16-4d detail and [How the Fed controls the money supply — open-market operations, lending, reserve requirements, interest on reserves, and limits](fed-tools-to-control-money-supply.md) for tools.

### Box — Why the Fed watches the stock market (and vice versa)

Samuelson quip: market highly volatile and can give wrong signals, but fluctuations signal broader developments — 1990s boom: rapid GDP, falling unemployment, stock prices about fourfold; 2008–09 recession: market lost about half Nov 2007–Mar 2009[^mankiw-063].

No intrinsic concern for stock prices, but Fed monitors overall economy and market is part of puzzle[^mankiw-063]:
- Boom → households wealthier → consumption up; firms find selling new shares more attractive → investment up → AD expands[^mankiw-063].
- To stabilize AD, Fed may keep money lower and rates higher than otherwise; contractionary rate effect offsets expansionary stock effect — describes late-1990s when real rates were high by historical standards during boom[^mankiw-063].
- Fall → consumption and investment decline → depresses AD toward recession; Fed would increase money and lower rates — e.g., Oct 19 1987 crash −22.6% (one of biggest one-day drops), Fed increased money and lowered federal funds rate 7.7% early Oct → 6.6% end Oct, helping avoid recession; similarly cut rates during 2008–09 decline but insufficient to avert deep recession[^mankiw-063].
- Reverse causality: Fed influences stock values — raising rates by reducing money makes stocks less attractive (bonds earn higher return; tighter policy reduces demand/profits) → prices often fall when Fed raises rates[^mankiw-063].

### 21-1e The zero lower bound

Monetary policy works through interest rates — what if target has fallen as far as it can? In 2008–09 federal funds rate fell to about zero[^mankiw-063].

Some economists call this a **liquidity trap**: expansionary policy normally reduces rates and stimulates investment, but if rates already around zero monetary policy may be ineffective because nominal rates cannot fall much below zero — person would just hold cash rather than lend at negative nominal rate[^mankiw-063]. Extra liquidity raises portfolio liquidity but because rates cannot fall further extra liquidity may have no effect; AD, production, employment "trapped" at low levels[^mankiw-063].

Skeptics note tools remain even at zero bound[^mankiw-063]:
1. **Forward guidance:** commit to keeping rates low for extended period; even if current target cannot fall further, promise of prolonged low rates may stimulate investment[^mankiw-063].
2. **Quantitative easing (unconventional policy):** conduct expansionary open-market operations with larger variety of instruments — normally Fed buys short-term government bonds, but can also buy mortgages, corporate debt, and longer-term government bonds to lower rates on those loans and increase quantity of bank reserves; Fed actively pursued after 2008–09 financial crisis[^mankiw-063].

Some economists argue possibility of hitting zero bound justifies **setting inflation target well above zero** — under zero inflation, real rate like nominal can never go below zero; with normal inflation say 4%, central bank can push real rate to −4% by lowering nominal toward zero, giving more room to stimulate and reducing risk of liquidity trap[^mankiw-063]. Connects to [Deflation, the zero lower bound, and liquidity traps](deflation-zero-lower-bound-and-liquidity-trap.md) and [Inflation targeting and the case for higher inflation (2% vs 3–4% and NGDP)](inflation-targeting-optimal-inflation-and-ngdp.md).

## 21-2 How fiscal policy influences aggregate demand

**Fiscal policy:** government's choices regarding overall level of **government purchases and taxes**[^mankiw-063]. Long-run effects on saving/investment/growth were earlier; in short run primary effect is on AD[^mankiw-063].

### 21-2a Changes in government purchases

Policymakers change money or taxes shifts AD indirectly via firm/household spending decisions; government changing its own purchases shifts AD **directly**[^mankiw-063].

Example: Defense Department $20 billion order for new fighter planes from Boeing raises demand for Boeing output, induces hiring and production; because Boeing is part of economy, increase raises total quantity demanded at each price level → AD shifts right[^mankiw-063]. Naïve shift of exactly $20bn is not correct; two macroeconomic effects alter size: **multiplier effect** (could be larger) and **crowding-out effect** (could be smaller)[^mankiw-063].

### 21-2b The multiplier effect

When government buys $20bn from Boeing, workers see higher earnings and owners higher profits → they raise spending on consumer goods → demand for products of many other firms rises[^mankiw-063]. Because each dollar of government spending can raise aggregate demand by more than a dollar, purchases are said to have a **multiplier effect on AD**[^mankiw-063]. Feedback continues: higher consumer spending → firms hire more, profits higher → stimulate consumption again, and so on — positive feedback where higher demand → higher income → even higher demand[^mankiw-063].

Figure 4: $20bn initially shifts AD right AD1 → AD2 by exactly $20bn, but consumer response shifts still further to AD3[^mankiw-063]. Response of **investment** can strengthen multiplier — accelerator: Boeing may buy more equipment or build another plant in response to higher demand → higher demand for investment goods[^mankiw-063]. Defined: *multiplier effect — the additional shifts in aggregate demand that result when expansionary fiscal policy increases income and thereby increases consumer spending*[^mankiw-063].

### 21-2c Formula for the spending multiplier

Key number: **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)** — fraction of extra income household consumes rather than saves[^mankiw-063]. Example MPC = ¾ means household spends $0.75 of extra dollar and saves $0.25[^mankiw-063]. With MPC ¾, Boeing workers/owners earning $20bn increase consumption by ¾ × $20bn = $15bn[^mankiw-063].

Step-by-step:
- Government spends $20bn → national income (earnings/profits) rises $20bn[^mankiw-063].
- Raises consumption by MPC × $20bn → income for producers of consumption goods rises[^mankiw-063].
- That second income raises consumption by MPC × (MPC × $20bn) = MPC² × $20bn, and so on[^mankiw-063].

Total change in demand:
Change in government purchases = $20bn
First change in consumption = MPC × $20bn
Second = MPC² × $20bn
Third = MPC³ × $20bn
...
Total = (1 + MPC + MPC² + MPC³ + ...) × $20bn[^mankiw-063].

For x between −1 and +1, infinite geometric series 1 + x + x² + x³ + ... = 1/(1 − x)[^mankiw-063]. With x = MPC:

**Multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC)**[^mankiw-063].

Example MPC ¾ → 1/(1−¾)=4 → $20bn generates $80bn demand[^mankiw-063]. MPC ½ → multiplier 2[^mankiw-063]. Larger MPC → larger multiplier because consumption responds more to income[^mankiw-063].

### 21-2d Other applications of the multiplier

Logic not restricted to government purchases — applies to any event altering spending on any component of GDP — consumption, investment, government purchases, or net exports[^mankiw-063].

- Recession overseas reducing U.S. net exports by $10bn depresses U.S. income → reduces consumer spending; with MPC ¾ (multiplier 4) → $40bn AD contraction[^mankiw-063].
- Stock market boom increasing household wealth and consumption by $20bn → raises income → more consumption; with multiplier 4 → $80bn AD increase[^mankiw-063].

Multiplier shows how economy amplifies impact of spending changes — small initial change can have large effect on AD and production[^mankiw-063].

### 21-2e The crowding-out effect

Multiplier suggests $20bn Boeing purchase necessarily expands AD by more than $20bn, yet opposite effect works against it[^mankiw-063]. Increase in government purchases stimulates AD but also causes interest rate to rise, which reduces investment and puts downward pressure on AD[^mankiw-063]. *Crowding-out effect — the offset in aggregate demand that results when expansionary fiscal policy raises the interest rate and thereby reduces investment spending*[^mankiw-063].

Why crowding out occurs — money-market channel (Figure 5): government purchases raise incomes of workers/owners (and via multiplier other firms) → households plan to buy more goods/services and choose to hold more wealth in liquid form → **increase in demand for money**[^mankiw-063].

Figure 5(a) money market: Fed has not changed money supply (vertical supply unchanged); higher income shifts money demand right MD1 → MD2 → interest rate must rise r1 → r2 to keep supply and demand in balance[^mankiw-063].

Figure 5(b) AD: rate increase reduces quantity demanded because borrowing more expensive → demand for residential and business investment declines → **crowding out of investment** partially offsets impact of government purchases[^mankiw-063]. Increase initially shifts AD AD1 → AD2, but once crowding out takes place AD drops back to AD3 — final shift only to AD3, smaller than multiplier alone would imply[^mankiw-063].

**Net result (Figure 5 summary):** When government increases purchases by $20 billion, AD could rise by more or less than $20 billion depending on sizes of multiplier and crowding-out effects[^mankiw-064]. Multiplier makes shift greater than $20 billion; crowding-out pushes AD in opposite direction and, if large enough, could result in shift of less than $20 billion[^mankiw-064]. See source Figure 5 (a) Money Market / (b) Shift in AD images 000411–000412[^mankiw-064].

### 21-2f Changes in taxes

The other important fiscal instrument besides government purchases is the level of taxation[^mankiw-064]. When government cuts personal income taxes, it increases households' take-home pay; households save some of this additional income but also spend some on consumer goods; because it increases consumer spending, the tax cut shifts AD to the right; similarly a tax increase depresses consumer spending and shifts AD to the left[^mankiw-064].

Size of shift from a tax change is also affected by multiplier and crowding-out effects[^mankiw-064]. When government cuts taxes and stimulates consumer spending, earnings and profits rise which further stimulates consumer spending (multiplier); at same time higher income leads to higher money demand which tends to raise interest rates; higher rates make borrowing more costly, reducing investment spending (crowding out)[^mankiw-064]. Depending on sizes of multiplier and crowding-out, shift in AD could be larger or smaller than the tax change that causes it[^mankiw-064].

Another important determinant of tax-change shift size is households' perceptions about whether change is **permanent or temporary**[^mankiw-064]. If households expect cut of $1,000 per household to be permanent, they view it as adding substantially to financial resources and increase spending by large amount → large impact on AD; if they expect it to be temporary, they view it as adding only slightly and increase spending by only small amount → small impact[^mankiw-064]. Extreme example: 1992 President George H. W. Bush faced lingering recession and upcoming reelection campaign and announced reduction in amount of income tax withheld from paychecks; because legislated rates did not change, every dollar of reduced withholding in 1992 meant extra dollar of taxes due April 15, 1993 when returns filed — thus "tax cut" was actually only short-term loan from government; impact on consumer spending and AD was relatively small[^mankiw-064].

### FYI — How fiscal policy might affect aggregate supply

So far discussion stressed how changes in government purchases and taxes influence quantity of goods and services demanded; most economists believe short-run macroeconomic effects of fiscal policy work primarily through AD, yet fiscal policy can potentially influence quantity supplied as well[^mankiw-064].

*Tax-rate effects on AS:* One of Ten Principles is people respond to incentives; when policymakers cut tax rates, workers get to keep more of each dollar earned, so greater incentive to work and produce; if they respond, quantity supplied greater at each price level and AS shifts right[^mankiw-064]. Some economists called **supply siders** argued influence is large — so large that cut in rates will stimulate enough additional production/income that tax revenue will actually increase; this is theoretically possible but most economists do not consider it the normal case; while supply-side effects are important to consider, they are usually not large enough to cause revenue to rise when rates fall[^mankiw-064].

*Government-purchases effects on AS:* Suppose government increases expenditure on government-provided capital such as roads; roads are used by private businesses to make deliveries, increased quantity increases productivity → quantity supplied at any price increases → AS shifts right; effect probably more important long-run than short-run because it takes time to build new roads and put them into use[^mankiw-064].

> QuickQuiz (source): Suppose government reduces spending on highway construction by $10 billion. Which way does AD curve shift? Explain why shift might be larger or smaller than $10 billion[^mankiw-064]. — Answer from framework: AD shifts left; multiplier ( MPC-driven feedback) would make leftward shift larger than $10bn, crowding-out in reverse (lower money demand → lower interest rate → stimulates investment) offsets and makes shift smaller; net size depends on relative magnitudes as with $20bn fighter-plane example[^mankiw-064].

## 21-3 Using policy to stabilize the economy

Theoretical insights raise policy questions: should policymakers use monetary and fiscal instruments to control AD and stabilize economy? If so when? If not why not?[^mankiw-064]

### 21-3a The case for active stabilization policy

Return to chapter opening: when president and Congress raise taxes, how should Federal Reserve respond?[^mankiw-064] Level of taxation is one determinant of position of AD; when government raises taxes AD will fall, depressing production and employment in short run; if Fed wants to prevent adverse effect it can expand AD by increasing money supply; monetary expansion would reduce interest rates, stimulate investment, and expand AD; if set appropriately, combined changes in monetary and fiscal policy could leave AD unaffected[^mankiw-064]. This analysis is exactly sort followed by Federal Open Market Committee — they know monetary policy is important determinant of AD but also know other determinants including fiscal policy set by president and Congress, so FOMC watches debates over fiscal policy with keen eye[^mankiw-064]. This response is example of more general phenomenon: use of policy instruments to stabilize AD and as result production and employment[^mankiw-064].

Economic stabilization has been explicit goal of U.S. policy since the **Employment Act of 1946**, which states "it is the continuing policy and responsibility of the federal government to ... promote full employment and production"; in essence government has chosen to hold itself accountable for short-run macroeconomic performance[^mankiw-064].

The Act has two implications[^mankiw-064]:
1. **More modest:** government should avoid being a cause of economic fluctuations — most economists advise against large and sudden changes in monetary and fiscal policy, for such changes are likely to cause fluctuations in AD; moreover when large changes do occur, it is important that monetary and fiscal policymakers be aware of and respond to each others' actions[^mankiw-064].
2. **More ambitious:** government should respond to changes in private economy to stabilize AD[^mankiw-064]. The act was passed not long after Keynes's *The General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money*, one of most influential economics books, which emphasized key role of AD in explaining short-run fluctuations[^mankiw-064]. Keynes noted that aggregate demand fluctuates because of largely irrational waves of pessimism and optimism — "animal spirits" — which are to some extent self-fulfilling; when pessimism reigns households cut consumption and firms cut investment → lower AD, production, higher unemployment; optimism does the reverse leading to inflationary pressure; in principle government can adjust monetary and fiscal policy to offset these waves, for example when pessimistic the Fed can expand money to lower rates and expand AD, when optimistic contract money to raise rates and dampen AD — as former Fed Chairman William McChesney Martin put it, the Fed's job is to "take away the punch bowl just as the party gets going"[^mankiw-065].

### In the News — How Large Is the Fiscal Policy Multiplier? (Economist, Sept 24, 2009)

The 2008–09 global downturn prompted the biggest peacetime fiscal expansion in history: G20 stimulus packages averaged 2% of GDP in 2009 and 1.6% in 2010, meeting that week in Pittsburgh[^mankiw-065]. Coordinated scale suggested consensus, but economists were deeply divided about whether and how well stimulus works, hinging on the **fiscal multiplier** first formalised in 1931 by Richard Kahn, a student of Keynes: a multiplier of one means $1bn extra government spending raises GDP by $1bn[^mankiw-065].

**Determinants of multiplier size — Mankiw's synthesis of the Economist box:**

- **Economic conditions:** At full capacity multiplier should be zero — no spare resources, government demand merely replaces spending elsewhere; in recession with idle workers and factories, a boost can raise overall demand, and if it triggers a cascade among consumers and businesses the multiplier can be well above one[^mankiw-065].
- **Type of fiscal action:** Bridge-building may have larger multiplier than a tax cut if consumers save part of the windfall; tax cut targeted at poorer people (higher propensity to spend) may have larger multiplier than one for the affluent[^mankiw-065].
- **Reaction to borrowing:** If government action bolsters confidence and revives animal spirits, demand rises and private investment is "crowded in" → larger multiplier; if borrowing raises interest rates, private investment is "crowded out"; if consumers expect higher future taxes to finance borrowing they spend less today — both reduce multiplier, potentially below zero[^mankiw-065].

**Why estimates diverged 6-fold:**

- Obama administration economists, assuming the federal funds rate stays constant for four years, expected a multiplier of **1.6 for government purchases and 1.0 for tax cuts** from the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act[^mankiw-065].
- John Cogan, Tobias Cwik, John Taylor and Volker Wieland, using models where interest rates and taxes rise more quickly in response to higher borrowing, found much smaller multipliers — the U.S. stimulus would boost GDP by **only one-sixth** as much as the Obama team expected[^mankiw-065].

**Empirical approaches and limits:**

- **Micro case studies** (largely U.S. tax rebates/cuts): permanent cuts have bigger impact than temporary ones; consumers who find it hard to borrow (e.g., close to credit-card limit) spend more of the windfall; but case studies do not measure overall output impact[^mankiw-065].
- **Statistical/macroeconometric GDP regressions:** need to isolate discretionary stimulus from automatic rises in social-security spending and falls in tax revenues that naturally accompany recessions[^mankiw-065]. This approach narrowed estimates in some areas and yielded cross-country comparisons: multipliers bigger in **closed economies than open** (less leaks via imports), traditionally bigger in **rich countries than emerging** ones where investors take fright more quickly and push rates up; but overall range remains as wide as theoretical models[^mankiw-065].

**Why post-war evidence may not apply to 2008–09:**

Most spending evidence is based on military outlays, but 2008–09 packages were heavily infrastructure-focused; interest rates in many rich countries were near zero (may increase potency and need for fiscal stimulus); because of financial crisis relatively more people faced borrowing constraints (would increase effectiveness of a tax cut); at same time highly indebted consumers keen to deleverage, leading to lower multiplier; investors then had more reason to worry about rich-country fiscal positions than emerging markets — reversing traditional rich vs emerging ranking[^mankiw-065].

> Bottom line from the Economist via Mankiw: "economists are flying blind. They can make relative judgments with some confidence. Temporary tax cuts pack less punch than permanent ones, for instance. Fiscal multipliers will probably be lower in heavily indebted economies than in prudent ones. But policymakers looking for precise estimates are deluding themselves"[^mankiw-065].

### Keynesians in the White House

When a reporter in 1961 asked President John F. Kennedy why he advocated a tax cut, Kennedy replied, "To stimulate the economy. Don't you remember your Economics 101?" — based on this chapter's fiscal policy analysis where a tax cut raises consumer spending, expands AD, and increases production and employment[^mankiw-065].

Kennedy's team included **James Tobin and Robert Solow** (later Nobel laureates), who as students in the 1940s had studied Keynes's *General Theory* (then only a few years old) and were putting Keynes's ideas into action[^mankiw-065].

Part of the Kennedy proposal was an **investment tax credit** giving firms a break for investing in new capital: higher investment stimulates AD immediately and also increases productive capacity over time — coupling short-run higher AD goal with long-run higher aggregate supply goal; when finally enacted in **1964** it helped usher in robust growth[^mankiw-065]. Since then policymakers have periodically used fiscal policy to control AD, notably President Barack Obama's **American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA) 2009** with substantial spending increases; the In the News boxes discuss the debate over its effectiveness[^mankiw-065]. Although tax changes powerfully influence AD, they also alter incentives and can affect aggregate supply of goods and services — the theme of the FYI on AS effects and the Kennedy investment credit[^mankiw-065].

### 21-3b The case against active stabilization policy

Some economists argue government should **avoid active use** of monetary and fiscal policy to try to stabilize the economy, instead setting instruments to achieve long-run goals such as rapid growth and low inflation and leaving the economy to deal with short-run fluctuations on its own; they may admit policy can stabilize in theory but doubt it can in practice[^mankiw-065].

**Primary argument — long lags:**

- **Monetary policy:** works by changing interest rates → investment spending, but many firms plan investment far in advance; most economists believe it takes **at least 6 months** for changes to have much effect on output and employment, and effects then last for several years; critics say because of this lag the Fed often reacts too late and ends up being a **cause rather than cure** for fluctuations; they advocate **passive monetary policy**, such as slow and steady growth in the money supply[^mankiw-065].
- **Fiscal policy:** also works with a lag, but largely attributable to **political process**: in the United States most changes must go through congressional committees in both House and Senate, be passed by both bodies, and be signed by president — can take **months or, in some cases, years**; by the time change is passed and ready, economic conditions may have changed[^mankiw-065].

Lags are a problem in part because **economic forecasting is so imprecise**: if forecasters could accurately predict conditions a year ahead, policymakers could look ahead despite lags and stabilize; in practice major recessions and depressions arrive without much advance warning — best policymakers can do is respond as changes occur[^mankiw-065]. TOLES cartoon 2001 Washington Post (image 000420) illustrates forecasting difficulty[^mankiw-065].

### 21-3c Automatic stabilizers

All economists — advocates and critics of stabilization policy — agree that implementation lags reduce efficacy; the economy would be more stable if some lags could be avoided — in fact they have been[^mankiw-065].

*Definition:* **automatic stabilizers** are changes in fiscal policy that stimulate aggregate demand when the economy goes into a recession **without policymakers having to take any deliberate action**[^mankiw-065].

- **Tax system — most important stabilizer:** when economy goes into recession, taxes collected fall automatically because almost all taxes are tied to economic activity: personal income tax depends on households' incomes, payroll tax on workers' earnings, corporate income tax on firms' profits; because incomes/earnings/profits fall in recession, government's tax revenue falls → **automatic tax cut** stimulates AD and reduces magnitude of fluctuations[^mankiw-065].
- **Government spending:** when economy goes into recession and workers are laid off, more people apply for unemployment insurance benefits, welfare benefits, and other income support → **automatic increase in government spending** stimulates AD exactly when insufficient to maintain full employment; indeed when unemployment insurance was first enacted in the 1930s economists advocated it partly for this automatic-stabilizer power[^mankiw-065].

Automatic stabilizers are **not sufficiently strong to prevent recessions completely**, but without them output and employment would probably be more volatile[^mankiw-065].

**Policy implication — balanced-budget rule:** Many economists oppose a constitutional amendment requiring the federal government always to run a balanced budget, as some politicians propose: when economy goes into recession taxes fall, spending rises, and budget moves toward deficit; under a strict balanced-budget rule government would be forced to look for ways to raise taxes or cut spending in a recession — in other words the rule would **eliminate the automatic stabilizers** inherent in current taxes and spending[^mankiw-065]. ### 21-4 Conclusion

Before policymakers make any change in policy, they need to consider all effects of their decisions; earlier models examined classical long-run effects — fiscal policy on saving, investment and growth, monetary policy on price level and inflation; this chapter examined short-run effects via aggregate demand; policymakers must keep **both long-run and short-run goals** in mind[^mankiw-065]:

- When Congress reduces government spending to balance the budget, it needs to consider both long-run effects on saving and growth and short-run effects on AD and employment[^mankiw-065].
- When the Fed reduces growth rate of money supply, it must consider long-run effect on inflation as well as short-run effect on production[^mankiw-065].

"In all parts of government, policymakers must keep in mind both long-run and short-run goals"[^mankiw-065].

### Summary — Mankiw §21

- Developing a theory of short-run fluctuations, Keynes proposed **theory of liquidity preference** to explain determinants of interest rate: interest rate adjusts to balance supply and demand for money; increase in price level raises money demand and raises equilibrium interest rate for any given money supply; because higher rate raises borrowing cost it reduces investment and quantity demanded → downward-sloping AD[^mankiw-065].
- Policymakers can influence AD with **monetary policy**: increase in money supply reduces equilibrium interest rate for any given price level; because lower rate stimulates investment, AD shifts right; decrease shifts AD left[^mankiw-065].
- Policymakers can also influence AD with **fiscal policy**: increase in government purchases or cut in taxes shifts AD right; decrease shifts AD left[^mankiw-065].
- When government alters spending or taxes, resulting shift in AD can be larger or smaller than fiscal change: **multiplier effect** tends to amplify; **crowding-out effect** tends to dampen[^mankiw-065].
- Because monetary and fiscal policy can influence AD, government sometimes uses instruments to stabilize economy; economists disagree about how active it should be: advocates say changes in attitudes by households/firms shift AD and if government does not respond result is undesirable unnecessary fluctuations; critics say monetary and fiscal policy work with such long lags that attempts often end up destabilizing[^mankiw-065].

Key Concepts (p. 474): theory of liquidity preference, fiscal policy, multiplier effect, crowding-out effect, **automatic stabilizers**[^mankiw-065].
