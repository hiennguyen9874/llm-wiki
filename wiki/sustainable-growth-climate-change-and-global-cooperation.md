# Sustainable growth, climate change, and global cooperation

Economic growth tends to increase environmental harm unless countered by policy; the source distinguishes geographically bounded harm, which can be substantially reduced with political will and resources, from climate change, whose global externality requires coordination among countries.[^krugman-ch9-051]

## Mechanism and distribution

Burning coal, oil, and natural gas increases atmospheric carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that traps heat and changes weather patterns. The source associates the resulting climate risks with extreme weather, sea-level rise and flooding, agricultural disruption, and disproportionately high costs for poorer countries.[^krugman-ch9-051]

Growth has historically been energy intensive: more homes, factories, and vehicles usually require more power. At the source's time of writing, it reports fossil fuels as 85% of world energy consumption and clean renewables as 11%, reflecting historically lower fossil-fuel costs.[^krugman-ch9-051] This is a dated textbook statistic, not a current energy-accounting estimate.

The source frames burden sharing as a central obstacle. Wealthy economies produced much of the historical carbon dioxide stock, while rapidly growing economies—especially China—drove much of the more recent increase. Its 1990–2017 chart shows Chinese annual CO₂ emissions rising from roughly 2,500 to above 10,000 million metric tons and overtaking the comparatively flatter US and European series.[^krugman-ch9-051]

## Policy problem: local versus global

- **Local degradation:** Air pollution, habitat loss, and related locally bounded harms can be reduced by dedicated regulation and resources. The source's China example attributes improved Beijing air quality after a 2014 "war on pollution" to coal and plant restrictions, vehicle restrictions, and subsidized electric buses, alongside reported 38% real-GDP growth from 2014 to 2019.[^krugman-ch9-051]
- **Global degradation:** Emissions reductions require many countries to act even when costs and historic responsibility are distributed unevenly. The source presents the 2015 Paris Agreement as a commitment by 196 countries to reduce greenhouse-gas emissions and limit warming to no more than 2°C; cooperation between the United States, China, and India, including rich-country financing for poorer countries' transitions, was its stated linchpin.[^krugman-ch9-051]

The account of the United States' planned 2020 withdrawal is contemporaneous to the text and should not be treated as a statement of current treaty participation.[^krugman-ch9-051]

## Compatibility with long-run growth

The source reports that most economists studying the issue judged that substantial emissions reductions can coexist with long-run growth: the expected effect is a modest reduction in the *rise* of real GDP per capita, and clean-energy innovation lowers that cost over time.[^krugman-ch9-051] Its proposed policy mix is regulation and environmental standards, market incentives for firms and households to switch energy sources, technological progress in clean energy, and continued international cooperation.[^krugman-ch9-051]

This is not an argument that GDP automatically decouples from environmental damage. It instead treats environmental protection as an active policy and coordination problem, complementing the distinction between GDP value and physical throughput in [Decoupling economic growth from energy and material throughput](decoupling-economic-growth-from-energy-and-material-throughput.md).

### Per-capita growth and emissions are not mechanically coupled

A 2000–2014 exercise dataset makes the qualification concrete: China (9.24% real GDP per capita; 7.48% CO₂ per-capita growth) and Bangladesh (4.33%; 4.47%) had rapid growth in both, but Nigeria (5.03%; −1.30%) combined rapid output growth with falling per-capita emissions; Ireland (1.30%; −2.56%), the United Kingdom (1.02%; −2.20%), and the United States (0.85%; −1.27%) similarly had positive output growth and declining per-capita emissions.[^krugman-ch9-052] These descriptive rates cannot identify whether growth caused emissions changes or vice versa; they show that high growth does not *necessarily* entail high per-capita emissions growth.
