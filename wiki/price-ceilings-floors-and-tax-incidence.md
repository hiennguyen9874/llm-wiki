# Price Ceilings, Price Floors, and Tax Incidence — Gasoline Shortages, Rent Control, Minimum Wage, and Who Pays Taxes

Government attempts to legislate prices away from equilibrium create predictable distortions — price ceilings below equilibrium generate shortages while price floors above equilibrium generate surpluses — and attempts to legislate who pays a tax fail because the wedge between buyer and seller prices divides by relative elasticity, not statute, as shown by 1973 gasoline lines, rent control's short-run versus long-run shortages, teenage unemployment under the minimum wage, and the 1990 luxury-tax burden falling on yacht builders[^mankiw-025].

## 6-1 Price ceilings

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum on the price of a good or service; a **price floor** is a legal minimum[^mankiw-025]. Policymakers impose ceilings when they deem the equilibrium price unfair to buyers, floors when they deem it unfair to sellers, but prices in competitive markets are the outcome of millions of supply-and-demand decisions that balance and coordinate activity; legal decrees obscure those signals[^mankiw-025].

### Gasoline and the 1973 OPEC shock (Figure 2)

Before OPEC reduced crude-oil production, gasoline equilibrium price $P_{1}$ was below the government price ceiling — ceiling not binding, no effect (panel a)[^mankiw-025]. Crude oil is the major input to gasoline; higher crude prices raised gasoline production costs and shifted gasoline supply left from $S_{1}$ to $S_{2}$[^mankiw-025].

In an unregulated market, price would have risen from $P_{1}$ to $P_{2}$ and no shortage would have resulted[^mankiw-025]. With the binding ceiling preventing that rise, at the ceiling consumers wanted to buy $Q_{D}$ but producers would sell only $Q_{S}$; the gap $Q_{D} - Q_{S}$ measures the shortage that produced hours-long gas lines[^mankiw-025]. Economists therefore blame the price regulation as well as OPEC: without the ceiling the supply shift would have raised price rather than created lines; laws were later repealed so gasoline prices could adjust to equilibrate supply and demand[^mankiw-025].

### Rent control — short run versus long run (Figure 3)

Rent control is a common price ceiling intended to make housing affordable; economists criticize it as inefficient, one calling it "the best way to destroy a city, other than bombing"[^mankiw-025].

- **Short run (panel a):** supply and demand for housing are relatively inelastic — landlords have fixed apartment stock, people take time to adjust housing arrangements — so a binding ceiling below equilibrium causes only a small shortage; primary effect is lower rents[^mankiw-025].
- **Long run (panel b):** both curves more elastic — landlords build fewer new apartments and fail to maintain existing ones; low rents encourage people to seek own apartments rather than live with parents or roommates and draw more people into the city — quantity supplied falls substantially and quantity demanded rises substantially, creating a large shortage[^mankiw-025].

**Rationing and quality under shortage:** landlords allocate via long waiting lists, preference to tenants without children, racial discrimination, or under-the-table bribes to superintendents that push total price back toward equilibrium[^mankiw-025]. Incentives erode: in free markets landlords keep buildings clean and safe because desirable apartments command higher prices; under shortages with waiting lists that incentive disappears — "Why should a landlord spend money to maintain and improve the property when people are waiting to move in as it is?" Tenants get lower rents but lower-quality housing[^mankiw-025]. Policymakers then add anti-discrimination and minimum-adequacy laws that are costly to enforce; in a free market competition would largely make such laws less necessary because price adjustment eliminates the shortage that breeds undesirable behavior[^mankiw-025]. Principle recalled: people respond to incentives[^mankiw-025].

## 6-1b How price floors affect market outcomes

A binding price floor causes a **surplus** (quantity supplied > quantity demanded); a nonbinding floor has no effect[^mankiw-025].

Ice-cream market equilibrium $3, 100 cones (Figure 4)[^mankiw-025]:
- **Floor $2 (panel a) below equilibrium:** not binding — market moves to equilibrium, no effect[^mankiw-025].
- **Floor $4 (panel b) above equilibrium:** binding — market price equals $4; 120 cones supplied versus 80 demanded → surplus of 40 cones; some sellers unable to sell at the going price must be rationed, potentially by seller bias (racial or familial ties) rather than price; in a free market price rations and sellers can sell all they want at equilibrium[^mankiw-025].

### Minimum wage as a price floor (Figure 5)

The minimum wage is a floor on labor price, first federally under Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938; in 2015 federal level $7.25 per hour (some states higher); France, despite ~30% lower average income than US, had minimum more than 30% higher[^mankiw-025].

Labor market analysis (panel a free, panel b with binding minimum): if minimum above equilibrium, quantity of labor supplied exceeds quantity demanded → unemployment[^mankiw-025]. Those employed earn more, those unable to find jobs earn less[^mankiw-025].

- **Heterogeneity:** highly skilled/experienced workers have equilibrium wages well above minimum → not affected (not binding)[^mankiw-025].
- **Teenagers most affected:** lowest skill/experience, equilibrium wages low, willing to accept lower wage for on-the-job training (some intern for zero pay, exempt from law — otherwise opportunities might not exist); minimum binds more often for teenagers than others[^mankiw-025].
- **Employment estimate:** typical study finds 10% increase in minimum wage depresses teenage employment by 1 to 3 percent; interpreting requires noting 10% statutory increase does not raise average teenage wage by 10% (many already above minimum, imperfect enforcement), so 1–3% drop is significant[^mankiw-025].
- **Supply-side effects:** higher minimum increases number of teenagers looking for jobs; studies find some high-school students drop out to take jobs, displacing other dropouts who become unemployed[^mankiw-025].

**Debate:** advocates say it raises working-poor incomes — at $7.25, two adults working 40 hrs/week year-round earn $30,160, 24% above poverty line for family of four but less than half median family income; they concede some unemployment but view effects as small and beneficial on net[^mankiw-025]. Opponents say it is poorly targeted and counterproductive: causes unemployment, encourages school dropout, prevents on-the-job training, and less than a third of minimum-wage earners are in families below poverty — many are middle-class teenagers working part-time for extra spending money[^mankiw-025].

**Ask the Experts — Minimum Wage $15:** IGM question whether raising federal minimum gradually to $15 by 2020 would substantially lower employment rate for low-wage US workers: distribution shows majority agree (graphic 000154 not visually inspected)[^mankiw-025].

## 6-1c Evaluating price controls

Mankiw recalls Ten Principles: markets are usually a good way to organize activity (hence economists usually oppose price ceilings/floors — prices balance supply and demand and coordinate activity), but governments can sometimes improve outcomes (motivation for controls is perceived unfairness — rent control for affordability, minimum wage against poverty)[^mankiw-025].

Yet controls often hurt intended beneficiaries — rent control discourages maintenance and makes housing hard to find; minimum wage helps some but makes others unemployed[^mankiw-025].

**Alternatives that preserve price signals:**
- **Housing:** government rent subsidy paying fraction of rent for poor families — does not reduce quantity supplied, no shortage[^mankiw-025].
- **Labor:** wage subsidies supplementing low-wage incomes without discouraging hiring — e.g., **earned income tax credit (EITC)**[^mankiw-025].

Trade-off: subsidies cost government money and require higher taxes, which themselves have costs (analyzed next)[^mankiw-025].

## 6-2 Taxes

Taxes raise revenue for roads, schools, defense; studying their market effects uses supply and demand[^mankiw-025].

Annual $0.50 ice-cream-cone tax thought experiment: lobbies argue buyers versus sellers should pay; mayor proposes 50-50 split; analysis asks who actually bears burden — **tax incidence** (how burden is distributed)[^mankiw-025].

### 6-2a How taxes on sellers affect market outcomes (Figure 6)

Three steps[^mankiw-025]:
1. Tax not levied on buyers → demand curve unchanged; tax on sellers shifts supply[^mankiw-025].
2. Cost increase reduces quantity supplied at every price → supply shifts left/upward from $S_{1}$ to $S_{2}$ by exact tax $0.50 — for any market price effective price to sellers is $0.50 lower; to induce any given quantity market price must be $0.50 higher[^mankiw-025].
3. Equilibrium price rises $3.00 → $3.30, quantity falls 100 → 90 cones → market shrinks[^mankiw-025].

**Incidence:** buyers pay $0.30 more per cone ($3.30 vs $3.00); sellers receive $3.30 from buyers but keep only $2.80 after remitting $0.50, down from $3.00 — both worse off despite sellers remitting the tax[^mankiw-025]. Lessons: taxes discourage activity and burden is shared[^mankiw-025].

### 6-2b How taxes on buyers affect market outcomes (Figure 7)

1. Supply unchanged (sellers have same incentive at any given market price); demand shifts because buyers pay market price plus tax[^mankiw-025].
2. Buying less attractive → demand shifts left/downward $D_{1}$ → $D_{2}$ by $0.50 — effective price to buyers $0.50 higher than market price; to induce any given quantity market price must be $0.50 lower[^mankiw-025].
3. Equilibrium market price falls $3.00 → $2.80, quantity 100 → 90; sellers get lower price; buyers pay $2.80 to sellers plus $0.50 tax = $3.30 out-of-pocket, up from $3.00 — again quantity falls and burden shared[^mankiw-025].

**Equivalence:** taxes on sellers and on buyers are equivalent — both place same wedge between price buyers pay and price sellers receive; which curve shifts is irrelevant for incidence; only who physically sends money differs[^mankiw-025]. Bowl analogy: collecting $0.50 in a bowl on the counter — whether seller drops it in after sale or buyer drops it in at purchase, once new equilibrium reached sharing is same[^mankiw-025].

**Payroll tax application (Figure 8):** FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act) funds Social Security/Medicare; in 2015 total 15.3% of earnings; law says half paid by firms, half deducted from workers' paychecks; analysis as tax on labor where price is wage: payroll tax wedges wage firms pay above wage workers receive — workers' received wage falls, firms' paid wage rises; sharing has nothing to do with legislated 50-50 split; same outcome if entire tax levied on one side; lawmakers can dictate remittance but not true burden, which depends on supply and demand[^mankiw-025].

### 6-2c Elasticity and tax incidence (Figure 9)

Burden rarely 50-50; it splits by relative elasticity[^mankiw-025].

- **Panel (a) elastic supply / inelastic demand (demand steep, supply flat):** price received by sellers falls only slightly, price paid by buyers rises substantially → buyers bear most[^mankiw-025].
- **Panel (b) inelastic supply / elastic demand (supply steep, demand flat):** price paid by buyers rises only slightly, price received by sellers falls substantially → sellers bear most[^mankiw-025].

**General lesson:** tax burden falls more heavily on the less elastic side — that side has fewer good alternatives and is less willing to leave the market when conditions worsen[^mankiw-025].

Applied to payroll tax: most labor economists believe labor supply much less elastic than labor demand → workers bear most of burden, far from intended 50-50[^mankiw-025].

**Luxury tax case (1990–1993):** Congress taxed yachts, private airplanes, furs, jewelry, expensive cars to raise revenue from rich; demand for yachts quite elastic (millionaires can buy house, vacation, or bequest instead) while supply relatively inelastic short run (factories not easily repurposed, workers not eager to switch careers) → burden fell largely on firms and workers building yachts, who are not wealthy — burden on middle class rather than rich; hardship led to repeal of most luxury tax in 1993[^mankiw-025].

## Summary and key concepts

## Key Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Price ceiling | Legal maximum price; binding if below equilibrium → shortage $Q_{D} - Q_{S}$[^mankiw-025] |
| Price floor | Legal minimum price; binding if above equilibrium → surplus $Q_{S} - Q_{D}$[^mankiw-025] |
| Binding vs nonbinding | Ceiling effective only below $P^{*}$, floor only above $P^{*}$; otherwise irrelevant[^mankiw-025] |
| Rent control | Price ceiling on housing; short-run small shortage (inelastic), long-run large shortage (elastic)[^mankiw-025] |
| Minimum wage | Price floor on labor; 2015 federal $7.25; binds most for teenage least-skilled workers[^mankiw-025] |
| Tax incidence | Distribution of tax burden between buyers and sellers[^mankiw-025] |
| Wedge | Gap between price buyers pay and price sellers receive equal to tax; same regardless of statutory payer[^mankiw-025] |
| Payroll tax (FICA) | Tax on wages (labor price); 15.3% in 2015; statutory split does not determine economic incidence[^mankiw-025] |
| Elasticity rule of incidence | Burden falls more on less elastic side of market[^mankiw-025] |
| Total shortage / surplus | At ceiling: $Q_{D} - Q_{S}$; at floor: $Q_{S} - Q_{D}$; Figure 4 surplus 40 cones (120–80)[^mankiw-025] |

## Applied problems and quantitative illustrations (Mankiw Ch.6 Problems 1–10)

### 1. Classical-music price ceiling $40
Binding ceiling below equilibrium → shortage: quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied; fewer people attend concerts. Price cannot rise to ration scarce seats, so non-price allocation (queues, lottery, favoritism) determines who gets tickets and quantity transacted falls to quantity supplied[^mankiw-026].

### 2. Cheese price floor with government purchase
- **(a)** Binding floor above equilibrium raises price and lowers quantity sold; at the floor $Q_{S} > Q_{D}$ → surplus[^mankiw-026].
- **(b)** Producers' total revenue can fall — floor raises price but lowers quantity sold; with inelastic demand revenue rises, with elastic demand revenue falls, so loss is possible[^mankiw-026].
- **(c)** Government purchase of surplus at the floor: producers benefit (sell all they supply at floor price); taxpayers/consumers lose (pay higher price for cheese bought plus taxes to fund surplus purchase and storage/disposal costs); deadweight loss larger than basic floor[^mankiw-026].

### 3. Frisbee schedules — equilibrium and floor/ceiling sequence
Table: $(P, Q_{D}, Q_{S})$ = (11,1,15), (10,2,12), (9,4,9), (8,6,6), (7,8,3), (6,10,1) in millions[^mankiw-026].
- **(a)** Equilibrium $P^{*}=\$8$, $Q^{*}=6$ million where $Q_{D}=Q_{S}$[^mankiw-026].
- **(b)** Price floor $\$2$ above equilibrium → $\$10$: binding floor → market price $10, quantity sold = min(Q_{D},Q_{S}) = 2$ million, surplus 10 million (12−2)[^mankiw-026].
- **(c)** Repeal floor and impose ceiling $\$1$ below former floor → $\$9$: $9 > P^{*}=8$ → ceiling nonbinding (above equilibrium), so price returns to $\$8$ and quantity to 6 million; at $9$ itself $Q_{D}=4$, $Q_{S}=9$ would imply surplus 5 if it bound, but it does not bind[^mankiw-026].

### 4. $2 tax per case of beer
- Without tax: consumer price = producer price, quantity at intersection; gap zero[^mankiw-026].
- With tax: wedge $2 between price paid by consumers and price received by producers; quantity sold falls; consumer price rises, producer price falls, difference exactly $2[^mankiw-026].

### 5. Payroll tax shift trick
Raising payroll tax on firms and cutting it on workers by same amount leaves total wedge unchanged → no change in equilibrium labor quantity, gross wage, or net wage, and no change in tax revenue; incidence depends on elasticities, not statutory labels — the senator's goal fails[^mankiw-026]. Demonstrates equivalence result from 6-2a/6-2b applied to labor[^mankiw-026].

### 6. $500 tax on luxury cars
Price paid by consumers rises by *less* than $500 (and producer price falls to absorb remainder) except in the extreme where demand is perfectly inelastic or supply perfectly elastic; burden splits by elasticity, so neither side bears the full $500 and quantity falls[^mankiw-026].

### 7. $0.50 gasoline tax
- **(a)** Tax on producers vs consumers irrelevant — same wedge, same final consumer price, producer price, and quantity; statutory incidence does not matter[^mankiw-026].
- **(b)** More elastic demand makes tax more effective at reducing quantity consumed — flatter demand → larger quantity drop for same $0.50 wedge[^mankiw-026].
- **(c)** Consumers hurt: pay higher effective price and buy less[^mankiw-026].
- **(d)** Oil-industry workers hurt: lower producer price and lower quantity → lower employment/wages in that sector[^mankiw-026].

### 8. Minimum wage as binding floor on unskilled labor
- **(a)** Binding minimum → wage = floor > $P^{*}$, employment = $Q_{D}$ at that wage, unemployment = $Q_{S}-Q_{D}$, total wage payments = floor × $Q_{D}$[^mankiw-026].
- **(b)** Increase in floor reduces employment; magnitude depends on elasticity of *demand* for labor (firms' responsiveness), not supply — supply determines how many want jobs but employment is demand-determined[^mankiw-026].
- **(c)** Increase raises unemployment ($Q_{S}-Q_{D}$); change depends on *both* elasticities — supply elasticity governs rise in $Q_{S}$, demand elasticity governs fall in $Q_{D}$[^mankiw-026].
- **(d)** Total wage payments ($W \times Q_{D}$) rise if demand inelastic (wage gain outweighs job loss) and fall if demand elastic — so inelastic demand → higher payroll to remaining employed, elastic → lower[^mankiw-026].

### 9. Fenway Park — perfectly inelastic supply and ticket tax
Capacity fixed ~38,000 → vertical supply; $5$ per-ticket tax paid by buyer shifts demand down by $5$, but market quantity unchanged at 38,000 and market price received by sellers falls by full $5$ → burden falls entirely on team owners, not fans, because inelastic side (supply) bears the tax[^mankiw-026]. Classic illustration of elasticity rule: less elastic side bears more[^mankiw-026].

### 10. Linear model $Q^{S}=2P$, $Q^{D}=300-P$
- **(a)** Equilibrium $2P = 300-P$ → $P^{*}=100$, $Q^{*}=200$[^mankiw-026].
- **(b)** Ceiling $90 < 100$ → binding: $Q_{S}=180$, $Q_{D}=210$, shortage $30$; price $90$, transactions $180$[^mankiw-026].
- **(c)** Floor $90 < 100$ → nonbinding (below equilibrium): no effect — price $100$, $Q_{S}=Q_{D}=200$, neither shortage nor surplus[^mankiw-026].
- **(d)** Tax $30$ on producers → new supply $Q^{S}=2(P-30)=2P-60$: equilibrium $2P-60=300-P$ → $P_{buyer}=120$, $P_{seller}=90$, $Q=180$, wedge $30$, quantity falls 20, no shortage/surplus — market clears at the wedge[^mankiw-026].
