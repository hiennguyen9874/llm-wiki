# Money demand — liquidity, opportunity cost, and curve shifters

Money demand is the amount of monetary assets that households and firms choose to hold for transaction convenience. Holding money trades liquidity against forgone returns on comparable **short-term** assets: at a higher nominal short-term interest rate, that opportunity cost is higher and the quantity demanded is lower; at a lower rate, it is lower and the quantity demanded is higher.[^krugman-ch15-082]

## The money demand curve

The money demand curve plots the nominal interest rate vertically and quantity of money demanded horizontally. It slopes downward because a rise in the interest rate makes cash and low-yielding transaction deposits costlier to hold relative to short-term interest-bearing assets; a fall does the reverse.[^krugman-ch15-082]

- The relevant comparison is normally with **short-term** assets, which compete with money as relatively liquid stores of value. Long-term rates may differ from short-term rates, but are not the direct opportunity cost in this portfolio choice.[^krugman-ch15-082]
- Short-term asset rates usually move together through portfolio reallocation: investors sell relatively low-yielding assets and buy relatively high-yielding ones, tending to narrow persistent differences.[^krugman-ch15-082]
- The curve uses the **nominal**, not real, interest rate. A change in that rate causes a movement along the curve, not a shift.[^krugman-ch15-082]

Figures 15-1 and 15-2 were visually inspected: they respectively show the downward-sloping curve and a rightward shift for increased money demand versus a leftward shift for decreased money demand.[^krugman-ch15-082]

## What shifts money demand

At a given interest rate, four factors shift the whole curve:

| Change | Effect on money demand | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Higher aggregate price level | Right | More dollars are needed for the same basket of transactions. The source states that demand is proportional to the price level, other things equal. |
| Higher real GDP | Right | More output bought and sold requires more transaction balances. |
| Better payment, credit, or banking technology | Left | Cards, digital payments, ATMs, and online transfers reduce the need to hold cash or surplus checking balances. |
| Institutional change | Either direction | Rules that change money's relative return or convenience change desired balances. |

For example, the source says elimination of Regulation Q in 1980 allowed U.S. banks to pay interest on checking accounts, reducing the return forgone by holding such accounts and raising money demand.[^krugman-ch15-082] Its Japan example attributes high cash holdings partly to less pervasive card-payment infrastructure and low short-term rates; this is an illustrative account, not a general claim that technology alone determines cash use.[^krugman-ch15-082]

## Source-era illustration

The source compares March 2019 with March 2020: the federal funds rate fell from 2.41% to 0.08% and one-month Treasury-bill rate from 2.45% to 0.12%, while its reported demand-deposit rate remained 0.06% and currency's rate zero. Thus the stated opportunity-cost spreads narrowed sharply. These are dated observations, not current rate data.[^krugman-ch15-082]
