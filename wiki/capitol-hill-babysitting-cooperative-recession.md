# Capitol Hill Babysitting Cooperative recession and scrip monetary policy

A parable of a 1970s Washington D.C. recession that occurred without any change in real resources, cured temporarily by printing quasi-money, then recreated by oversupply — illustrating that demand shortfalls and monetary policy can have real effects when prices are sticky[^harford-ch2].

## The co-op and its scrip

- The Capitol Hill Babysitting Cooperative organized ~200 families, mostly congressional staff, trading babysitting via scrip: each piece worth 0.5 hours (15 minutes at peak times)[^harford-ch2].
- New members received 40 pieces; departing members had to return all scrip to the committee; bookkeeping via physical notes avoided complex owing/owed accounting[^harford-ch2].

## The babysitting recession

- Even new members with only 40 pieces (~10 hours prime-time) felt reserves were thin and preferred to babysit before going out; longer-standing members were even shorter due to a glitch where scrip was slowly removed to pay administrators[^harford-ch2].
- Result: everyone wanted to save scrip by staying in, so no one could earn by babysitting — a self-perpetuating circle because one couple's income is another's spending, and with little spending there was little income[^harford-ch2].
- Harford notes the parallel to wider economies that sicken for no obvious reason: no loss of factories, roads, resources, skills, or willingness to trade — yet mutually desired exchanges do not happen[^harford-ch2].

## Documented history and popularization

- Described by members Joan and Richard Sweeney (one a mid-ranking Treasury monetary-research official) in a 1977 paper in the *Journal of Money, Credit and Banking*[^harford-ch2].
- Popularized by Paul Krugman (Nobel Memorial Prize winner, *New York Times* columnist), who wrote it changed his life and helped him resist fatalism about recessions; Krugman called it a case for simple technical fixes in the spirit of Keynes's "magneto trouble" and Bill Phillips's machine metaphor[^harford-ch2].

## Failed legalistic fix

- The lawyer-run co-op first diagnosed shirking and imposed a rule requiring couples to go out at least once every six months; Harford notes this minimal requirement signals desperation and it did not work[^harford-ch2].

## Monetary fix and its reversal

- Committee issued an extra 20 pieces per member and to new joiners (60 total) while requiring only 40 back on departure, making supply generous and growing; the recession abated[^harford-ch2].
- The story's neglected ending: the committee overshot — "after a while, it naturally followed there was too much scrip and more people wanted to go out than to sit" — recreating a recession on the opposite side, now with unwilling sitters, as fewer evenings were exchanged than desired[^harford-ch2].
- Burned by the botched experiment, the committee abandoned monetary approaches and returned to crude enforcement, with the Sweeneys drily noting "A truth squad is envisaged to find out why individuals aren't sitting enough"[^harford-ch2].

## What the parable demonstrates

- Even a simple economy with a few hundred like-minded traders and a central directory can be hard to manage[^harford-ch2].
- Well-chosen stories can illuminate how economies work[^harford-ch2].
- Monetary policy — changing the money supply — can cure a demand-driven downturn in a straightforward way, which should be surprising because money itself creates no additional labour, machines, or infrastructure[^harford-ch2].
- The Douglas Adams epigraph about making leaves legal tender (inflation to three forests per ship's peanut) warns that unlimited money creation is not costless[^harford-ch2].

## Harford's assessment

- The co-op's lawyers lacked monetary expertise; a competent technocratic authority could in principle issue a sensible amount of scrip[^harford-ch2].
- Conversely, a modern economy of 300+ million people embedded in global trade with a complex financial sector is far harder to calibrate than a babysitting circle, so correct monetary calibration remains tricky even for experts[^harford-ch2].
- Lesson retained under optimism: in principle the economy can be stimulated by printing money, via sticky prices, but details and dosage are difficult[^harford-ch2].
