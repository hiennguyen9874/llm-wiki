# Elasticity of Demand and Supply — Income, Cross-Price, and Three Applications

Elasticity quantifies how much buyers and sellers respond to price, income, and related-good changes; income and cross-price elasticities classify normal versus inferior goods and substitutes versus complements, while supply elasticity depends on flexibility and time horizon — together determining whether a technological improvement helps or hurts farmers, why OPEC can raise oil prices only temporarily, and why drug interdiction and drug education push crime in opposite directions[^mankiw-024].

## 5-1g Other demand elasticities

Beyond price elasticity of demand, economists use income and cross-price elasticities to describe buyer behavior[^mankiw-024].

### Income elasticity of demand

$$
\text{income elasticity of demand} = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity demanded}}{\text{percentage change in income}} [^mankiw-024]
$$

- **Normal goods:** higher income raises quantity demanded → move in same direction → positive income elasticity[^mankiw-024].
- **Inferior goods:** higher income lowers quantity demanded → move in opposite directions → negative income elasticity; example: bus rides[^mankiw-024].
- **Magnitude among normal goods:** necessities (food, clothing) have small income elasticities — consumers buy some even when income is low; luxuries (caviar, diamonds) have large income elasticities — consumers feel they can do without if income is too low[^mankiw-024].

### Cross-price elasticity of demand

$$
\text{cross-price elasticity of demand} = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity demanded of good 1}}{\text{percentage change in price of good 2}} [^mankiw-024]
$$

Sign depends on relationship discussed in Ch.4[^mankiw-024]:

- **Substitutes** (hamburgers/hot dogs) — typically used in place of one another: increase in hot-dog price induces hamburgers instead; price of good 2 and quantity of good 1 move same direction → positive[^mankiw-024].
- **Complements** (computers/software) — typically used together: increase in computer price reduces quantity of software demanded → negative[^mankiw-024].

## 5-2 The elasticity of supply

### 5-2a Definition and determinants

**Price elasticity of supply** — measure of how much quantity supplied responds to a change in price of that good[^mankiw-024]. Elastic supply responds substantially to price; inelastic supply responds only slightly[^mankiw-024].

Determinants:

- **Flexibility of sellers to change amount produced.** Beachfront land is almost impossible to produce more of → inelastic; manufactured goods (books, cars, televisions) → elastic because firms can run factories longer in response to higher price[^mankiw-024].
- **Time horizon — most important in most markets.** Supply is usually more elastic in long run than short run: over short periods firms cannot easily change factory size, quantity supplied not very responsive; over longer periods firms can build/close plants and firms can enter/exit, so quantity supplied can respond substantially[^mankiw-024].

### 5-2b Computing with the midpoint method

$$
\text{price elasticity of supply} = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity supplied}}{\text{percentage change in price}} [^mankiw-024]
$$

**Milk example:** price $2.85 → $3.15 per gallon, quantity 9,000 → 11,000 per month[^mankiw-024].

- Percentage change in price = (3.15 − 2.85) / 3.00 × 100 = 10 percent[^mankiw-024].
- Percentage change in quantity = (11,000 − 9,000) / 10,000 × 100 = 20 percent[^mankiw-024].
- Elasticity = 20% / 10% = 2 → quantity supplied changes proportionately twice as much as price[^mankiw-024].

### 5-2c Variety of supply curves (Figure 5)

Elasticity is reflected in the slope of the supply curve; all percentage changes calculated via midpoint method[^mankiw-024].

- **Zero elasticity — perfectly inelastic:** vertical curve, same quantity regardless of price[^mankiw-024].
- **0 < elasticity < 1 — inelastic:** steep curve[^mankiw-024].
- **Elasticity = 1 — unit elastic:** intermediate[^mankiw-024].
- **Elasticity > 1 — elastic:** flatter curve, quantity responds more[^mankiw-024].
- **Infinite elasticity — perfectly elastic:** horizontal curve, very small price changes lead to very large quantity changes[^mankiw-024].

**Not constant along a curve (Figure 6):** firms with limited capacity are very elastic at low quantity and very inelastic at high quantity[^mankiw-024].

- Idle capacity (plants/equipment not fully used): small price increase makes it profitable to use idle capacity → high elasticity. Example: price $3 → $4 (29% increase midpoint) raises quantity 100 → 200 (67% increase) → elasticity > 1, elastic range[^mankiw-024].
- Near full capacity: further production requires building new plants, price must rise substantially → low elasticity. Example: price $12 → $15 (22% increase) raises quantity 500 → 525 (5% increase) → elasticity < 1, inelastic range[^mankiw-024].

## 5-3 Three applications of supply, demand, and elasticity

All three questions — farming good news, OPEC, and drug crime — are answered with supply-demand shifts plus elasticity[^mankiw-024].

### 5-3a Can good news for farming be bad news for farmers? (Figure 7)

Kansas wheat farmer producing more per acre; university announces hybrid raising yield 20% per acre[^mankiw-024].

Three steps:

1. Supply curve shifts (more willing to supply at any price); demand unchanged (desire to buy wheat unchanged)[^mankiw-024].
2. Supply shifts right S₁ → S₂[^mankiw-024].
3. Quantity sold 100 → 110, price $3 → $2 in the numerical example[^mankiw-024].

**Total revenue P × Q:** conflicting effects — Q rises but P falls[^mankiw-024]. Elasticity determines outcome: demand for basic foodstuffs like wheat is usually inelastic (inexpensive, few substitutes) → price fall proportionately larger than quantity rise → total revenue falls $300 → $220 ($3×100 to $2×110)[^mankiw-024].

**Paradox of competition:** each farmer is small, takes price as given — for any given price, adopting hybrid to sell more is better; yet when all do, supply rises, price falls, farmers collectively worse off[^mankiw-024].

**Historical magnitude:** 200 years ago most Americans farmed; by 1950 10 million (17% of labor force) worked on farms; today fewer than 3 million (2%) yet produce about five times as much as in 1950 — technology increase plus inelastic demand drove revenues and labor out of farming[^mankiw-024].

**Policy implication:** certain farm programs pay farmers not to plant all land → reduce supply, raise price, raise total revenue under inelastic demand; no single farmer would fallow alone as price-taker, but all together benefit — though what is good for farmers is not necessarily good for society (consumers pay less when technology improves, or pay more under supply-reduction programs)[^mankiw-024].

### 5-3b Why did OPEC fail to keep the price of oil high? (Figure 8)

Timeline (inflation-adjusted)[^mankiw-024]:

- 1973–74: OPEC jointly reduced supply → price up >50%[^mankiw-024].
- 1979–81: price approximately doubled[^mankiw-024].
- 1982–85: price declined ~10% per year, dissatisfaction and disarray[^mankiw-024].
- 1986: cooperation broke down → price plunged 45%[^mankiw-024].
- 1990: back to 1970 level, stayed low through most of 1990s; 2000s–2010s fluctuations driven not by OPEC restrictions but by world booms/busts shifting demand and fracking increasing supply[^mankiw-024].

**Short run (panel a):** supply and demand relatively inelastic — oil reserves/capacity cannot change quickly; buying habits do not respond immediately → both curves steep → shift S₁ → S₂ causes large price rise P₁ → P₂[^mankiw-024].

**Long run (panel b):** more elastic — outside producers increase exploration/capacity; consumers conserve (replace inefficient cars) → both curves flatter → same horizontal shift S₁ → S₂ causes much smaller price increase[^mankiw-024].

Lesson: OPEC incomes rose in short run even though each member sold less, because price rose by so much; in long run same supply reduction proved less profitable because elastic response muted price increase — raising prices is easier short run than long run[^mankiw-024].

### 5-3c Does drug interdiction increase or decrease drug-related crime? (Figure 9)

Drug interdiction (more federal agents) raises cost of selling drugs → reduces quantity supplied at any price; demand unchanged → supply shifts left S₁ → S₂; equilibrium price rises P₁ → P₂, quantity falls Q₁ → Q₂ → drug use falls[^mankiw-024].

**Crime effect depends on total amount paid P×Q and demand elasticity.** If demand is inelastic — likely because addicts do not break habits quickly in response to higher price — price rises proportionately more than quantity falls → total revenue (amount paid by users) rises, even as use falls → addicts who stole to support habit need more cash → drug-related crime can rise[^mankiw-024].

**Policy alternative — drug education (panel b):** reduces demand D₁ → D₂; both price and quantity fall → total revenue falls → both drug use and drug-related crime fall[^mankiw-024].

**Time horizon nuance:** interdiction advocates note long-run elasticity may be higher than short-run: higher prices do not substantially affect established addicts short run but may discourage youth experimentation, leading to fewer addicts over time → interdiction could increase crime short run but decrease it long run[^mankiw-024].

## 6-1 Controls on prices — preview (ice-cream price ceilings)

Chapter 6 opens by noting two roles — scientists versus policy advisers — and turns to policies that directly control prices using only supply-demand tools[^mankiw-024].

- **Price ceiling:** legal maximum price (e.g., rent control); **price floor:** legal minimum (e.g., minimum wage) — enacted when policymakers deem market price unfair to buyers or sellers, but can generate inequities of their own[^mankiw-024].
- **American Association of Ice-Cream Eaters vs National Organization of Ice-Cream Makers** lobbying over the $3 equilibrium cone price illustrates competing demands for ceilings versus floors[^mankiw-024].

**How price ceilings affect outcomes (Figure 1 ice-cream market, equilibrium $3, 100 cones):**

- **Nonbinding ceiling $4 (panel a):** above equilibrium → forces do not hit ceiling, no effect on price or quantity[^mankiw-024].
- **Binding ceiling $2 (panel b):** below equilibrium → market price equals $2; quantity demanded 125 exceeds quantity supplied 75 → shortage (excess demand) of 50 cones; price cannot rise to equilibrate[^mankiw-024].

**Rationing under shortage:** mechanism could be long lines (wasteful), seller bias selling only to friends/relatives/own racial or ethnic group (inefficient and unfair, may not go to highest-value buyer) — contrast with free competitive market rationing by price, which is efficient and impersonal: anyone willing to pay market price can get a cone[^mankiw-024]. Even though ceiling aims to help buyers, not all benefit — some pay less but wait, others get nothing[^mankiw-024].

## Key Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Income elasticity of demand | % change Q demanded / % change income; >0 normal, <0 inferior; small for necessities, large for luxuries[^mankiw-024] |
| Cross-price elasticity of demand | % change Q demanded of good 1 / % change price of good 2; >0 substitutes, <0 complements[^mankiw-024] |
| Price elasticity of supply | % change Q supplied / % change price; depends on flexibility and time horizon[^mankiw-024] |
| Perfectly inelastic / perfectly elastic supply | Elasticity 0 vertical / infinity horizontal[^mankiw-024] |
| Midpoint method | Percent changes computed relative to midpoint of start and end values[^mankiw-024] |
| Total revenue (P×Q) | Moves with price when demand inelastic, opposite when elastic — drives farm-paradox and drug-crime results[^mankiw-024] |
| Price ceiling / price floor | Legal maximum/minimum price; binding only when set below/above equilibrium, causing shortage/surplus[^mankiw-024] |
