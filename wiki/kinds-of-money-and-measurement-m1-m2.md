# Kinds of money and measurement — commodity vs fiat and M1/M2

The quantity of money — the money stock — powerfully influences the economy, but the boundary between monetary and nonmonetary assets is not sharp; the U.S. measures money as currency plus readily spendable bank deposits (M1/M2), built historically on commodity money and now on fiat decreed by government and sustained by social convention[^mankiw-16-1].

## Commodity money

- **Commodity money** is money that takes the form of a commodity with intrinsic value — value even if not used as money[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Gold** is the classic example: intrinsic use in industry and jewelry; easy to carry, measure, and verify; historically common; an economy using gold (or paper convertible into gold on demand) is on a **gold standard**[^mankiw-16-1].
- Why gold endures scientifically: chemical stability (not a gas, does not corrode or combust, not radioactive), rarity but not too rare, does not kill you, low melting point vs platinum — the Columbia chemical-engineer elimination of 118 elements leaves gold as the sweet spot, per Mankiw's *In the News* reprint of Goldstein/Kestenbaum[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Cigarettes** as commodity money: in WWII prisoner-of-war camps and in Moscow in the late 1980s as the Soviet Union broke up, cigarettes served as medium, unit, and store — even nonsmokers accepted them knowing others would[^mankiw-16-1].

## Fiat money

- **Fiat money** is money without intrinsic value, established as money by government decree — *fiat* = order or decree[^mankiw-16-1].
- Example: U.S. paper dollars vs Monopoly dollars from Parker Brothers; the former reads "This note is legal tender for all debts, public and private" and is prosecuted against counterfeiting; the latter is not accepted[^mankiw-16-1].
- Government decree alone is insufficient: acceptance depends on expectations and convention. In 1980s Moscow the ruble remained official yet people preferred cigarettes or U.S. dollars because they expected others to accept them[^mankiw-16-1].

## Money in the U.S. economy — what counts?

- **Currency** — paper bills and coins in the hands of the public — is the most obviously included component[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Demand deposits** — balances in bank accounts that depositors can access on demand by writing a check or swiping a debit card — are almost as convenient and are counted[^mankiw-16-1].
- The boundary blurs: savings-account balances transferable to checking, and money-market mutual-fund balances checkable, are also plausibly money[^mankiw-16-1].
- In practice different analysts draw the line differently; the two standard U.S. measures are **M1 and M2**, where M2 includes more assets than M1 (Figure 1 in source)[^mankiw-16-1]. For Mankiw's purposes the distinction does not hinge — the point is the stock includes currency plus readily accessible bank deposits usable to buy goods and services[^mankiw-16-1].

## Puzzle: where is all the currency?

- In January 2016, $1.4 trillion currency outstanding ÷ 252 million adults (16+) ≈ $5,500 per adult — far more than most people carry[^mankiw-16-1].
- Two plausible explanations: (1) more than half circulates abroad — people in unstable monetary systems prefer dollars to domestic assets; (2) much held by drug dealers, tax evaders, and other criminals who avoid bank paper trails despite loss/theft risk and foregone interest[^mankiw-16-1].

## Why credit cards aren't money

- Credit cards are excluded from money measures: they are a method of **deferring** payment, not a method of payment[^mankiw-16-1]. The issuer's bank pays the restaurant; the cardholder later repays the bank, usually by check against demand deposits — that checking balance is the money[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Debit cards** differ: they automatically withdraw from a bank account, giving immediate access to deposits — more like a check than a credit card — so underlying balances are counted in money[^mankiw-16-1].
- Nonetheless credit cards matter analytically: by allowing monthly settlement rather than sporadic cash holding, they likely reduce average money holdings; introduction and spread of credit cards may reduce quantity of money people choose to hold[^mankiw-16-1].
