# Kinds of money and measurement — commodity vs fiat and M1/M2

The quantity of money — the money stock — powerfully influences the economy, but the boundary between monetary and nonmonetary assets is not sharp; the U.S. measures money as currency plus readily spendable bank deposits (M1/M2), built historically on commodity money and now on fiat decreed by government and sustained by social convention[^mankiw-16-1].

## Commodity money

- **Commodity money** is money that takes the form of a commodity with intrinsic value — value even if not used as money[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Gold** is the classic example: intrinsic use in industry and jewelry; easy to carry, measure, and verify; historically common; an economy using gold (or paper convertible into gold on demand) is on a **gold standard**[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Commodity-backed money** is a medium of exchange with no intrinsic value whose ultimate value is guaranteed by conversion into valuable goods. In the eighteenth-century transition, private-bank notes redeemable for gold or silver economized on the metal tied up in circulation: a bank needed only enough reserves for ordinary redemptions and could lend the rest[^krugman-ch14-076]. Smith's “waggon-way through the air” metaphor characterizes this saving of real resources[^krugman-ch14-076].
- Why gold endures scientifically: chemical stability (not a gas, does not corrode or combust, not radioactive), rarity but not too rare, does not kill you, low melting point vs platinum — the Columbia chemical-engineer elimination of 118 elements leaves gold as the sweet spot, per Mankiw's *In the News* reprint of Goldstein/Kestenbaum[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Cigarettes** as commodity money: in WWII prisoner-of-war camps and in Moscow in the late 1980s as the Soviet Union broke up, cigarettes served as medium, unit, and store — even nonsmokers accepted them knowing others would[^mankiw-16-1].

## Fiat money

- **Fiat money** is money without intrinsic value, established as money by government decree — *fiat* = order or decree[^mankiw-16-1].
- The U.S. transition illustrates that money's form and backing can change: early colonists used scarce European coin alongside tobacco and wampum; before the Civil War private-bank notes promised redemption in gold or silver, with perceived bank weakness making some notes less acceptable. The federal government issued unbacked Civil War *greenbacks* in 1862, linked the dollar to gold after 1873, and ended the gold link for good in 1971 after an earlier 1933 break and temporary restoration[^krugman-ch14-077].
- Example: U.S. paper dollars vs Monopoly dollars from Parker Brothers; the former reads "This note is legal tender for all debts, public and private" and is prosecuted against counterfeiting; the latter is not accepted[^mankiw-16-1].
- Government decree alone is insufficient: acceptance depends on expectations and convention. In 1980s Moscow the ruble remained official yet people preferred cigarettes or U.S. dollars because they expected others to accept them[^mankiw-16-1].
- Relative to commodity-backed money, fiat money consumes virtually no real resource to create and its supply can be adjusted to economic conditions rather than discoveries of gold or silver. Its corresponding risks are counterfeiting and governments' incentive to overissue money to finance bills, which can produce high inflation[^krugman-ch14-076].

## Money in the U.S. economy — what counts?

- **Currency** — paper bills and coins in the hands of the public — is the most obviously included component[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Demand deposits** — balances in bank accounts that depositors can access on demand by writing a check or swiping a debit card — are almost as convenient and are counted[^mankiw-16-1]. Krugman/Wells calls these **checkable bank deposits** and includes access by checks, debit cards, and digital payments[^krugman-ch14-076].
- The boundary blurs: savings-account balances transferable to checking, and money-market mutual-fund balances checkable, are also plausibly money[^mankiw-16-1].
- In practice different analysts draw the line differently; the two standard U.S. measures are **M1 and M2**, where M2 includes more assets than M1 (Figure 1 in source)[^mankiw-16-1]. For Mankiw's purposes the distinction does not hinge — the point is the stock includes currency plus readily accessible bank deposits usable to buy goods and services[^mankiw-16-1].
- More precisely, M1 contains currency and checkable deposits; M2 adds near-moneys such as savings deposits, small-denomination time deposits, and money-market funds. Near-moneys cannot be spent directly but can readily be converted to cash or checkable deposits; they typically pay interest, unlike currency[^krugman-ch14-076]. The Fed stopped publishing M3 in 2006 after judging it no longer useful[^krugman-ch14-076].
- The source's **March 2020** snapshot is historical, not current: M1 was $4,267.6 billion (40.9% currency; 59.1% checkable deposits), while M2 was $16,103.9 billion, comprising 26.5% M1, 63.3% savings deposits, 6.6% money-market funds, and 3.6% time deposits[^krugman-ch14-076].

### Aggregate-accounting checks

The following are derived from the problem source's stated components and M1/M2 definitions, rather than reported source answers.[^krugman-ch14-081]

- Moving proceeds from a nonmoney asset (such as stock) to a **savings** account raises M2 but not M1; moving them to a **checking** account raises both. Moving savings to checking raises M1 but leaves M2 unchanged.
- Depositing cash already held by the public into checking leaves both M1 and M2 unchanged: currency falls while checkable deposits rise by the same amount. Depositing it into savings lowers M1 but leaves M2 unchanged.
- With M1 = currency + checkable deposits and M2 = M1 + savings + time deposits + money-market funds, the supplied December data imply M1 rose from **$1,692.8 billion in 2009** to **$3,977.1 billion in 2019**, and M2 from **$8,483.4 billion** to **$15,302.2 billion** (all amounts nominal and historical). Currency's share of M1 fell from **51.0%** to **43.0%**; its share of M2 moved from **10.2%** to **11.2%**.

## Puzzle: where is all the currency?

- In January 2016, $1.4 trillion currency outstanding ÷ 252 million adults (16+) ≈ $5,500 per adult — far more than most people carry[^mankiw-16-1].
- Krugman/Wells reports a later, similarly dated snapshot: nearly $1.8 trillion, or almost $5,500 per person, in circulation; it attributes roughly 60% to foreign residents using dollars as a trusted medium and store of value, while cash registers and concealment of illegal or tax-evading transactions account for some of the remainder[^krugman-ch14-076].
- Cash use differs sharply even among technologically advanced countries: currency in circulation as a share of 2019 GDP was 1.2% in Sweden, 8.2% in the United States, and 21.1% in Japan. The source characterizes Sweden as nearly cashless and Japanese cash use as chiefly cultural; these are contextual observations, not timeless measurements[^krugman-ch14-076].
- Two plausible explanations: (1) more than half circulates abroad — people in unstable monetary systems prefer dollars to domestic assets; (2) much held by drug dealers, tax evaders, and other criminals who avoid bank paper trails despite loss/theft risk and foregone interest[^mankiw-16-1].

## Why credit cards aren't money

- Credit cards are excluded from money measures: they are a method of **deferring** payment, not a method of payment[^mankiw-16-1]. The issuer's bank pays the restaurant; the cardholder later repays the bank, usually by check against demand deposits — that checking balance is the money[^mankiw-16-1].
- **Debit cards** differ: they automatically withdraw from a bank account, giving immediate access to deposits — more like a check than a credit card — so underlying balances are counted in money[^mankiw-16-1].
- Nonetheless credit cards matter analytically: by allowing monthly settlement rather than sporadic cash holding, they likely reduce average money holdings; introduction and spread of credit cards may reduce quantity of money people choose to hold[^mankiw-16-1].
