# Decoupling economic growth from energy and material throughput

In rich economies GDP per person has grown strongly while energy use per person has been flat or falling, because value increasingly comes from efficiency, design, and digital forms rather than additional physical throughput[^harford-growth-limits].

## Core distinction

GDP measures willingness to pay, not physical resource use. Worries about water, CO₂, coal, or rare earths are real environmental constraints, but they do not imply the economy itself cannot keep growing; energy growth and economic growth are not the same thing[^harford-growth-limits].

Intuition: a worried household may turn off heating and wear coats; extra income removes coats but does not imply a lottery winner will boil themselves alive. Food intake, clothing weight, and similar physical needs saturate — the rich may waste more (e.g., wearing clothes once) but not exponentially more[^harford-growth-limits].

## Evidence of decoupling (Harford's comparisons)

- **United States, 1986–2011:** GDP per person grew >2.5% per year (near doubling of total GDP); energy use rose <25% total; energy per person fell -0.17% per year. Peak energy per person was 1978. Offshoring correction is small: imports <20% of GDP, offset by exports[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Other rich economies:** Same pattern, all with far lower energy per person than the US. Peak energy per person: UK 1973, Germany 1979, Japan 2000, EU similarly. UK energy per person at 50-year low at time of writing. Population growth slowing dramatically, so with flat/falling per-capita use there is no necessary indefinite rise in total energy consumption. Forecasting remains hazardous; period choice changes exact numbers but not the story[^harford-growth-limits].

Harford presents these as illustrative calculations, not formal energy accounts; precise figures depend on interval but trend is robust across choices[^harford-growth-limits].

## Mechanisms of dematerialization

- **Efficiency:** LEDs vs incandescent bulbs; laptops vs mainframes[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Design and aesthetic value:** same materials become more valuable in better-designed objects — food, clothes, computers, bicycles, washing machines; haute couture and haute cuisine derive value from design, not extra material[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Digital substitution:** e-books and audiobooks have almost no physical form[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Urban and service intensity:** New York City — high-income creative powerhouse for >1 century (publishing, music, fashion, art, finance, software) — uses less energy per person than the US average and less than any US state, showing high-value activity need not be energy-intensive[^harford-growth-limits].

## Future sources of value without proportional energy

Harford speculates but does not predict: virtual reality (already visible from Facebook to World of Warcraft), longevity, freedom from discomfort, medical advances, or household machines converting trash into new goods (circular production). The point is logical possibility, not a forecast of flying cars, jetpacks, or teleporters[^harford-growth-limits]. Such dematerialized growth would not trigger Murphy's thermodynamic boiling-point calculation, which assumes energy throughput grows exponentially with the economy[^harford-growth-limits].

## Implications

Physical limits on economic growth may one day exist, but Harford argues we do not currently need to worry about them; tangible environmental problems (carbon, water, etc.) should be addressed by pricing the resource, not by targeting growth itself (see [Zero-growth economy — leisure, debt, and pricing real constraints](zero-growth-leisure-debt-and-carbon-pricing.md))[^harford-growth-limits].
