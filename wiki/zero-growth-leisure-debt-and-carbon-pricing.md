# Zero-growth economy — leisure, debt, and pricing real constraints

Even if physical throughput forced economic growth to stop, the economic model does not collapse; the adjustments concern how productivity gains are split between leisure and employment, and how households and governments manage debt when incomes no longer rise. Harford argues the binding policy lever is pricing scarce resources, not capping GDP[^harford-growth-limits].

## Growth, productivity, and the work-leisure choice

Technological progress raises output per hour. Without GDP growth, one of two outcomes must eventually occur: we work less, or some work intensively while others are jobless[^harford-growth-limits].

- **Keynes's 1930 prediction.** In "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren" Keynes expected that by ~2030 basic material needs would be met and the "real...permanent problem" would be using freedom from want to "live wisely and agreeably and well" — filling leisure[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Record since.** Keynes was largely right about growth in the UK and other rich countries, but leisure has expanded far more slowly than he expected. Time-use surveys 1965–2003: American women gained 4–6 hours/week leisure, men 6–8 hours/week. Combined with longer lives, more schooling, and earlier retirement, the share of life spent working is falling, but slowly[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Zero-growth risk.** If output per hour keeps rising while GDP is flat and behavior does not change, inequality of work could emerge — some flat out, many jobless. Harford notes this is conditional on continuing as today[^harford-growth-limits].

Policy option: economist Robert Frank's proposal to tax consumption (higher consumption → higher tax). As the tax rises, leisure (stroll, painting) becomes relatively attractive. Progressive income taxes already approximate this, since a consumption tax is like an income tax with an exemption for saving/investing; Harford suggests doing more is an open political choice, not an economic necessity[^harford-growth-limits].

## Debt without growth

Rising incomes make debts easier to bear (mortgage with annual raises), but growth is not required to repay debt[^harford-growth-limits].

- **Households:** Lifecycle borrowing remains rational when each generation is no richer than the previous: borrow for college, car, house; repay; save for retirement; live off savings. Desire to move purchasing power across time does not depend on aggregate growth[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Governments:** A zero- or low-growth world would require rethinking conventions that assume ever-larger, richer future generations:
  - Cannot fund social security/health care on expectation of larger/richer cohorts paying the bill[^harford-growth-limits].
  - Cannot routinely run small deficits assuming stable debt/GDP ratio[^harford-growth-limits].
  - May need to gradually pay off debts and even accumulate assets, as some resource-rich countries do. Harford calls this a modest behavioral change requiring more care with debt, not a revolution in economics[^harford-growth-limits].

## The real constraint and the right instrument

If concerned about CO₂ — Harford says we should be — raise the cost of emitting CO₂ via a carbon tax or emissions-permit scheme. The same logic applies to energy, rare earth metals, water, or other nonrenewables. Economic growth itself is not the problem; consuming nonrenewable resources is. Harford sees no need to "click the YouTube links" on growth impossibility when tangible environmental problems are addressable now through prices[^harford-growth-limits].

This connects to decoupling (see [Decoupling economic growth from energy and material throughput](decoupling-economic-growth-from-energy-and-material-throughput.md)): to the extent value dematerializes, physical limits do not automatically cap GDP, though they do justify pricing externalities.
