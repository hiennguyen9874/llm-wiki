# Unions and collective bargaining — cartel wages, insider-outsider unemployment, and the efficiency debate

A union is a worker association that bargains with employers over wages, benefits, and working conditions; by acting together as a cartel rather than as individuals, unions can use collective bargaining and the strike threat to obtain wages above the competitive equilibrium, creating unemployment for outsiders and pushing down nonunion wages, with effects shaped by antitrust exemptions and labor law[^mankiw-048].

## Definitions

- **Union:** a worker association that bargains with employers over wages, benefits, and working conditions[^mankiw-048].
- **Collective bargaining:** the process by which unions and firms agree on terms of employment[^mankiw-048].
- **Strike:** the organized withdrawal of labor from a firm by a union[^mankiw-048].
- **Cartel:** a group of sellers acting together to exert joint market power — unions are a cartel of labor sellers[^mankiw-048].

## Union prevalence

- **United States:** only 11 percent of U.S. workers now belong to unions, but at peak in the 1940s–1950s about a third of the labor force was unionized[^mankiw-048].
- **Europe:** Belgium, Norway, and Sweden >50% belong to unions; in France and Germany a majority of workers have wages set by collective bargaining by law, even though only some are union members — wages not determined by competitive supply-demand equilibrium[^mankiw-048].

## Economics of unions as a labor cartel

Union bargaining asks for higher wages, better benefits, and better working conditions than the firm would offer without a union; if no agreement, the union can strike, reducing production, sales, and profit, so a firm facing a strike threat is likely to agree to higher wages[^mankiw-048].

Empirically economists find union workers earn about 10 to 20 percent more than similar nonunion workers[^mankiw-048].

When the union wage is above equilibrium:

- Quantity of labor supplied rises and quantity demanded falls → unemployment[^mankiw-048].
- Insiders who remain employed at the higher wage are better off; those previously employed who now lose jobs are worse off — unions cause conflict between insiders who benefit and outsiders who do not get union jobs[^mankiw-048].

### Insider-outsider spillover

Outsiders respond in two ways: remain unemployed waiting for a chance to become insiders at the high union wage, or take jobs in nonunion firms[^mankiw-048]. When unions raise wages in one sector, labor supply increases in the nonunion sector, which in turn reduces wages there — workers in unions reap the benefit of collective bargaining while workers not in unions bear some of the cost[^mankiw-048].

Mankiw prompt: a union in the auto industry that raises wages at General Motors and Ford reduces employment there and creates a surplus; displaced workers increase supply in other industries, depressing wages elsewhere[^mankiw-048].

## Legal framework — why cartels are legal for labor

Normally explicit agreements among cartel members are illegal as "conspiracy in restraint of trade" under antitrust laws, prosecuted civilly and criminally[^mankiw-048]. Unions are exempt — policymakers who wrote the antitrust laws believed workers needed greater market power when bargaining with employers, and various laws encourage union formation[^mankiw-048]:

- **Wagner Act of 1935:** prevents employers from interfering when workers try to organize unions and requires employers to bargain with unions in good faith[^mankiw-048].
- **National Labor Relations Board (NLRB):** government agency that enforces workers' right to unionize[^mankiw-048].

Ongoing policy margins:

- **Right-to-work laws:** give workers in a unionized firm the right to choose whether to join; absent such laws, unions can insist via collective bargaining that membership be a condition of employment; debated by state lawmakers[^mankiw-048].
- **Striker replacement:** Washington at times debated a law preventing firms from hiring permanent replacements for workers on strike — would make strikes more costly for firms, increasing union market power[^mankiw-048].

## Are unions good or bad for the economy? The debate

Economists disagree; both sides are presented without consensus[^mankiw-048].

### Critics — unions as inefficient, inequitable cartels

- Raise wages above competitive levels → reduce quantity demanded, cause unemployment, reduce wages in rest of economy[^mankiw-048].
- Resulting allocation is inefficient because high union wages reduce employment in unionized firms below efficient competitive level, and inequitable because some workers benefit at expense of others[^mankiw-048].

### Advocates — unions as antidote to employer market power and as efficient voice

1. **Countervailing power / company town:** In extreme case a single firm does most hiring in a region; without a union workers must move or stop working; firm could use market power to pay lower wages and offer worse conditions than under competition; union balances power and protects workers from being at mercy of owners[^mankiw-048].
2. **Efficient job-attribute mix:** Jobs involve many non-wage attributes — hours, overtime, vacations, sick leave, health benefits, promotion schedules, job security — unions representing workers' views allow firms to provide the right mix and keep a happy, productive workforce, even if wages are pushed above equilibrium causing some unemployment[^mankiw-048].

Mankiw conclusion: "In the end, there is no consensus among economists about whether unions are good or bad for the economy. Like many institutions, their influence is probably beneficial in some circumstances and adverse in others."[^mankiw-048]

## Connection to natural rate and other wage-above-equilibrium sources

Unions are the third of four reasons Mankiw cites for natural (persistent) unemployment alongside job search, minimum-wage laws, and efficiency wages — all produce wages above the level that balances supply and demand, but minimum-wage laws and unions prevent firms from lowering wages while efficiency wages are voluntary profit-maximizing choices to keep wages high despite surplus[^mankiw-048].

The chapter's conclusion emphasizes the natural rate changes over time as job-search technology, legislated minimum wages, union membership, and firms' reliance on efficiency wages evolve; unemployment is not a simple problem with a simple solution but is profoundly influenced by how society is organized[^mankiw-048].
