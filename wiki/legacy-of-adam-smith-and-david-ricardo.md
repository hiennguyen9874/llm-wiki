# The Legacy of Adam Smith and David Ricardo — Comparative Advantage, Free Trade, and Everyday Applications

Smith's 1776 household maxim and Ricardo's 1817 England-Portugal model together established comparative advantage as the core logic for gains from specialization; that logic still underpins the modern near-consensus among economists for free trade and explains interdependence in everyday decisions from international wine and cloth to whether a highly talented individual should outsource lawn mowing[^mankiw-018].

## Smith — the household analogy for trade

Economists have long understood gains from trade; Mankiw opens with Smith's maxim from *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776), described as a landmark in the analysis of trade and economic interdependence[^mankiw-018]:

> "It is a maxim of every prudent master of a family, never to attempt to make at home what it will cost him more to make than to buy. The tailor does not attempt to make his own shoes, but buys them of the shoemaker. The shoemaker does not attempt to make his own clothes but employs a tailor. The farmer attempts to make neither the one nor the other, but employs those different artificers. All of them find it for their interest to employ their whole industry in a way in which they have some advantage over their neighbors, and to purchase with a part of its produce, or what is the same thing, with the price of part of it, whatever else they have occasion for."[^mankiw-018]

The analogy extends family specialization to nations: each employs its whole industry where it has some advantage and purchases the rest — the intuitive precursor to formal comparative advantage[^mankiw-018].

## Ricardo — formal comparative advantage and politics

Smith inspired David Ricardo, a millionaire stockbroker who became an economist, to formalize the insight[^mankiw-018].

- **1817 *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation*** developed the principle of comparative advantage as known today[^mankiw-018].
- **Canonical example:** two goods (wine and cloth) and two countries (England and Portugal); Ricardo showed both can gain by opening trade and specializing based on comparative advantage[^mankiw-018].
- **Foundational status:** the theory is the starting point of modern international economics[^mankiw-018].
- **Applied politics:** Ricardo's defense was not merely academic; as a member of the British Parliament he opposed the Corn Laws, which restricted the import of grain[^mankiw-018].

## Durability of the free-trade consensus

The conclusions of Smith and Ricardo on gains from trade have held up well over time[^mankiw-018]:

- Although economists often disagree on policy questions, they are united in support of free trade[^mankiw-018].
- The central argument for free trade has not changed much in the past two centuries[^mankiw-018].
- Even though economics has broadened scope and refined theories since Smith and Ricardo, economists' opposition to trade restrictions is still based largely on the principle of comparative advantage[^mankiw-018].

## Applications of comparative advantage

The principle explains interdependence and gains from trade, and because interdependence is so prevalent in the modern world, it has many applications; Mankiw introduces two examples, one fanciful and one of great practical importance[^mankiw-018].

### Should Serena Williams mow her own lawn?

Used to show opportunity cost determines comparative advantage even when one person is more productive at everything[^mankiw-018]:

- Serena Williams — one of the most talented tennis players, can hit ball with speed/accuracy most cannot, spends time running on grass at Wimbledon — imagined to be able to mow her lawn faster than anyone else[^mankiw-018].
- **Technology and alternatives given:** Serena can mow in 2 hours; in those same 2 hours she could film a television commercial and earn $30,000. Forrest Gump, the boy next door, can mow Serena's lawn in 4 hours; in those same 4 hours he could work at McDonald's and earn $50[^mankiw-018].

**Implications (directly computable from given numbers, source text truncated after $50):**

- Absolute advantage in mowing belongs to Serena (2h < 4h)[^mankiw-018].
- Opportunity cost of one mowing: Serena = $30,000 of forgone commercial income; Forrest = $50 of forgone wages[^mankiw-018].
- Therefore Forrest has far lower opportunity cost in mowing and thus comparative advantage in lawn mowing, while Serena has comparative advantage in commercial/filming/tennis[^mankiw-018].
- Just because Serena *can* mow faster does not mean she *should*; specialization and trade (hiring Forrest) leaves both better off — the same logic as Frank and Ruby though the source's concluding sentence is not present in the truncated capture[^mankiw-018].

> Synthesis note: the final inference that Serena should hire Forrest follows necessarily from the opportunity-cost comparison above and the chapter's stated purpose of using opportunity cost/comparative advantage to answer the question; no additional source numbers are invented.

## Key Definitions

| Term | Meaning as used |
|---|---|
| gains from trade | increase in consumption/ welfare from specialization and exchange rather than self-sufficiency[^mankiw-018] |
| comparative advantage (Ricardian) | ability to produce at lower opportunity cost; basis for mutually beneficial specialization as formalized by Ricardo[^mankiw-018] |
| Corn Laws | British laws restricting import of grain, opposed by Ricardo in Parliament[^mankiw-018] |
| opportunity cost | value of forgone alternative (e.g., $30,000 commercial vs $50 wages for 2–4 hours)[^mankiw-018] |
