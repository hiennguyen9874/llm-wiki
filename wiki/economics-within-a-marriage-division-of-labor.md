# Economics within a Marriage — Increasing Marginal Cost, Comparative Advantage, and Household Division of Labor

Household chores should be divided not by who is best overall, by equal counts, or by who likes what, but by equating marginal effectiveness across partners — assigning enough tasks to the less-skilled partner that the last task each does is done equally well — and by assigning each task to whoever has comparative advantage, not absolute advantage; specialization then improves further through learning by doing[^mankiw-019].

## Why chore division is contentious

Housework ranks with commuting at the bottom of happiness surveys, so allocating chores prompts tense discussion or fighting[^mankiw-019]. If partners have different strengths (one better at shopping, the other at laundry) assignment is easy, but often one person is better at everything — often the woman — raising the question whether she should do everything[^mankiw-019].

## Increasing marginal cost: why the best should not do everything

The principle at work is increasing marginal cost — people get worse when they are tired[^mankiw-019].

- **Employee illustration:** A good employee at 2 a.m. after a 17-hour workday is likely worse than a not-so-good employee at 9 a.m. after full sleep, so giving at least a few tasks to the worse employee is efficient[^mankiw-019].
- **Household extension:** Anyone doing laundry at 4 a.m. is likely to put red towels in with white T-shirts — fatigue raises the cost of the marginal task[^mankiw-019].
- **How much splitting depends on how fast skills decay:** the faster effectiveness falls with fatigue, the more tasks should be shifted to the less-skilled partner[^mankiw-019].

## Efficient allocation: equalize effectiveness on the marginal task

To optimize family efficiency — every economist's ultimate goal — equalize effectiveness on the final task each person is doing[^mankiw-019].

- **Example:** Partner does dishes, mows lawn, and makes the grocery list (3 tasks) while you do cooking, laundry, shopping, cleaning, and paying bills (5 tasks). This looks imbalanced, but by his third task he is wearing thin and nodding off, just able to figure out how much milk is needed, while you on your fifth task (paying bills) are equally effective. The marginal effectiveness is equalized[^mankiw-019].
- **If forced to 4-and-4:** making the already-exhausted partner also do cleaning while you are still doing fine would leave the house a disaster[^mankiw-019].
- **Implication:** the system may mean one person does more tasks, but it is unlikely to result in one person doing everything; the exact split turns on the slope of the marginal-cost increase[^mankiw-019].

## Comparative advantage — not absolute advantage or preference — decides who does what

Once splitting is accepted, the assignment rule is comparative advantage[^mankiw-019].

Rejected alternatives[^mankiw-019]:

- Random assignment
- Each person does some of everything
- Divide by which tasks people like best — which would leave no one assigned to clean the bathroom

**Illustrative model:** Finland is better than Sweden at both reindeer hats and snowshoes but much better at hats and only a little better at snowshoes; world production is maximized when Finland makes hats and Sweden makes snowshoes. Finland has absolute advantage in both but comparative advantage only in hats[^mankiw-019].

Applied to the household: it does not matter that you have absolute advantage in everything. If you are much better at laundry and only a little better at cleaning the toilet, you should do laundry and your spouse should get the scrub brush — just explain it is efficient[^mankiw-019].

> **Absolute advantage** is lower input time; **comparative advantage** is lower opportunity cost — the guideline for trading tasks in a house[^mankiw-019].

Serena Williams / Forrest Gump figures are repeated in this file's OCR as a numerical illustration: Serena mows in 2 hours with opportunity cost $30,000 (commercial), Forrest in 4 hours with opportunity cost $50 (McDonald's wages); Forrest has comparative advantage in mowing despite absolute disadvantage, so Serena should hire Forrest if the price is >$50 and <$30,000[^mankiw-019].

## The Oster household application

Before her daughter was born, Emily Oster (economist, University of Chicago at the time, later Brown professor, writing in Slate November 21, 2012) both cooked and did dishes and was a lot better at both than her husband — his repertoire was eggs and chili, and his dishwasher loading was one pot and eight forks run as full[^mankiw-019].

After more to do and less time with a child, reassignments were needed despite the continued absolute advantage[^mankiw-019]. Using comparative advantage: other than grilling (acknowledged husband domain), Oster was much better at cooking and only moderately better at dishes, so the husband got clean-up after meals[^mankiw-019].

## Learning by doing

A second principle reinforced the specialization: as people do a task they improve[^mankiw-019]. Eighteen months into the new arrangement the dishwasher was almost a work of art — neat rows, top-rack-only screening — and Oster was forbidden from getting near it for fear she would ruin it[^mankiw-019].

## Fairness, time-use evidence, and rejected appeals

The source notes the alternative appeals Oster *could* have made but did not rely on for the efficiency argument[^mankiw-019]:

- Fairness: each does half
- Feminism / rectifying imbalance: time-use data show women do about 44 minutes more housework than men (2 hours 11 minutes versus 1 hour 27 minutes); men outwork women only in lawn and exterior maintenance[^mankiw-019]
- Equal-parenting signaling (Free To Be You and Me) that housework is fun if done together
- Passive aggression (smashing pans while sighing loudly)

The economic solution is presented as more effective than these norms-based appeals, though distributional concerns remain outside the efficiency framing[^mankiw-019].

## Contradictions

No internal contradictions.
