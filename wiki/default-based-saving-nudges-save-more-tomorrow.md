# Default-Based Saving Nudges and Save More Tomorrow

The source presents low household saving as partly a behavioral-design problem rather than solely an interest-rate or tax-incentive problem. Save More Tomorrow uses advance commitment and a saving-plan default to counter present bias and procrastination: people can defer the consumption sacrifice and inaction selects saving rather than non-saving.[^blanchard-041]

## Behavioral mechanisms and design

- **Hyperbolic discounting:** a person strongly prefers consumption now to the same consumption next week, yet is relatively indifferent between two equally delayed choices. This present bias can lead to undersaving from the person's own perspective.[^blanchard-041]
- **Procrastination:** decisions about future saving are deferred, so an opt-in plan can leave intended saving unrealized.[^blanchard-041]
- **Precommitment:** participants choose in advance to increase saving later, avoiding an immediate reduction in consumption.[^blanchard-041]
- **Default enrollment:** the saving option, rather than no saving, is the default; inaction therefore implements the saving choice.[^blanchard-041]

The source reports that the 2006 Pension Protection Act encouraged employers to adopt the program's core principles and quotes a Benartzi website estimate that Save More Tomorrow had helped about 15 million Americans significantly increase saving. This is a reported program claim, not an independently verified causal estimate or a current participation count.[^blanchard-041]

## Relationship to macro saving

A nudge can change household saving behavior, but its effect on **national** saving also depends on fiscal offsets and other responses. The source's capital-accumulation framework links a sustained increase in total saving to a higher long-run output level, while not implying permanently faster growth.[^blanchard-041]
