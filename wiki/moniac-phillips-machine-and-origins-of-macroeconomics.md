# Alban William Phillips, the MONIAC hydraulic computer, and the Great Depression origins of macroeconomics

Harford opens with Bill Phillips's 1949 demonstration at the Lionel Robbins Seminar at the London School of Economics — a hydraulic model of the economy that stunned Robbins, Hayek, Hicks, Lewis, Meade and others — to argue that macroeconomics was born to fix a broken machine and that understanding its workings requires a system-level lens distinct from microeconomics[^harford-intro].

## The 1949 demonstration

- Late 1949, Robbins Seminar: Meade persuaded Robbins to invite mature student Alban William Phillips, a small, shy, incessantly smoking New Zealander who had just failed to get honours in sociology[^harford-intro].
- Phillips brought a Rube Goldberg–like apparatus of half a dozen transparent plastic tanks linked by pipes, dams and sluice gates, filled with water dyed pink with cochineal[^harford-intro].

## Bill Phillips — engineer, tinkerer, survivor

- **Family workshop:** Born 1914 on a dairy farm in Te Rehunga; father Harold fitted flush toilet, waterwheel generator and electric light, and a bedroom winch that cut power for "lights out"; mother Edith (schoolteacher) encouraged study; Harold taught children to build crystal radios and zoetropes[^harford-intro].
- **Teen repairman:** Bored by a nine-mile cycle to secondary school, at 14 repaired a broken truck adults deemed beyond repair and drove classmates to school[^harford-intro].
- **Depression block:** Passed every exam but 1929 Wall Street crash → collapse in agricultural prices meant parents could not fund university; became apprentice electrician at a hydroelectric power station instead[^harford-intro].
- **Swashbuckling gap years (1935–46):** Gold mine, crocodile hunting, self-taught violin busking, Trans-Siberian Railway, arrest by Japanese as suspected spy, then LSE enrolment interrupted by war[^harford-intro]. *Wall Street Journal* later called Steve Levitt "Indiana Jones of economics" — Harford argues the label fits Phillips better[^harford-intro].
- **Wartime engineering and bravery:** As RAF engineer upgraded obsolete aircraft defending Singapore; on the last convoy out aboard the refrigerated cargo ship *Empire Star* (designed for 23, packed with >2,000), improvised a mounting for a machine gun and fended off Japanese air attack for hours despite bomb strikes — awarded MBE[^harford-intro].
- **POW ingenuity:** >3 years in Japanese camp; noted small men survived while taller starved, himself <100 lb at liberation; built concealed radios (one tiny enough for a shoe heel, discovery meant torture/death) and nightly immersion heaters causing camp lights to flicker, mystifying guards[^harford-intro].
- **Death camp, summer 1945:** Transferred with thousands to a camp with machine guns mounted inward and forced to dig own mass graves; with writer Laurens van der Post (*The Night of the New Moon*) and officer Donaldson, broke into commander's office for radio parts and heard of Hiroshima — war's end at hand[^harford-intro].

## The MONIAC (Monetary National Income Analog Computer)

- **Construction:** After war resumed LSE sociology, skipped lectures to build hydraulics in landlady's Croydon garage, modelling equations lecturers scribbled on blackboards; scavenged differential equations by correspondence, hydraulic engineering from apprenticeship, and parts including a Lancaster bomber pump and window tanks[^harford-intro].
- **Patronage:** Lecturer James Meade championed the plumbing-as-calculus proposal and secured the Robbins Seminar slot; Phillips started the Lancaster pump — screeching "like a kitchen blender" — and pink water flowed top to bottom[^harford-intro].
- **What it computed:** First computer model of a national economy; nine differential equations solved simultaneously in minutes — impossible by hand (then done by rooms of women "computers" with mechanical calculators), years before digital computers matched it[^harford-intro]. Calibrated to British economy (savings propensity, supply/demand price responses); accurate to ~2%, exceeding then-needed statistical precision[^harford-intro].
- **Physical form (Mark II):** ~7 ft tall, 4–5 ft wide; central transparent column with weirs/sluice gates to side chambers; sections marked INCOME AFTER TAXES, CONSUMPTION EXPENDITURE, DOMESTIC EXPENDITURE; small fish-tank sized INVESTMENT FUNDS compartment; curved flesh-coloured dam marked LIQUIDITY PREFERENCE FUNCTION; top spools of paper with four float-linked pens tracing like seismographs; washing-machine-like pipes behind; bottom NATIONAL INCOME tank recirculating to top[^harford-intro].
- **Carving equations:** Each equation physically cut as a shaped slot in small plexiglass squares in a white frame with thermometer scale; peg on brass rails linked to float and sluice gate — rising water moves peg sideways per slot shape to open/close gate[^harford-intro].
- **Economics embodied:** Produced cycles or turbulence when moving between steady states after a shock — ahead of theorists who ignored such dynamics — and allowed floating exchange rates while world was on pegged/gold systems[^harford-intro].
- **Legacy:** Within five minutes the room buzzed; LSE gave Phillips a job, professor within decade despite no honours degree or economics qualification[^harford-intro]. Duplicates of MONIAC Mark II sold to Cambridge, Harvard, developing-country governments and Ford Motor Company; celebrated in *Punch* and later Terry Pratchett's *Making Money*[^harford-intro]. Meade linked two MONIACs (export pipe of one to import pipe of other) to model US–UK trade, inviting student pairs to play Fed chair vs. Chancellor — among them future Fed chair Paul Volcker[^harford-intro]. Surviving machines: Cambridge (refurbished to working order by engineering professor Allan McRobie), Reserve Bank of New Zealand display, and LSE's until 1992 then Science Museum London facing Babbage's Difference Engine[^harford-intro].

## The Great Depression as birth of macroeconomics

- **Scale:** US industrial production fell by almost half, income per head by a third, unemployment averaged 25% through 1930s; US tariffs worsened exporters' distress, mass unemployment in Germany aided Hitler's rise; Depression reached Te Rehunga via farm prices[^harford-intro].
- **New questions:** Economists asked what was happening economy-wide, why, and what could be done, taking new measurements, theories and policies — the emergence of macroeconomics as distinct from sum-of-parts micro[^harford-intro].
- **Pioneers:** John Maynard Keynes (*The Economic Consequences of the Peace*, 1920s UK policy critic), Simon Kuznets (first US national income calculation), James Meade (abandoned classics for economics in late 1920s, later wartime economic governance) — shared genius and determination to act[^harford-intro].
- **Repair metaphor:** Keynes called Depression "magneto trouble" — a technical fault that can halt the whole machine but be fixed with right tools/understanding; Harford parallels 14-year-old Phillips fixing the truck others abandoned — understand and mend it[^harford-intro].

## Micro lens versus macro lens

Harford contrasts two views of a Jobcentre Plus office on a rainy day, with typo-filled touch-screen ads for "Security Officer £7.88", "Weekend Manager £7.50", "Retail Town Supervisor exceeds national mini"[^harford-intro]:

- **Micro:** Incentives, prices, productivity — worth of harassed young mother to employer vs. £7.50/hr net of childcare/benefit loss; hoodied teenager's human-capital investment; rationality and nudges (randomized trial in Loughton near London shows yes)[^harford-intro].
- **Macro:** Bird's-eye — recession, falling average wages, rising unemployment as system-wide shocks: supply capacity (oil price, bank lending) vs. demand shortfall (willingness to spend on Main Street), their causes and remedies; abstract but vital to millions[^harford-intro]. Macro asks whether economy is demand-constrained or supply-constrained as a whole[^harford-intro].

## Why the book matters now

- Water flowing around MONIAC analogizes financial flows/reservoirs — consumption, government spending, investment, imports — dammed, redirected or siphoned by citizens and policymakers via rates, tax, money creation at Bank of England / Federal Reserve[^harford-intro].
- Phillips revolutionized economics but did not permanently solve stabilization; the 2007 crisis (less severe/long than Great Depression but comparable in hunger for action) shows need for Phillips-like attitude to truck — fixable if understood — revived by Harford as a practical poke under the hood[^harford-intro].
- Harford disclaims strident calls, blame lists, or personal/business how-to (as in prior books); quantitative easing is as useless for daily life as quantum physics, and everyday household/firm balancing is a poor guide to running a national economy[^harford-intro]. The task: learn how system works, ask what can make it work better — with reader in lead role[^harford-intro].
