# Six Debates over Macroeconomic Policy — Should Policymakers Try to Stabilize the Economy?

Mankiw's six classic macroeconomic policy debates ask whether policymakers should actively smooth the business cycle and how, contrasting Keynesian stabilizer views with cautionary views about lags, forecasting limits, and credibility — illustrated by the 2001 Bush tax cuts versus the 2009 Obama $800bn stimulus, the zero lower bound, and the Volcker disinflation[^mankiw-068][^mankiw-069].

## 23 — Framing: six unsettled questions

Mankiw presents six classic questions as the culmination of short-run tools (aggregate demand / aggregate supply, monetary and fiscal shifts of AD): whether to stabilize, how to fight recessions (spending vs tax cuts), whether to target rules vs discretion, and other perennial disputes that continue because theory and evidence leave them unsettled[^mankiw-068].

## 23-1 Should monetary and fiscal policymakers try to stabilize the economy?

Even if policymakers *can* influence short-run fluctuations by shifting AD, should they?[^mankiw-068]

### 23-1a Pro: Policymakers should try to stabilize

- **Economies fluctuate when left alone.** Example: households and firms become pessimistic → cut spending → aggregate demand falls → production falls → firms lay off workers, unemployment rises, Real GDP and incomes fall → rising unemployment and falling income confirm the initial pessimism (self-reinforcing downturn)[^mankiw-068].
- **Recessions are sheer waste with no social benefit.** Workers who lose jobs to deficient AD would rather be working; owners of idle factories would rather be producing and selling at a profit[^mankiw-068].
- **Society need not suffer booms and busts.** Macroeconomic theory shows how to reduce severity by *leaning against the wind*: stabilize AD and thereby production and employment[^mankiw-068].
- **Prescription is symmetric:**
  - When AD is inadequate for full employment: boost government spending, cut taxes, expand money supply[^mankiw-068].
  - When AD is excessive and risks inflation: cut government spending, raise taxes, reduce money supply[^mankiw-068].
- **Result:** more stable economy benefits everyone — the best use of macroeconomic theory[^mankiw-068].

### 23-1b Con: Policymakers should not try to stabilize

Theory may allow stabilization, but practice confronts substantial obstacles[^mankiw-068].

**1. Long lags:**
- **Monetary policy lag (~6 months):** works primarily by changing interest rates → spending, particularly residential and business investment; many households and firms set spending plans in advance, so rate changes take time to alter AD; many studies find little effect until about 6 months after the change[^mankiw-068].
- **Fiscal policy lag (months to years):** governed by long political process — bill through congressional committees, passage by House and Senate, presidential signature; proposing, passing, and implementing a major change can take years[^mankiw-068].

**2. Need to forecast, but forecasting is highly imprecise:**
- Because of lags, policymakers must target conditions likely to prevail *when actions take effect*, not today[^mankiw-068].
- Forecasting is imprecise because macroeconomics is a primitive science and shocks causing fluctuations are intrinsically unpredictable[^mankiw-068].
- Policymakers must rely on educated guesses about future conditions[^mankiw-068].

**3. Risk of destabilization:**
- Conditions can change between the time action begins and when it takes effect; well-intended stabilization can inadvertently *exacerbate* fluctuations[^mankiw-068].
- Some economists trace many major historical fluctuations, including the **Great Depression of the 1930s**, to destabilizing policy actions[^mankiw-068].

**4. Medical analogy — 'first, do no harm':**
- Like a doctor facing an uncertain diagnosis who should let the body's natural restorative powers work rather than intervene without reliable knowledge and risk harm, the economy has self-correcting tendencies[^mankiw-068].
- Eliminating all fluctuations is desirable but not realistic given limits of macroeconomic knowledge and unpredictability of world events; policymakers should refrain from frequent monetary/fiscal intervention and be content if they do no harm[^mankiw-068].

> Quick check from source: *Explain why monetary and fiscal policies work with a lag. Why do these lags matter in the choice between active and passive policy?* — Monetary lag via interest→investment with advance planning (~6 months); fiscal lag via political process; lags matter because they force forward-looking guesses with poor forecasts, risking mistimed, pro-cyclical action that destabilizes rather than stabilizes[^mankiw-068].

## 23-2 Should the government fight recessions with spending hikes rather than tax cuts?

Illustrated by the contrast between George W. Bush (2001 recession → tax-rate cuts) and Barack Obama (2009 Great Recession → stimulus with some tax cuts but substantial spending increases)[^mankiw-069].

### 23-2a Pro: Fight recessions with spending hikes (Keynesian view)

- **Keynes's diagnosis:** Since *The General Theory* (1936) in the Great Depression, fundamental problem in recessions is inadequate aggregate demand; firms unable to sell enough reduce production and employment; restoring AD to full-employment level is key[^mankiw-069].
- **Monetary policy is first line:** increasing money supply → lower interest rates → cheaper borrowing for factories/housing → investment adds to AD → restores production/employment[^mankiw-069].
- **Fiscal policy is additional tool:** tax cuts raise disposable income → consumption; government purchases add directly to AD; both can have multiplier effects (higher AD → higher incomes → more consumption → further AD)[^mankiw-069].
- **Zero lower bound makes fiscal policy particularly useful:** In 2008–09 Fed cut target to about zero; cannot go below zero because people would hold cash rather than lend at negative rate; once at zero Fed loses its most powerful tool, so fiscal policy must prop up AD[^mankiw-069].
- **Traditional Keynesian potency ranking:** A dollar of tax cut may be partly saved (especially if viewed temporary) and that saved portion does not add to AD; a dollar of government purchases immediately and fully adds to AD, so spending is more potent[^mankiw-069].
- **Obama-administration model simulations (2009):** each $1 of tax cuts raises GDP by **$0.99**, each $1 of government purchases raises GDP by **$1.59** — bigger "bang for the buck" from spending[^mankiw-069].
- **Three spending focuses in 2009:**
  1. *Shovel-ready* public works (highway/bridge repairs that could begin immediately, putting unemployed back to work)[^mankiw-069].
  2. Federal aid to state/local governments (many constitutionally required to balance budgets; falling revenues would otherwise force layoffs of teachers/police)[^mankiw-069].
  3. Increased unemployment-insurance payments to jobless (financially stretched recipients thought likely to spend rather than save, contributing more to AD than tax cuts)[^mankiw-069].
- **Model prediction:** $800bn stimulus would create or save >3 million jobs by end of second year[^mankiw-069].
- **Ex post assessment:** One run at history so counterfactual unobservable, but while 2008–09 was severe it could have been worse: Great Depression real GDP −27% unemployment 25% vs Great Recession −4% and 10% — Great Recession did not approach Great Depression magnitude[^mankiw-069].

> Quick check in source: *According to traditional Keynesian analysis, which has a larger impact on GDP—a dollar of tax cuts or a dollar of additional government spending? Why?* — Spending, because tax cuts are partly saved while purchases add fully and directly to AD and have larger multiplier ($1.59 vs $0.99 in Obama model)[^mankiw-069].

### 23-2b Con: Fight recessions with tax cuts (supply-side and practical view)

- **Tradition of tax-cut stimulus:** Kennedy proposed, Johnson passed 1964; Reagan 1981 — both soon followed by robust growth[^mankiw-069].
- **Tax cuts affect both AD and AS:**
  - AD: raise disposable income (Keynesian) and also alter incentives — e.g., expanded investment tax credit induces investment spending (most volatile GDP component, key to ending recessions); well-designed tax policy can target investment specifically[^mankiw-069].
  - AS: lower marginal rates → workers keep higher fraction of income → unemployed greater incentive to search, employed to work longer hours → AS expands, so output can rise without inflationary pressure[^mankiw-069].
- **Problems with spending hikes:**
  1. **Future-tax anticipation:** consumers understand higher spending + borrowing likely → higher future taxes → cut spending today; future taxes also create deadweight losses; businesses expecting more distorted future economy reduce profit expectations and cut investment today; multipliers may be smaller than conventionally believed[^mankiw-069].
  2. **Wise-and-quick dilemma:** large projects require years of planning to weigh costs/benefits; but need for AD is immediate when unemployment soars; spending fast risks buying things of little value; being careful risks missing the timing window[^mankiw-069].
  3. **Centralization vs decentralization:** tax cuts decentralize decisions to households/firms who spend on what they value/expect profitable; government spending fast under political pressures may build "bridges to nowhere" — employing workers but creating little lasting value and leaving future taxpayers with debt; short-run AD benefits may not compensate long-run costs[^mankiw-069].

## 23-3 Should monetary policy be made by rule rather than by discretion?

In the U.S. the Federal Open Market Committee meets about every 6 weeks to evaluate the economy and set short-term interest rates, adjusting money supply to hit target until next meeting, operating with almost complete discretion — 1977 amendment to 1913 Federal Reserve Act gives only vague mandate: maintain long-run growth of monetary and credit aggregates commensurate with economy's long-run potential to promote maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term rates, without weights or method[^mankiw-069].

### 23-3a Pro: Monetary policy should be made by rule

- **Problem 1 — incompetence and abuse of power:** Like police given strict guidelines because power is dangerous, central bankers with undisciplined discretion may abuse it[^mankiw-069].
  - *Political business cycle:* central bankers sympathetic to incumbent may pursue expansionary policy before election to stimulate production/employment knowing inflation arrives after election; fluctuations reflecting electoral calendar; example President Nixon pressuring Chairman Arthur Burns before 1972 election to pursue more expansionary policy for reelection[^mankiw-069].
- **Problem 2 — inflation bias from time inconsistency:** central bankers announce zero-inflation goal knowing no long-run inflation-unemployment trade-off, but once public forms expectations, short-run trade-off tempts reneging to lower unemployment; discrepancy between announcements and actions is *time inconsistency of policy*; public skepticism → always expect more inflation than claimed → higher expected inflation shifts short-run Phillips curve upward, worsening trade-off[^mankiw-069].
- **Rule solution:** commit central bank to a rule, e.g., Congress requires Fed to increase money supply by exactly 3% per year (3% ≈ average real GDP growth + money-demand growth with GDP, rate consistent with long-run price stability); eliminates incompetence/abuse and political cycle and time inconsistency; people would believe low-inflation announcement because legally required; more favorable short-run trade-off[^mankiw-069].
- **Other possible rules:** more active rule requiring Fed to increase monetary growth by 1 percentage point for each percentage point unemployment rises above natural rate; any commitment to some rule yields advantages by limiting incompetence, abuse, and time inconsistency[^mankiw-069].

### 23-3b Con: Monetary policy should not be made by rule

- **Advantage of discretion — flexibility for unforeseeable shocks:** Fed must confront contingencies no designer could foresee — 1930s bank failures, 1970s oil skyrocketing, October 1987 stock market −22% in one day, 2007–09 house-price collapse/foreclosures/financial-system problems; must decide response; specifying in advance the right response to all contingencies is impossible; better to appoint good people and give freedom[^mankiw-069].
- **Alleged problems are largely hypothetical:**
  - *Political business cycle:* unclear practical importance; Nixon may have pressured Burns in 1972 but rates rose significantly during election year (unclear success); opposite example: Carter appointed Volcker 1979, Volcker switched to contractionary policy October 1979 to fight inherited inflation → recession → Carter popularity decline → defeat by Reagan November 1980; Volcker acted in national interest over presidential ally interest[^mankiw-069].
  - *Time inconsistency:* also unclear; most people are skeptical but credibility can be earned over time by backing words with actions; 1990s–2000s Fed achieved and maintained low inflation despite temptation to exploit short-run trade-off, showing low inflation does not require rule commitment[^mankiw-069].
- **Inflation-targeting compromise (FYI box):** Many central banks adopted inflation targeting past decades — announcing inflation intention or law specifying goal (often range like 1–3% rather than single number), allowing temporary adjustment for shocks like oil; leaves fair discretion but constrains it, increases transparency/accountability; not ironclad rule; Fed slow but in January 2012 FOMC explicitly stated longer-run goal 2% as measured by annual change in personal consumption expenditures price index, to anchor expectations and promote price stability, moderate long-term rates, and maximum employment[^mankiw-069].
- **Rule specification problem:** Despite research on costs/benefits of alternative rules, no consensus on what good rule would be; until consensus, society has little choice but to give central bankers discretion[^mankiw-069].

Why might it be worse?* — e.g., 3% money growth or unemployment-feedback rule; better: prevents abuse, political cycle, time inconsistency, improves credibility and trade-off; worse: loses flexibility for unforeseen shocks, may be poorly specified and inappropriate for novel circumstances[^mankiw-069].

## 23-4 Should the central bank aim for zero inflation?

Two Ten Principles meet: prices rise when government prints too much money, but society faces short-run inflation-unemployment trade-off — how much inflation should central bank tolerate?[^mankiw-069]

### 23-4a Pro: Aim for zero inflation

- **Six costs of inflation (from Ch.17):** shoeleather costs (reduced money holdings), menu costs (frequent price adjustment), increased variability of relative prices, unintended tax-liability changes from nonindexation, confusion/inconvenience from changing unit of account, arbitrary redistributions of wealth via dollar-denominated debts[^mankiw-069].
- **Moderate-inflation assessment disputed:** Some say costs small at 3% as in 1990s–2000s U.S.; others say substantial even at moderate; public clearly dislikes inflation — polls identify it as leading problem when it heats up[^mankiw-069].
- **Benefits vs costs of disinflation:** Reducing inflation usually requires temporary high unemployment/low output (short-run Phillips curve), but trade-off only temporary; once people understand zero-inflation aim, expectations fall and trade-off improves; no long-run inflation-unemployment trade-off[^mankiw-069].
- **Temporary costs, permanent benefits:** Once recession ends, benefits persist; farsighted policymakers should incur temporary costs; Volcker early 1980s tightened to cut inflation ~10% in 1980 to ~4% in 1983; unemployment reached highest since Great Depression in 1982 but economy recovered leaving low-inflation legacy; Volcker now considered hero[^mankiw-069].
- **Credibility can reduce costs:** Credible announced commitment to zero can directly shift expectations, improving short-run trade-off and lowering cost; Congress could help by making price stability Fed's primary goal[^mankiw-069].
- **Zero as natural focal point:** 3% is arbitrary — if events push to 4–5% why not raise target? Nothing special about 3; zero is only rate where Fed can claim price stability and full elimination of inflation costs[^mankiw-069].

### 23-4b Con: Do not aim for zero inflation

- **Benefits small vs costs large:** Sacrifice ratio estimates: reducing inflation by 1 point requires giving up ~5% of one year's output; 4%→0% requires 20% of a year's output loss; unclear public would/should pay that for 4% inflation[^mankiw-069].
- **Costs inequitable and concentrated:** Lost income not spread proportionally — fall concentrated on workers who lose jobs, often least skilled/experienced least able to afford it[^mankiw-069].
- **Professional consensus lacking:** Shoe leather, menu, etc. costs do not seem great at moderate rates; public dislike may reflect inflation fallacy (inflation erodes living standards) — economists note living standards depend on productivity, not monetary policy; nominal incomes rise with prices so reducing inflation would not raise real incomes faster[^mankiw-069].
- **Many costs can be reduced without reducing inflation:** rewrite tax laws to index for inflation; issue indexed government bonds (Clinton administration did 1997) insulating holders and encouraging private indexation, reducing arbitrary creditor-debtor redistributions[^mankiw-069].
- **Painless disinflation is hard:** Almost always involves high unemployment/low output despite theory; believing credibility quickly achievable is risky[^mankiw-069].
- **Hysteresis/scarring:** Disinflationary recession can leave permanent scars: firms cut plant/equipment investment sharply (most volatile GDP component); smaller capital stock thereafter reduces productivity/incomes/living standards below otherwise; unemployed workers lose skills, permanently reducing value[^mankiw-069].
- **Greasing the wheels:** A little inflation may be good — workers resist nominal wage cuts, so real-wage fall easier with rising price level; inflation makes real wages adjust more easily to labor-market changes[^mankiw-069].

### 23-4c In the News: On Kiwis and Currencies — The Goal of 2% Inflation, Rethought (Neil Irwin, NYT Dec 21, 2014) — deeper history of the 2% target[^mankiw-070]

- **New Zealand's accidental invention (1989):** Don Brash, ex-managing director of the NZ Kiwifruit Authority (kiwi farmer/banker), took over the Reserve Bank in 1988 after two rough decades of high inflation and disappointing growth; his uncle had sold an apple orchard in 1971 for government bonds and seen inflation wipe out 90% of life savings by maturity — illustrating predecessor's incomplete, un-codified disinflation progress[^mankiw-070].
- **Reserve Bank Act of 1989:** Finance minister and bank head must agree a formal inflation target, bank gets political independence to guide rates to hit it, head can be dismissed for failure; finance minister David Caygill said inflation targeting wasn't the main point — independence from politics was[^mankiw-070].
- **Contentious politics:** Labor unions feared higher unemployment, some business interests agreed; majority leaders brushed off concerns, helped by a key opponent hospitalized and Christmas approaching — quick unanimous parliamentary vote eager to get home[^mankiw-070].
- **How the number was chosen:** Predecessor Roger Douglas had plucked "around zero to 1 percent" almost as a chance remark in a 1988 TV interview to influence expectations; Brash and Caygill expanded slightly for maneuver room to **zero to 2 percent**[^mankiw-070].
- **Self-fulfilling success and diffusion:** At the time announcing a target was almost radical (central bankers prized mystique); by announcing 0–2% and granting independence, NZ made it reality — wage/price setters assumed ~2%, so wages and prices rose more slowly; inflation 7.6% end-1989 → 2% end-1991; Brash campaigned at Jackson Hole, Wyoming; Canada adopted 2% in 1991, then Sweden, Britain, and thereafter all advanced nations emulated in one form or another — 2% became virtually an economic religion[^mankiw-070].
- **Zero vs 2% intellectual history:** Zero advocated by Paul Volcker (dollar should buy same in a decade) and Alan Greenspan (inflation low enough not to factor into business decisions) implying 0–1% given measurement error; alternate view championed inside Fed in 1990s by Governor Janet Yellen[^mankiw-070].
- **Yellen's two arguments for positive inflation (July 1996 closed-door FOMC):**
  1. *Greasing the wheels:* firms rarely cut nominal pay even in severe downturns — they cut hours, lay off, or leave vacancies; this raises unemployment instead of lowering wages; with a bit of inflation employers can hold nominal pay steady (e.g., $20/hour) while real pay falls ~2% a year, reducing layoffs[^mankiw-070].
  2. *Negative real rates at the zero lower bound:* nominal rates never fall below zero (lenders would hold cash); if inflation is zero real can never be negative; if inflation is positive, cutting nominal below inflation produces negative real rates; "A little inflation permits real interest rates to become negative on the rare occasions when required to counter a recession. This could be important."[^mankiw-070]
- **Vindication and renewed doubt:** Late-1990s Japan stuck in deflation even at zero rates; U.S. 2001 mild recession Fed cut to 1%; 2007–09 Great Recession forced rates to zero globally — leading Blanchard (IMF chief economist, 2010 paper with co-authors) to ask: "Should policy makers therefore aim for a higher target... are the net costs of inflation much higher at, say, 4 percent than at 2 percent?"[^mankiw-070].
- **Cost of higher target:** At 2% inflation dollar halves in 35 years, at 4% in 18 years (Rule of 72); 2% transactions need little adjustment; 3–4% in mid-1980s hardly remembered as nightmare; but shifting target could disrupt markets and spike long-term rates and slow growth; current central bankers (Vice Chair Stanley Fischer, Yellen) saw costs as too high, reasserting 2%[^mankiw-070].
- **Blinder's cost-benefit framing (Hard Heads, Soft Hearts, ex vice-chair):** Costs of low and moderate inflation are modest — "more like a bad cold than a cancer"; "As rational individuals, we do not volunteer for a lobotomy to cure a head cold. Yet, as a collectivity, we routinely prescribe the economic equivalent of lobotomy (high unemployment) as a cure for the inflationary cold." Better to learn to live with moderate inflation[^mankiw-070].

> Quick check from source: *Explain the costs and benefits of reducing inflation to zero. Which are temporary and which are permanent?* — Benefits of zero are permanent: elimination of shoeleather, menu, relative-price variability, tax distortion, confusion, and redistribution costs; costs are temporary: recession/depressed output and employment to disinflate, but can leave hysteresis scars; thus trade-off is temporary costs for permanent benefits (pro-zero) vs benefits small and costs large/inequitable plus greasing/maneuver-room value (con-zero)[^mankiw-070].

## 23-5 Should the government balance its budget?

Persistent debate: whenever $G > T$ government covers deficit by issuing debt; Ch.13 showed deficits affect saving, investment, and interest rates; question is how big a problem[^mankiw-070].

### 23-5a Pro: The government should balance its budget

- **Scale:** Federal debt $712 billion (1980) → $13.1 trillion (2015) → ~$41,000 per person[^mankiw-070].
- **Direct burden on future taxpayers:** When debts and accumulated interest come due, future taxpayers must choose higher taxes + less spending to pay, or borrow again to pay old debt+interest; current taxpayers pass the bill to future taxpayers, lowering future living standards[^mankiw-070].
- **Macroeconomic burden:** Deficits are negative public saving → lower national saving (private + public) → higher real interest rates → lower investment → smaller capital stock → lower labor productivity, real wages, and production of goods/services; future generations born into lower-income, higher-tax economy[^mankiw-070].
- **Justifiable exceptions:**
  - *War:* most common historic cause; temporarily high military spending financed by borrowing avoids precipitous tax hikes with large deadweight distortions and is fairer — those fighting secure freedom for future generations too[^mankiw-070].
  - *Temporary downturn/recession:* tax revenue falls automatically (income/payroll levies); balancing would require tax hikes or spending cuts at high unemployment, depressing aggregate demand and magnifying fluctuations[^mankiw-070].
- **Counterexample of unjustifiable:** Debt/GDP 26% (1980) → 50% (1995) with neither major war nor major downturn, because president and Congress found it easier to raise spending than taxes[^mankiw-070].
- **Recent justification with caveat:** Wars in Iraq/Afghanistan and recessions 2001 and 2008–09 perhaps rationalize recent deficits, but must not signal return to earlier unsustainable fiscal policy; as economy recovers to natural unemployment, government should align spending with revenue — versus ongoing deficits, balanced budget means greater national saving, investment, and growth, so future college graduates enter more prosperous economy[^mankiw-070].

### 23-5b Con: The government should not balance its budget

- **Burden is often exaggerated:** $41,000 per person is ~2% of a $2 million lifetime earnings ($50k × 40 years)[^mankiw-070].
- **Deficit is one piece of fiscal picture:** Budget deficit/surplus should be considered with other policies that also redistribute across generations; focusing only on deficit is misleading[^mankiw-070].
- **Cutting spending can hurt future more than debt:** E.g., reducing deficit by cutting public investments like education leaves smaller debt but less-educated workforce with lower productivity/incomes; return to schooling is large, so deficit reduction via education cuts could make future worse off[^mankiw-070].
- **Other generational policies matter more:** 1960s–70s Social Security benefit increases financed by payroll taxes on working-age redistributed from young to old without affecting debt — deficit alone understates generational transfer[^mankiw-070].
- **Bequest offset (Ricardian-adjacent):** Forward-looking parents can reverse adverse debt effects by saving and leaving larger bequests to cover future taxes; higher private saving would offset public dissaving, making deficits neutral; some probably do, anyone could; deficits give opportunity to consume at children's expense but do not require it; if debt were truly great problem, some parents would help solve it[^mankiw-070].
- **Debt can grow forever if slower than income:** Like a bank comparing debts to income, burden is debt/income; population growth + technological progress make national income grow; as long as debt grows slower than income, debt/income need not rise; U.S. real output ~3% + 2% inflation = 5% nominal growth → debt can rise 5% per year without raising debt/income; 2015 debt $13.1T ×5% = $655B sustainable deficit ceiling; as long as deficit <$655B policy is sustainable; very large deficits (> $1T 2009–12 driven by extraordinary financial crisis/recession) cannot persist forever, but zero is wrong target — moderate deficits need not force reckoning or collapse[^mankiw-070].

> Quick check from source: *Explain how reducing a government budget deficit makes future generations better off. What fiscal policy might improve the lives of future generations more than reducing a deficit?* — Lower deficit → higher national saving → lower rates → more capital → higher productivity/wages/incomes and lower future taxes; but investing in education, infrastructure, or other high-return public capital could improve future welfare more even if deficit falls less[^mankiw-070].

## 23-6 Should the tax laws be reformed to encourage saving?

Standard of living depends on ability to produce goods/services (Ch.1); productive capability largely determined by how much society saves and invests — last debate is whether to reform taxes to encourage saving[^mankiw-070].

### 23-6a Pro: The tax laws should be reformed to encourage saving

- **Why saving matters:** Higher saving → more resources for plant/equipment → larger capital stock → higher labor productivity, wages, incomes; international data show strong correlation between national saving rates and well-being[^mankiw-070].
- **Incentive principle (Ch.1):** People respond to incentives; if laws make saving attractive, people save higher fraction, leading to more prosperous future[^mankiw-070].
- **How U.S. tax system discourages saving:**
  1. *Tax on interest:* 25-year-old saves $1,000 to retire at 70 at 10% → $72,900 tax-free in 45 years, but at 40% marginal rate after-tax 6% → only $13,800 — an ~80% reduction in benefit[^mankiw-070].
  2. *Double taxation of capital:* Corporation earns profit → pays corporate income tax → if paid as dividends stockholder pays individual income tax second time — substantially reduced return[^mankiw-070].
  3. *Estate tax:* Bequest beyond exemption taxed up to 40%, discouraging direct intergenerational help — odd given concern for future prosperity[^mankiw-070].
  4. *Means-tested benefits:* Welfare/Medicaid reduced for those who past-saved; college financial aid as function of wealth acts like tax on wealth[^mankiw-070].
- **Small step:** Expand eligibility and contribution limits for tax-advantaged retirement accounts — IRA, 401(k), 403(b), profit-sharing — where income+interest not taxed until withdrawal, but penalties before retirement give little incentive for house/college saving[^mankiw-070].
- **Comprehensive approach:** Replace income tax (dollar earned taxed same whether spent or saved) with **consumption tax** — household pays only on what it spends; income saved exempt until later withdrawn and spent; essentially automatic IRA for all saving; would greatly increase incentive to save; IGM panel captured related trade-offs about capital vs labor taxation and prosperity vs redistribution[^mankiw-070].

### 23-6b Con: The tax laws should not be reformed to encourage saving

- **Equity vs efficiency:** High-income households save greater fraction than low-income; any tax change favoring savers tends to favor high-income; retirement accounts lead to less egalitarian society — reducing wealthy burden forces raising poor's burden[^mankiw-070].
- **Effectiveness uncertain:** Theory gives ambiguous prediction — higher return creates *substitution effect* (higher benefit → more saving) and *income effect* (lower need to save to hit target future consumption → less saving); if they approximately cancel, as some studies suggest, saving will not change[^mankiw-070].
- **Alternative path to national saving:** National saving = private + public saving; instead of altering code to encourage private saving, raise public saving by reducing budget deficit (perhaps raising taxes on wealthy) — direct and more equitable way to raise national saving and future prosperity[^mankiw-070].
- **Backfire risk:** Provisions reducing taxation of capital income reduce government revenue → larger deficit (lower public saving); to raise national saving, stimulus to private saving must exceed fall in public saving; if not, so-called saving incentives make matters worse[^mankiw-070].

> Quick check from source: *Give three examples of how our society discourages saving. What are the drawbacks of eliminating these disincentives?* — Examples: interest taxed (80% cut in 45-yr example), double corporate/dividend tax, estate tax, means-tested welfare/Medicaid, wealth-tested college aid; drawbacks: benefits accrue to wealthy (equity), ambiguous net effect on saving due to offsetting substitution/income effects, potential revenue loss widening deficit and lowering national saving[^mankiw-070].

## 23-7 Conclusion

Mankiw notes the six debates illustrate classic macro trade-offs with no easy choice; by clarifying trade-offs (lags, multipliers, credibility, sacrifice ratios, debt burden, saving incentives) economics makes the choice more difficult, not easier; if a politician sounds like offering a free lunch, look for hidden price tag — few if any policies have benefits without costs; economics helps see through rhetoric to be better participant in national debates[^mankiw-070].
