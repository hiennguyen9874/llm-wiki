# Mankiw's Ten Principles of Economics

Economics studies how society manages scarce resources through the combined choices of households and firms; scarcity forces trade-offs at every level — from a household allocating time between studying and work to a society choosing between defense and consumption — and ten linked principles show how individual decision logic aggregates into market interaction and economy-wide outcomes[^mankiw-013].

## How People Make Decisions (Principles 1–4)

### 1. People Face Trade-offs

To get something we like, we give up something else we like — "there ain't no such thing as a free lunch"[^mankiw-013]. Examples:

- **Individual time:** a student allocating hours between economics and psychology, or studying versus napping, working, or watching TV[^mankiw-013].
- **Family budget:** income spent on food, clothing, vacation, or saved for retirement/college — an extra dollar on one means one less on another[^mankiw-013].
- **Society:** "guns versus butter" (defense vs consumer goods) and clean environment versus income — pollution regulation cleans air but raises costs, lowering profits, wages, or raising prices[^mankiw-013].
- **Efficiency vs equality:** *efficiency* = maximum benefits from scarce resources (size of the pie); *equality* = uniform distribution (how slices are divided)[^mankiw-013]. Policies for equality (welfare, unemployment insurance, progressive income tax) often reduce efficiency by weakening work incentives, so a more equal pie is smaller[^mankiw-013]. Recognizing trade-offs does not prescribe the choice but is required for good decisions[^mankiw-013].

### 2. The Cost of Something Is What You Give Up to Get It — Opportunity Cost

Decision makers compare costs and benefits of alternatives, but opportunity cost — what is given up — is not always obvious[^mankiw-013]. College cost is not just tuition, books, room and board: room and board would be paid anyway, and the largest cost is forgone earnings from time not working; college athletes with multi-million-dollar professional opportunities face very high opportunity costs and often forgo college[^mankiw-013].

> **Opportunity cost** — what you give up to get an item[^mankiw-013].

### 3. Rational People Think at the Margin

Rational people do the best they can to achieve objectives given opportunities; decisions are rarely all-or-nothing but involve *marginal changes* — small incremental adjustments at the edge of a plan[^mankiw-013]. Rational choice compares marginal benefit to marginal cost and acts only if marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost[^mankiw-013].

- **Phone call:** paying $40 fixed + $0.50/minute, 100 minutes/month = $90 bill. Average cost $0.90/minute is irrelevant; a 10-minute call's marginal cost is $5, so a $7 benefit justifies the call. Users with free-at-the-margin unlimited minutes make longer calls because marginal cost is zero[^mankiw-013].
- **Airline standby:** flying a 200-seat plane costs $100,000, average seat cost $500, but with 10 empty seats the marginal cost of an extra passenger is tiny (e.g., a soda). Selling a $300 standby ticket above marginal cost raises profit[^mankiw-013].
- **Water–diamond puzzle:** willingness to pay reflects marginal benefit given abundance; water is essential but plentiful so extra cup has small marginal benefit; diamonds are unnecessary but rare so extra diamond has large marginal benefit[^mankiw-013].

### 4. People Respond to Incentives

An *incentive* — prospect of punishment or reward — induces action; because rational people weigh costs and benefits, they respond to incentives: "People respond to incentives. The rest is commentary"[^mankiw-013].

- **Prices as incentives:** higher apple price induces buyers to eat fewer apples and orchards to hire more workers and harvest more — how markets allocate scarce resources[^mankiw-013].
- **Gasoline tax:** encourages smaller, fuel-efficient cars, carpooling, transit, living near work, and over time hybrids/electrics; Europe's high gas taxes vs US illustrates the response[^mankiw-013].
- **Seat belts and Peltzman effect:** mandated seat belts lower cost of accidents, reducing benefit of slow, careful driving; drivers respond by driving faster/less carefully, increasing accidents — fewer deaths per accident but more accidents, little change in driver deaths and more pedestrian deaths (Sam Peltzman, 1975 study)[^mankiw-013]. Policy analysis must include indirect incentive effects, not just direct effects[^mankiw-013].

## How People Interact (Principles 5–7)

### 5. Trade Can Make Everyone Better Off

Competition framing misleads: US–China trade is not zero-sum like sports[^mankiw-013]. Like families specializing and trading — rather than each growing food, making clothes, building houses — countries specialize in what they do best and enjoy greater variety at lower cost; partners as much as competitors[^mankiw-013].

### 6. Markets Are Usually a Good Way to Organize Economic Activity

The collapse of Soviet/Eastern European central planning (late 1980s–early 1990s) showed government planners could not replicate market allocation[^mankiw-013]. In a *market economy*, decentralized decisions of millions of firms (whom to hire, what to make) and households (where to work, what to buy) are coordinated by prices and self-interest[^mankiw-013].

Adam Smith's *invisible hand* (Wealth of Nations, 1776): participants motivated by self-interest are led to promote general well-being — "it is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest"[^mankiw-013]. Prices reflect value to society and cost of production; Smith's corollary is that preventing prices from adjusting (taxes distorting prices, rent controls, central diktat) impedes coordination and reduces well-being[^mankiw-013].

- **Uber vs regulated taxis:** city medallion limits and fixed prices kept by police power were disrupted by Uber's app (2009); surge pricing — opposed by taxi incumbents, supported by economists — raises price when demand surges (rainstorm, New Year's Eve) to increase supply and allocate scarce rides to highest-value users, reducing search/waiting; 2014 survey: 100% of economists said Uber raised consumer well-being, 85% said surge pricing did[^mankiw-013].

### 7. Governments Can Sometimes Improve Market Outcomes

The invisible hand needs government to enforce rules and *property rights* — ability to own and control scarce resources — via police and courts; without enforcement, investment in crops, meals, or films would not occur[^mankiw-013].

Two rationales for intervention:

- **Efficiency:** *market failure* where the market alone fails to allocate efficiently[^mankiw-013].
  - *Externality:* one person's action affects bystander well-being (e.g., factory pollution harming neighbors)[^mankiw-013].
  - *Market power:* single actor/group substantially influences price (e.g., single well owner restricting water to raise price)[^mankiw-013]. Well-designed policy can enhance efficiency in these cases.
- **Equality:** markets reward ability to produce what others pay for (basketball vs chess); invisible hand does not guarantee food, clothing, healthcare for all — motivating redistribution via income taxes and welfare, at efficiency cost[^mankiw-013].

Government is not angels: policies can reward the powerful or reflect imperfect information; economics helps judge when intervention truly promotes efficiency or equality[^mankiw-013].

## How the Economy as a Whole Works (Principles 8–10)

### 8. A Country's Standard of Living Depends on Its Ability to Produce Goods and Services

Large cross-country gaps (2014: US ~$55,000, Mexico ~$17,000, China ~$13,000, Nigeria ~$6,000) and growth over time (US incomes ~2% real per year, doubling every 35 years, ~8× over a century) are almost entirely explained by *productivity* — quantity of goods/services per unit of labor[^mankiw-013]. Unions or minimum wages and foreign competition are secondary; the "hero" is rising productivity and the "villain" when it lags is flagging productivity[^mankiw-013]. Policy implication: living standards rise by raising productivity through education, tools (capital), and best available technology[^mankiw-013].

### 9. Prices Rise When the Government Prints Too Much Money — Inflation

Inflation — increase in overall price level — is in large/persistent cases caused by growth in quantity of money; more money lowers its value[^mankiw-013].

- **German hyperinflation 1921–1922:** newspaper 0.30 marks → 70,000,000 marks; prices and money both tripled monthly[^mankiw-013].
- **United States:** high inflation in 1970s (prices more than doubled) associated with rapid money growth; low inflation in 1980s and early 2000s (~2.5% per year, doubling in ~30 years) with slower money growth; President Ford calling inflation "public enemy number one" in the 1970s[^mankiw-013]. Keeping inflation low remains a core policy goal given its costs[^mankiw-013].

> **Productivity** — output per unit of labor[^mankiw-013]. **Inflation** — sustained rise in overall price level[^mankiw-013].

### 10. Society Faces a Short-Run Trade-off between Inflation and Unemployment

In the long run, more money mainly raises prices; in the short run, more money stimulates spending and demand, causing firms to raise prices with a lag while hiring more workers and producing more, so unemployment falls — a short-run inflation–unemployment trade-off[^mankiw-013].

- This trade-off underlies the *business cycle* — irregular, unpredictable fluctuations in production and employment[^mankiw-013].
- Policymakers can exploit it via spending, taxes, and money supply — the debate is how and whether to do so[^mankiw-013]. **2008–2009 crisis example:** financial/housing shock caused incomes to fall and unemployment to soar; Obama stimulus (tax cuts + spending) and Federal Reserve money expansion aimed to reduce unemployment, with fear of later inflation[^mankiw-013]. Many policies push inflation and unemployment in opposite directions over a year or two regardless of starting levels[^mankiw-013].

## Key Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | Limited resources cannot produce all desired goods/services[^mankiw-013] |
| Economics | Study of how society manages scarce resources[^mankiw-013] |
| Efficiency / Equality | Max benefits from resources vs uniform distribution of benefits[^mankiw-013] |
| Opportunity cost | What is given up to obtain an item[^mankiw-013] |
| Marginal change | Small incremental adjustment to a plan[^mankiw-013] |
| Incentive | Something inducing a person to act (reward/punishment)[^mankiw-013] |
| Market economy | Resource allocation via decentralized firm/household decisions in markets[^mankiw-013] |
| Property rights | Ability to own and control scarce resources[^mankiw-013] |
| Market failure | Market alone fails to produce efficient allocation[^mankiw-013] |
| Externality / Market power | Spillover on bystanders / ability to influence price[^mankiw-013] |
| Productivity | Output per unit of labor[^mankiw-013] |
| Inflation | Rise in overall price level[^mankiw-013] |
| Business cycle | Fluctuations in economic activity (output, employment)[^mankiw-013] |
