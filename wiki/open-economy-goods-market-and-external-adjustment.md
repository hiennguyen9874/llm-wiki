# Open-economy goods market, external adjustment, and the current account

In the short-run fixed-price model, demand for domestic goods is domestic spending less the domestic-good value of imports plus exports. Imports rising with domestic output create an import leakage: domestic fiscal expansion raises output by less than in a closed economy and worsens the trade balance, while stronger foreign demand or a real depreciation raises both output and net exports.[^blanchard-072] The same accounting can be expressed as the current account equaling private plus public saving minus investment, but the identity does not itself establish which component adjusts.[^blanchard-074]

## Demand for domestic goods and equilibrium

With $\varepsilon$ defined as the price of domestic goods in foreign goods, import quantity rises with domestic output and real appreciation, while exports rise with foreign output and fall with real appreciation:[^blanchard-072]

$$
Z=C(Y-T)+I(Y,r)+G-\frac{IM(Y,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}+X(Y^*,\varepsilon).
$$

The $IM/\varepsilon$ term is needed because imports must be valued in domestic goods before subtracting them from domestic demand. Holding the real exchange rate fixed, some increment to domestic income is spent on imports, so the demand-for-domestic-goods schedule is flatter than domestic demand $C+I+G$. Net exports consequently decline as domestic output rises. Goods-market equilibrium ($Y=Z$) and trade balance ($X=IM/\varepsilon$) are separate conditions; equilibrium can therefore coexist with either a deficit or a surplus.[^blanchard-072]

## Demand shocks and international spillovers

- An increase in government spending raises output through the multiplier but, with exports unchanged, raises imports and worsens the trade balance. The multiplier is smaller than in a closed economy because part of each induced spending round is imported.[^blanchard-072]
- Higher foreign output raises exports, shifting both demand for domestic goods and net exports upward. Output rises, but the induced import increase does not offset the initial export increase, so the trade balance improves.[^blanchard-072]
- These channels transmit demand shocks across trading partners. They also create a coordination problem: each recession-hit country may prefer foreign expansion, which improves its own trade balance, to domestic expansion, which worsens it. Simultaneous domestic expansions can raise each participant's exports and imports together, avoiding bilateral trade-balance deterioration within the group; cooperation is difficult because each participant can gain by not delivering its promised expansion.[^blanchard-072]

## Real depreciation and the Marshall–Lerner condition

Holding domestic and foreign price levels fixed in the short run, a nominal depreciation produces a one-for-one real depreciation in this framework. A real depreciation (a fall in $\varepsilon$) changes net exports through three channels:[^blanchard-072]

1. exports increase as domestic goods become cheaper to foreigners;
2. the quantity of imports falls as foreign goods become relatively more expensive domestically; and
3. the domestic-good price of a given import quantity, $1/\varepsilon$, rises, increasing the import bill.

The **Marshall–Lerner condition** is the requirement that the quantity responses of exports and imports outweigh the higher domestic-good price of imports, so that a real depreciation increases net exports.[^blanchard-072] The source assumes this condition holds for subsequent short-run analysis; it flags, but does not develop in these supplied excerpts, a later dynamic complication.[^blanchard-072]

Conditional on that assumption, depreciation shifts expenditure toward domestic goods, increasing both output and the trade balance. This is not costless welfare improvement: for a given income, domestic residents pay more for imports and are worse off in that respect.[^blanchard-072]

## Two targets require two instruments

If output is initially at its desired level but the trade balance is in deficit, depreciation alone improves net exports but expands output; fiscal contraction alone reduces the deficit by reducing output. The model therefore pairs depreciation with a fiscal contraction calibrated to offset the output expansion while retaining the trade-balance improvement.[^blanchard-072] More generally, the source's policy lesson is that targeting both output and the trade balance requires two instruments, exchange-rate and fiscal policy.[^blanchard-072]

## Current account as saving minus investment

Adding net income from abroad ($NI$) to the goods-market identity gives domestic residents' income $Y+NI$. With private saving $S=(Y+NI-T)-C$ and current account $CA=NX+NI$, the accounting identity is:[^blanchard-074]

$$
CA=S+(T-G)-I.
$$

Thus a current-account surplus means private plus public saving exceeds investment and corresponds to net lending abroad; a deficit means the country saves less than it invests and corresponds to net borrowing.[^blanchard-074] It follows that an investment rise must be matched by higher saving or a weaker current account, and a weaker government budget balance must be matched by higher private saving, lower investment, or a weaker current account.[^blanchard-074]

These are accounting constraints, not behavioral predictions. They do not show whether a budget deficit necessarily causes a current-account deficit, nor rule out improvement through depreciation: depreciation can increase output and thereby change saving and investment as well as net exports.[^blanchard-074]

## Greece: external adjustment through import compression

The source's 2000–18 Greece case illustrates why a narrowing current-account deficit is not automatically good news. Greece's deficit widened from 6% of GDP in 2000 to more than 15% in 2008; when foreign borrowing became difficult after the financial crisis, it fell to under 1% by 2018.[^blanchard-073] As a euro-area member, Greece could not use nominal depreciation against euro partners, and the source characterizes wage- and price-based real depreciation as slow and difficult.[^blanchard-073]

The source attributes much of the adjustment instead to **import compression**: output fell roughly 30% from its 2008 peak and real imports about 36%, while real exports in 2018 were only about 10% above their 2008 level despite cumulative world-output growth of 34%.[^blanchard-073] If output was still below potential, recovery without further real depreciation would raise imports and could bring deficits back; this is the source's conditional diagnosis, not a current forecast.[^blanchard-073]
