# Bastiat's seen and unseen and macro policy fallacies

Macroeconomics is about what is not seen as well as what is seen. Frédéric Bastiat (1850, *What Is Seen and What Is Not Seen*): "In the economic sphere an act, a habit, an institution, a law produces not only one effect, but a series of effects. Of these effects, the first alone is immediate; it appears simultaneously with its cause; it is seen. The other effects emerge only subsequently; they are not seen; we are fortunate if we foresee them"[^harford-ch4].

## Broken-window fallacy

Bastiat's thought experiment: a careless child breaks a window; the glazier gains six francs — *that is what is seen*[^harford-ch4]. What is not seen is the cobbler (or shopkeeper/landlord) who would have received the six francs for shoes but does not; the parents end with less in the coin jar and spend less elsewhere[^harford-ch4]. Breaking a window *could* stimulate the economy through longer, twisted causal chains, but not via the naive glazier-income accounting[^harford-ch4].

McTeer (Wall Street Journal, 2003, reproduced in Mankiw) retells it as the bakery window: teenagers toss a brick through a bakery window; the crowd sees a silver lining — the baker pays for repair, the repairman's income rises and is spent onward in a multiplier chain, perhaps a boom if the window is large enough[^mankiw-016]. Economics majors answer "Hey, wait a minute!" — the baker would have spent the money on a new suit, giving the tailor the income instead; the broken window does not create net new spending but diverts spending from elsewhere — different, not new, activity; people see what takes place, not what would have taken place[^mankiw-016]. Mankiw's summary restates the lesson: the broken window does not create new activity, just different activity[^mankiw-016].

## Two commonsense proposals and their unseen offsets

**Left-leaning: hire 100,000 temporary public-works workers (e.g., digging drainage ditches).**
Seen: employment rises, economy stimulated. Unseen: where workers come from (may poach from private sector), wage competition and automation (call-center → computers, sweepers → machines, self-checkout), private firms shrinking or growing slower; funding via taxes (less private spending) or borrowing (higher interest rates, more saving) — may do more harm than good depending on context[^harford-ch4].

**Right-leaning: cut taxes to stimulate.**
Seen: more spending money, stronger work incentives. Unseen: for given spending, more borrowing must come from somewhere — perhaps the same pockets via government debt — and taxpayers may spend less anticipating future tax rises to plug the hole[^harford-ch4].

Both plans *might* work in some situations and fail in others; context matters and simple rules are dangerous[^harford-ch4].

## Job-counting fallacy — McTeer's extension

Whenever job creation or retention is the primary objective, McTeer calls it the **job-counting fallacy** — a perpetuated form of the broken-window fallacy[^mankiw-016]. Real progress, he argues, comes from job *destruction*: it once took 90% of the population to grow food, now 3%; would-be farmers are now college professors and computer gurus — society is not worse off for that job loss[^mankiw-016]. Instead of counting jobs, "we should make every job count"; temporary mismatches of supply and demand in the labor market are transient, but Luddite machinery-destruction or protectionist "grow bananas in New York City" responses misread the unseen reallocation[^mankiw-016].

## Rejection of simplistic panaceas

Harford warns to safely ignore claims of one-line fixes: tie currency to gold, always balance budget, protect manufacturing, eliminate red tape — running a modern economy is not plain common sense[^harford-ch4]. In macroeconomics, an obvious change before your eyes is almost always accompanied by something else changing behind your back via invisible strings and pulleys[^harford-ch4]. McTeer echoes the caution: economics training helps anticipate unintended consequences — "the study of how to anticipate unintended consequences" — and heed the magic and limits of markets lest we "kill or cripple the goose that lays the golden eggs"[^mankiw-016].
