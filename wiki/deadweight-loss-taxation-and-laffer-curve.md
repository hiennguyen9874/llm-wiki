# Deadweight Loss, Taxation, and the Laffer Curve — Labor Supply Elasticity and How Revenue Varies with Tax Size

A tax reduces welfare by more than the revenue it raises — the excess is **deadweight loss** — and the size of that loss determines whether government programs are cheap or costly, which is why economists' disagreement over how elastically workers respond to wages (and thus how large the loss from the ~40% marginal labor tax is) maps directly onto disagreement over how big government should be, while the geometry of that loss (a triangle growing with the square of the tax) and the arithmetic of revenue (a rectangle that first rises then falls) together produce the inverted-U **Laffer curve** behind supply-side economics[^mankiw-029].

## THE DEADWEIGHT LOSS DEBATE — How big should government be?

Source frames the political question as an elasticity question[^mankiw-029]:

- **Core trade-off:** larger deadweight loss of taxation → larger cost of any government program → argument for leaner government that does less and taxes less; smaller deadweight loss → government programs less costly → argument for more expansive government[^mankiw-029].
- **Most important U.S. tax is on labor:** Social Security tax, Medicare tax, and much of federal income tax plus many state income taxes are labor taxes; together they place a wedge between wage firms pay and wage workers receive; for a typical worker the marginal tax rate on the last dollar of labor income is about 40% when all forms are added[^mankiw-029].
- Size of the tax is easy to determine; size of the deadweight loss is not — disagreement centers on **elasticity of labor supply**[^mankiw-029].

### Two views

**Inelastic view — small distortion:** labor supply fairly inelastic; most people would work full-time regardless of wage; supply curve almost vertical; tax has small deadweight loss. Some evidence this holds for prime-working-age main breadwinners[^mankiw-029].

**Elastic view — highly distortionary:** many groups respond to incentives, even if some do not[^mankiw-029]:

- Workers adjusting hours including overtime — higher wage → more hours.
- Second earners — often married women with children — choosing between unpaid home work (including saved child-care costs) and paid market work.
- Elderly choosing when to retire and whether to work part-time in retirement.
- People deciding between legal work and illegal/underground economy (e.g., drug trade, under-the-table jobs to evade taxes) — comparing illegal earnings to legal wage.

In each case quantity of labor supplied responds to the price of labor, so taxing earnings distorts decisions: encourages fewer hours, second earners staying home, early retirement, and entry into underground economy[^mankiw-029].

Cartoon caption in source: "What's your position on the elasticity of labor supply?" underscores the centrality of the debate[^mankiw-029].

**Persistence:** whenever two political candidates debate more services versus lower taxes, part of the disagreement may rest on different views of labor-supply elasticity and deadweight loss[^mankiw-029].

## 8-3 Deadweight Loss and Tax Revenue as Taxes Vary

Policymakers constantly consider raising one tax or lowering another; analysis holds supply and demand curves fixed while varying tax size[^mankiw-029].

### Figure 6 — Small, medium, and large taxes

- **Deadweight loss = triangle** between supply and demand curves — reduction in total surplus when tax shrinks market below optimum. In panel (a) small tax → small triangle; panels (b) and (c) larger taxes → progressively larger triangles[^mankiw-029].
- **Tax revenue = rectangle** between supply and demand curves = size of tax × amount of good sold. Panel (a) small tax → small revenue; panel (b) larger tax → larger revenue; panel (c) very large tax → drastically shrinks market so revenue falls (for a prohibitive tax, no revenue because people stop buying/selling)[^mankiw-029].

**Scaling rule — triangle area depends on square of size:** deadweight loss rises even more rapidly than tax size. Doubling tax doubles base and height of triangle → loss rises by factor of 4; tripling → factor of 9[^mankiw-029].

### Panels (d) and (e) summaries

- **Panel (d):** as tax size increases, deadweight loss continually and increasingly grows larger[^mankiw-029].
- **Panel (e):** as tax size increases, tax revenue first rises then falls — this inverted-U relationship is called the **Laffer curve**[^mankiw-029].

## THE LAFFER CURVE AND SUPPLY-SIDE ECONOMICS

### Laffer's napkin (1974)

Arthur Laffer, in a Washington restaurant with journalists and politicians, drew on a napkin how tax rates affect revenue — looking much like Figure 6 panel (e) — and suggested the United States was on the downward-sloping side where rates so high that cutting them might actually increase revenue[^mankiw-029].

- Most economists accepted the theory that a rate cut could increase revenue in principle but doubted it did so in practice; scant evidence U.S. rates had reached such extreme levels[^mankiw-029].
- Curve captured Ronald Reagan's imagination. Budget director David Stockman recounts Reagan's personal anecdote: during World War II wartime income surtax hit 90% — "You could only make four pictures and then you were in the top bracket ... So we all quit working after four pictures and went off to the country." High rates caused less work, low rates more — experience proved it to him[^mankiw-029].

### Supply-side economics

Because the rate cut was intended to encourage people to increase quantity of labor supplied, the views of Laffer and Reagan became known as **supply-side economics**[^mankiw-029]. Reagan ran in 1980 on cutting taxes: lower taxes → more incentive to work → higher incomes and well-being; incomes could rise enough that revenue might increase despite lower rates[^mankiw-029].

### Ongoing debate

- Many economists believe subsequent history refuted Laffer — lower rates reduced revenue.
- Others view 1980s events more favorably for supply-siders; history is open to alternative interpretations.
- Definitive test would require rerunning history without Reagan cuts and comparing revenues — impossible[^mankiw-029].

**Intermediate position:** overall rate cut normally reduces revenue, but some taxpayers may occasionally be on the wrong side of the Laffer curve; other things equal, a cut is more likely to raise revenue if it applies to those facing the highest rates. Argument more compelling for countries with much higher rates than the U.S. — e.g., Sweden in early 1980s where typical worker faced ~80% marginal rate, providing substantial disincentive; studies suggested Sweden would have raised more revenue by lowering rates[^mankiw-029].

**Elasticity consensus gap:** disagreement persists partly because no consensus on size of relevant elasticities; more elastic supply and demand → more distortion → more likely a tax cut raises revenue[^mankiw-029].

**General lesson not debated:** how much revenue government gains or loses from a tax change cannot be computed just by looking at rates; it also depends on how the change affects behavior[^mankiw-029].

## 8-4 Conclusion

- One of the Ten Principles (Ch.1) is that markets are usually a good way to organize activity; Ch.7 made it precise with producer and consumer surplus; here taxes show society loses some benefits of market efficiency — costly not only because they transfer resources to government but also because they alter incentives and distort outcomes[^mankiw-029].
- Analysis of Ch.6 (controls on prices) plus this chapter gives a good basis for understanding economic impact of taxes, but not the end: microeconomists study optimal tax-system design balancing equality and efficiency; macroeconomists study how taxes influence overall economy and stabilize activity and growth — taxation will recur[^mankiw-029].

## Summary

Source summary restates: tax reduces welfare of buyers and sellers; reduction in consumer and producer surplus usually exceeds revenue raised; fall in total surplus (consumer surplus + producer surplus + tax revenue) is deadweight loss; taxes cause buyers to consume less and sellers to produce less, shrinking market below surplus-maximizing level; because elasticities measure responsiveness, larger elasticities imply larger deadweight losses; as tax grows larger, deadweight loss grows larger, but revenue does not continually increase — first rises, then if tax large enough falls[^mankiw-029].

**Key concept:** deadweight loss, p. 157[^mankiw-029].

## Problems and Applications 1–10 (Mankiw Ch.8)

Concise synthesis of end-of-chapter problems; all problem statements and numerical values from source[^mankiw-029].

### 1. Pizza market

- (a) Competitive equilibrium: no deadweight loss; consumer + producer surplus maximized.
- (b) $1 per-pizza tax on pizzerias: shifts supply up, price buyers pay rises, price sellers keep falls, quantity falls; consumer and producer surplus shrink, government revenue = tax × Q2, deadweight loss = triangle between Q1 and Q2.
- (c) If tax removed and consumers/producers voluntarily transferred some gains to government, all parties including government could be better off than with tax — because deadweight triangle is a pure loss not transferred to anyone, eliminating it creates surplus to compensate government while leaving buyers/sellers better off[^mankiw-029].

### 2. Two statements

- (a) "A tax that has no deadweight loss cannot raise any revenue" — disagree; perfectly inelastic supply or demand yields vertical/horizontal curve shift with no quantity change → no triangle but revenue = tax × Q still raised.
- (b) "A tax that raises no revenue cannot have any deadweight loss" — disagree; prohibitive tax drives quantity to zero → revenue zero yet all potential surplus destroyed as deadweight loss[^mankiw-029].

### 3. Rubber bands — burden by elasticity

- (a) Very elastic supply, very inelastic demand: buyers bear most burden (demand less responsive) — consumer surplus falls more, producer surplus little change.
- (b) Very inelastic supply, very elastic demand: sellers bear most; contrast shows burden falls on less elastic side regardless of statutory payer[^mankiw-029].

### 4. Heating-oil tax — time horizon

- (a) Deadweight loss likely greater in fifth year than first: supply and demand more elastic long run → larger quantity response → larger triangle.
- (b) Revenue likely greater in first year than fifth: long-run larger quantity fall shrinks base tax × Q more[^mankiw-029].

### 5. Taxing food (inelastic demand) to raise revenue

- "Good" sense: inelastic demand → small deadweight loss and relatively stable revenue — efficient for raising revenue.
- "Not good" sense: food is necessity, large burden on poor, equity concerns; small elasticity means consumers absorb burden and have little ability to avoid it; also normative fairness issue[^mankiw-029].

### 6. Moynihan 10,000% hollow-tipped-bullet tax

- (a) Would raise little or no revenue — prohibitive rate drastically reduces quantity sold, possibly to near zero; revenue rectangle collapses.
- (b) Purpose even with zero revenue is Pigouvian/prohibitive — to discourage production/consumption and reduce external harm, not to fund government; illustrates policy can aim at behavior change[^mankiw-029].

### 7. Sock market

- (a) Tax shifts equilibrium, divides between price paid by consumers and received by producers; total spending, producer revenue, government revenue areas identifiable before/after.
- (b) Price received by producers falls; total receipts for producers (P_seller × Q) may rise or fall depending on supply elasticity — not determinable without it.
- (c) Price paid by consumers rises; total spending by consumers (P_buyer × Q) may rise or fall depending on demand elasticity; if spending falls it does not imply consumer surplus rises — surplus = value minus spending, and quantity fall reduces value more for elastic/inelastic cases, so surplus still falls[^mankiw-029].

### 8. Subsidy opposite of tax

$2 per unit paid to buyer: demand perceived price falls, quantity rises beyond efficient level; consumer and producer surplus rise, government revenue negative (cost), total surplus falls because sellers' cost exceeds buyers' value for marginal units beyond optimum — triangle deadweight loss on the overproduction side; subsidy creates deadweight loss just as tax does[^mankiw-029].

### 9. Hotel rooms — Smalltown numerical

- (a) $10 tax: price $100→$108 (+$8 to buyer, -$2 to seller), Q 1,000→900. Revenue = $10×900 = $9,000. DWL = ½ × base $10 × height 100 = $500.
- (b) Doubled to $20: price $116 (+$16), Q→800. Revenue = $20×800 = $16,000 (less than double $18,000 due to quantity fall). DWL = ½×$20×200 = $2,000 — quadruple (more than double) because DWL rises with square of tax; illustrates scaling rule[^mankiw-029].

### 10. Linear model Q^S = 2P, Q^D = 300 − P

- (a) Equilibrium: 2P=300−P → P*=100, Q*=200.
- (b) Tax T on buyers: new demand Q^D=300−(P+T). Equilibrium 2P=300−P−T → P_seller = (300−T)/3, P_buyer = P_seller + T = (300+2T)/3, Q = 2(300−T)/3. Price sellers receive falls by T/3, price buyers pay rises by 2T/3, quantity falls by 2T/3 — burden splits by relative slopes (supply coefficient 2 vs demand 1 → demand steeper/more inelastic so buyers bear more).
- (c) Revenue = T×Q = T×2(300−T)/3 = (600T−2T²)/3 — inverted-U peaking at T=150 (midpoint), zero at 0 and 300.
- (d) DWL = ½×T×(Q*−Q) = ½×T×2T/3 = T²/3 — rises quadratically with T (graph parabola through origin).
- (e) T=$200: beyond revenue peak (150), lowers revenue versus T=150 and creates DWL = 13,333.33; not good policy — same or more revenue could be raised with lower T and smaller loss; better policy is smaller tax near revenue-maximizing or lower level if goal is efficiency; extreme T destroys most surplus[^mankiw-029].

## Key Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Deadweight loss | Fall in total surplus — consumer + producer surplus loss exceeds tax revenue — because tax prevents mutually beneficial trades; area of triangle between supply and demand from Q2 to Q1 (or triangle ½×base×height for subsidy/tax)[^mankiw-029] |
| Tax wedge | Gap between price buyers pay (P_B) and price sellers receive (P_S) equal to tax; determines quantity fall[^mankiw-029] |
| Labor-tax wedge | Gap between wage firms pay and wage workers receive created by payroll/income taxes; marginal rate ~40% for typical U.S. worker[^mankiw-029] |
| Elasticity and deadweight loss | Larger price elasticities of supply/demand ⇒ larger quantity response ⇒ larger DWL[^mankiw-029] |
| Laffer curve | Relationship where tax revenue first rises with tax size then falls — inverted-U summarized in Figure 6 panel (e)[^mankiw-029] |
| Supply-side economics | View that lower tax rates encourage greater labor supply (and thus income) and may in some cases raise revenue; associated with Laffer and Reagan 1980 platform[^mankiw-029] |
| Underground economy | Legal or illegal activity hidden to evade taxes; elasticity margin for labor supply[^mankiw-029] |
| Total surplus | Consumer surplus + producer surplus + tax revenue; maximized at competitive equilibrium without tax[^mankiw-029] |
