# Foreign exchange market — nominal exchange rates, currency demand, and balance-of-payments adjustment

The **foreign exchange market** is the global electronic market in which currencies trade, and an **exchange rate** is the price at which one currency trades for another.[^krugman-ch18-095] In the source’s two-currency model, foreign demand for domestic goods, services, and assets creates demand for the domestic currency, while domestic purchases abroad create its supply; their intersection determines the exchange rate.[^krugman-ch18-095] A change in capital inflows changes currency demand, moving the exchange rate and thereby goods-and-services flows until the current and financial accounts offset.[^krugman-ch18-095]

## Quotation and currency movements

A rate has reciprocal forms: the source’s May 6, 2020 example gives both €0.9259 per US$1 and US$1.0800 per €1.[^krugman-ch18-095] Neither convention is inherently correct, so interpretation requires stating the numerator and denominator.[^krugman-ch18-095]

- A currency **appreciates** when it becomes more valuable in other currencies; it **depreciates** when it becomes less valuable.[^krugman-ch18-095]
- “Strong” and “weak” commonly mean appreciation and depreciation, respectively, but are not welfare judgments.[^krugman-ch18-095]
- A quote described merely as “up” is ambiguous: it can indicate appreciation or depreciation depending on whether it is foreign currency per domestic currency or the reciprocal.[^krugman-ch18-095]

## Supply, demand, and equilibrium

For dollars against euros, Europeans who want U.S. goods, services, or assets demand dollars and supply euros; Americans making European purchases supply dollars and demand euros.[^krugman-ch18-095] The exchange rate acts as the price in an ordinary supply-and-demand market.

A higher price of dollars in euros makes U.S. products relatively more expensive for Europeans, reducing dollar demand; it makes European products cheaper for Americans, increasing dollars supplied to buy euros.[^krugman-ch18-095] Thus the source depicts a downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve for dollars. The **equilibrium exchange rate** is where quantities demanded and supplied are equal.[^krugman-ch18-095]

The relative-price channel also connects currency movement to trade: domestic-currency appreciation reduces exports and increases imports, while depreciation increases exports and reduces imports, other things equal.[^krugman-ch18-095]

## Exchange-rate adjustment of the accounts

The current account records international goods-and-services transactions, while the financial account records international asset transactions. At foreign-exchange equilibrium, their balances offset in the source’s simplified model.[^krugman-ch18-095]

For example, an increase in European investment in U.S. assets raises demand for dollars. The dollar appreciates; U.S. residents then buy more European goods and services and Europeans buy fewer U.S. goods and services, lowering the U.S. current account by the same amount as the financial account rises.[^krugman-ch18-095] Conversely, reduced capital inflows lower dollar demand and depreciate the dollar, which raises U.S. net exports and the current account.[^krugman-ch18-095]

This is a model mechanism, not a claim that every observed exchange-rate movement has one cause or adjusts instantly. The presentation temporarily omits factor income and international transfers.[^krugman-ch18-095]

## Inflation-adjusted relative prices

A **real exchange rate** adjusts a nominal exchange rate for the countries’ aggregate price levels.[^krugman-ch18-096] With the quote expressed as Mexican pesos per U.S. dollar, the source defines the bilateral rate as:

$$
\text{Real exchange rate}=\text{pesos per U.S. dollar}\times\frac{P_{US}}{P_{Mex}}.
$$

It is therefore the relative price relevant to cross-border purchases, rather than a currency quote alone.[^krugman-ch18-096] A 50% peso depreciation accompanied by a 50% rise in Mexican prices, with U.S. prices unchanged, leaves this real rate unchanged: a room that rises from 1,000 to 1,500 pesos still costs US$100 when the quote moves from 10 to 15 pesos per dollar.[^krugman-ch18-096] In the source’s framework, the current account responds to real, not merely nominal, depreciation or appreciation.[^krugman-ch18-096]

**Purchasing power parity (PPP)** is the nominal exchange rate at which an equivalent goods-and-services basket costs the same in both countries.[^krugman-ch18-096] It is useful as a long-run benchmark, but the source says market exchange rates almost always differ from it; broad price-level differences are systematically lower in poorer countries in part because services are cheaper there.[^krugman-ch18-096] Its 1990–2019 Canada–U.S. figure shows PPP near C$1.20 per US$1 while the nominal rate moved substantially around it.[^krugman-ch18-096]

## Check-your-understanding applications

A rise in U.S. purchases of Mexican oil raises demand for pesos and the supply of dollars: the peso appreciates and the dollar depreciates. Holding peso prices fixed, other Mexican goods become more expensive to U.S. buyers and U.S. goods cheaper to Mexican buyers, reducing Mexican non-oil exports to the United States and increasing Mexican imports from the United States.[^krugman-102]

The source also works a bilateral-rate calculation. With a quote of 10 pesos per U.S. dollar and both price levels initially 100, the real rate is 10. If U.S. prices rise to 120 and Mexican prices to 150 while the nominal quote stays fixed, it falls to 8; the corresponding basket-based PPP quote rises from 8 to 10 pesos per dollar.[^krugman-102]
