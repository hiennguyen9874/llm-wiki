# Running the economy as a system

Harford places the reader in the driver's seat with a dashboard: how fast is the economy running, accelerating or slowing, fed by a small army of government statisticians[^harford-ch4].

## Micro vs. macro

Epigraph from P. J. O'Rourke: microeconomics concerns things economists are specifically wrong about, macroeconomics things they are wrong about generally[^harford-ch4]. The book's focus here is macro — understanding why the whole system misfires and what to do, which occupies the first two-thirds of the book[^harford-ch4].

## The master-economist

Keynes: the master-economist must be "mathematician, historian, statesman, philosopher — in some degree... understand symbols and speak in words... contemplate the particular in terms of the general, and touch abstract and concrete in the same flight of thought... study the present in light of the past for purposes of the future. No part of man's nature or his institutions must lie entirely outside his regard"[^harford-ch4]. Economics alone is insufficient.

Harford illustrates with leaders: David Cameron and Ben Bernanke studied economics (Bernanke taught at Princeton), but George Osborne (history), George W. Bush (history), Barack Obama, François Hollande, Mariano Rajoy (law), and Angela Merkel (chemistry) did not — yet all held levers of the world economy[^harford-ch4].

## System thinking vs. hammer

Following Phillips and Keynes as men of action who wanted to reengineer the economy to work better, Harford warns against both paralysis at complexity and random hammering at "magneto trouble"; the task is to understand the economy as a system, tracking what is not seen as well as what is seen[^harford-ch4].
