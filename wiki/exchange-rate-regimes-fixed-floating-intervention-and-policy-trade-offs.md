# Exchange-rate regimes — fixed and floating rates, intervention, and policy trade-offs

An **exchange-rate regime** is the rule governing policy toward an exchange rate.[^krugman-ch18-096] A **fixed** regime keeps the rate against another currency at or near a target, while a **floating** regime lets market forces determine it.[^krugman-ch18-096] Governments can hold a target by intervening with reserves, changing monetary policy, or restricting currency transactions, but those choices consume resources, constrain other policy objectives, or distort trade.[^krugman-ch18-096]

## Regime choices

- **Fixed exchange rate:** a government maintains a rate near a particular target. The source names Hong Kong's HK$7.80 per US$1 policy as an illustration.[^krugman-ch18-096]
- **Floating exchange rate:** market forces determine the rate; the source lists Britain, Canada, and the United States as examples.[^krugman-ch18-096]
- Regimes also lie between these poles: a rate can be periodically adjusted, managed to limit swings, or allowed to float inside a target zone.[^krugman-ch18-096]

The listed country examples and rate are source-era descriptions, not current regime classifications or quotations.[^krugman-ch18-096]

Under a credible peg and perfect capital mobility, interest parity requires the domestic nominal interest rate to equal the anchor country's rate. A country consequently gives up independent monetary policy; it retains fiscal policy but cannot raise its own rate to offset fiscal expansion.[^blanchard-079]

### Currency union as the limiting fixed-rate case

The euro illustrates an ultimate fixed-rate commitment: participating countries locked bilateral rates in 1999 and replaced national currencies at the end of 2001.[^krugman-ch18-097] The source describes the earlier European Exchange Rate Mechanism as a target zone and its 1987-onward stabilization of the franc–mark rate; Figure 18-8 also shows the 1999 lock before euro conversion.[^krugman-ch18-097] A common currency removes bilateral exchange-rate uncertainty but also removes national monetary policy and nominal depreciation as adjustment tools.[^krugman-ch18-097] The source contrasts 2008-era Iceland, whose krona depreciation reduced foreign-currency wages without nominal wage cuts, with Greece's slower, painful wage adjustment inside the euro; it presents this as an illustration of the adjustment tool retained by a national currency, not a general welfare ranking.[^krugman-ch18-097] It also describes Spain's relative wage restraint after its early-2010s recession as an “internal devaluation” that supported export-oriented auto production, illustrating an alternative adjustment path when nominal depreciation is unavailable.[^krugman-ch18-097]

## Defending or depressing a target

When the target differs from the market-clearing rate, the government must offset the excess supply or demand for its currency.[^krugman-ch18-096]

| Target relative to equilibrium | Market pressure | Intervention direction | Other directions stated in the source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Above equilibrium | Surplus of domestic currency; pressure to depreciate | Buy domestic currency and sell foreign currency | Raise domestic interest rates, or limit residents' purchases of foreign currency.[^krugman-ch18-096] |
| Below equilibrium | Shortage of domestic currency; pressure to appreciate | Sell domestic currency and buy foreign currency | Reduce domestic interest rates, or limit foreigners' ability to buy domestic currency.[^krugman-ch18-096] |

An **exchange-market intervention** is a government purchase or sale of currency in the foreign-exchange market.[^krugman-ch18-096] **Foreign-exchange reserves** are foreign-currency holdings used to buy the home currency and support its price; interventions that hold the home currency down instead acquire foreign assets and add to reserves.[^krugman-ch18-096] **Foreign-exchange controls** are licensing systems limiting the right to buy foreign currency.[^krugman-ch18-096]

## Policy trade-off

A fixed rate can reduce uncertainty about the future currency value and so reduce a deterrent to cross-border trade.[^krugman-ch18-096] It can also serve as a commitment against inflationary policy; the source uses Argentina's 1991 peso–dollar peg as an illustration, while noting that the regime collapsed in late 2001.[^krugman-ch18-096]

The source identifies three corresponding costs:

1. Intervention requires foreign-currency reserves, typically low-return holdings that can be rapidly exhausted by capital outflows.[^krugman-ch18-096]
2. Defending the rate through interest-rate policy diverts monetary policy from output stabilization and inflation management.[^krugman-ch18-096]
3. Exchange controls distort import and export incentives and can add red tape and corruption.[^krugman-ch18-096]

Thus the source frames fixed versus floating rates as a policy dilemma, not a regime with universally superior outcomes.[^krugman-ch18-096]

## Devaluation, revaluation, and the China illustration

A **devaluation** is a reduction in a fixed exchange-rate target; a **revaluation** is an increase.[^krugman-ch18-097] By changing relative prices, a devaluation raises exports and reduces imports, increasing aggregate demand; a revaluation works in reverse. The source also presents both as ways to resolve foreign-exchange-market imbalances.[^krugman-ch18-097]

In the source's historical China case, a current-account surplus and private inflows created excess demand for yuan at the target rate. China held the yuan below its market-clearing value by selling yuan and accumulating mainly dollar reserves—reported as a rise of about $2 trillion from early 2009 to early 2014, to roughly $4 trillion.[^krugman-ch18-097] When flows later reversed, it bought yuan with reserves instead; these are source-period illustrations, not current policy descriptions.[^krugman-ch18-097]

## Check-your-understanding applications

The source's China diagram treats the 2005 target of 8.28 yuan per U.S. dollar (US$0.121 per yuan) as below the market-clearing yuan price, creating a yuan shortage that would otherwise bid the yuan up. Restricting foreign investment in China shifts yuan demand left; allowing Chinese residents to invest abroad shifts yuan supply right; and taxing Chinese exports reduces yuan demand. Each can reduce the shortage at the target rate.[^krugman-102]

It identifies sudden franc–mark changes in 1974, 1976, the early 1980s, 1986, and 1993–94 as likely devaluations or revaluations in the source's historical figure. It also gives higher Canadian interest rates as an illustration: induced capital inflows raise demand for Canadian dollars, appreciate the currency, and make Canadian goods more expensive to foreigners.[^krugman-102]

## Adjustment, crises, and credibility

### Fixed rates do not fix the real exchange rate forever

In the textbook model, the nominal peg fixes the real exchange rate in the short run when domestic and foreign prices are sticky. In the medium run, however, a country below potential has lower domestic inflation than foreign inflation; with the nominal rate fixed, its relative price level falls and produces a real depreciation. Output can therefore return to potential under either fixed or flexible rates, but price-based adjustment can be prolonged and costly in lost output and unemployment.[^blanchard-082]

A one-time devaluation changes the nominal rate and, before prices adjust, produces a real depreciation. It can thus achieve faster adjustment than waiting for relative prices to move, creating political pressure to devalue when a peg coexists with recession or a large external deficit.[^blanchard-082] Britain’s 1925 return to gold at its pre-war parity is the source’s historical illustration: because British prices had risen relatively more, the choice implied a real appreciation. Keynes argued for a lower parity; the source reports that historians generally assign substantial blame for Britain’s ensuing recession to the initial overvaluation.[^blanchard-083]

### Expected devaluation can create a crisis

A peg becomes vulnerable when investors expect a devaluation or a float. The source identifies an overvalued real exchange rate—often accumulated when a higher-inflation country pegs to a lower-inflation one—and domestic demand for lower interest rates as reasons for that expectation.[^blanchard-083] Under interest parity, anticipated devaluation requires a domestic interest-rate premium to induce investors to hold domestic bonds. Defending the parity otherwise entails capital outflows and reserve losses; fully defending it can require rates high enough to depress borrowing, demand, and output.[^blanchard-083]

This creates a potentially self-fulfilling choice between validating expectations by devaluing and imposing a recession to preserve the parity. The source explicitly analogizes the mechanism to a bank run: an initially unfounded belief can make its own feared outcome more likely.[^blanchard-083] Its 1992–93 EMS illustration attributes the pressure on Germany’s partners to German reunification-era high rates, which members had to match; several countries devalued or left, while France maintained parity at an output cost. The figure confirms sharp depreciations for several currencies after September 1992, while France remained near its January-1992 level.[^blanchard-083]

## Flexible rates, common currencies, and hard pegs

### Exchange rates are forward-looking and volatile

The current rate reflects current and expected future domestic and foreign interest rates as well as the expected future exchange rate; it is therefore not mechanically controlled by today’s policy rate.[^blanchard-083] For example, a rate cut can have a modest effect if markets see it as temporary, a much larger one if it changes the expected rate path, or even an appreciation if the cut was smaller than expected. Flexible-rate countries consequently retain monetary autonomy but must accept substantial exchange-rate volatility.[^blanchard-083]

### Common-currency areas

The source attributes to Mundell three alternative conditions that reduce the cost of sharing a currency: members experience similar shocks; or relative prices and wages are flexible enough to restore competitiveness after asymmetric shocks; or factors, especially labor, can move to where jobs are.[^blanchard-083] A common currency also lowers exchange-conversion costs and makes price comparison easier. The United States is presented as relatively close to an optimal currency area because interstate labor mobility helps offset state-specific shocks.[^blanchard-083]

The source regards the euro area as more contested: it reports differing shocks, slow wage/price adjustment, and lower cross-country labor mobility. In its source-period illustration, Portugal, Greece, and Ireland faced a spending and financing reversal without the option of nominal depreciation, leaving relative-price adjustment slow and painful.[^blanchard-083] Spain’s figure is consistent with its narrative: a 1994–2007 boom coincided with lower unemployment and real appreciation; after 2008, unemployment rose sharply while the real rate depreciated.[^blanchard-083]

### Currency boards and dollarization

A country with a history of high inflation may use a hard peg to make monetary financing less credible. Dollarization eliminates the national currency; under a currency board, the authority exchanges domestic for foreign currency at the official rate but cannot conduct open-market operations. Neither is a substitute for a sustainable fiscal and macroeconomic package: credibility depends on investors believing the parity will endure.[^blanchard-084]

Argentina’s 1991 peso–dollar currency board sharply reduced inflation and was initially accompanied by strong growth, according to the source. Later dollar appreciation made the peso overvalued, and the hard peg prevented a rate cut or depreciation. In 2001 the recession, government-debt concerns, and doubts about the peg reinforced each other; Argentina defaulted in December and abandoned the board in early 2002. Dollar debt then became much more burdensome in pesos, contributing to bankruptcies and a banking collapse.[^blanchard-084]

The source preserves a live interpretive disagreement rather than a settled lesson: one position says the board was insufficiently hard and Argentina should have dollarized; the other says the board was initially useful for stabilization but retained too long and should have yielded to a float once inflation was controlled.[^blanchard-084]
