# Job search, matching, and policies to shift the Beveridge curve

Modern search models treat unemployment as mutual hunting for a suitable match — like dating — rather than a supermarket sale at a posted price, pointing to policies that can shift the Beveridge curve left (a near free lunch: lower unemployment at any vacancy level)[^harford-ch8].

## Search and matching framing

- Real labor markets rarely clear by wage alone: employers spend time advertising, screening and recruiting; job seekers sample and compare rather than selling labor at a take-it-or-leave-it wage[^harford-ch8].
- Christopher Pissarides (Nobel Memorial Prize) models this: unemployment is the initial condition of economic existence as being single is of romantic existence — all are born unemployed and single and must search for a match; the framework resolves the supply-demand puzzle and guides structural policy[^harford-ch8].

## Policies to shift the curve left

### 1. Subsidize matching and training, especially for youth

- Young seekers lack experience, contacts, knowledge of fit, and a track record to signal worth; employers may underinvest in training because social benefit exceeds private benefit and trained workers may leave for competitors (positive externalities)[^harford-ch8].
- **Future Jobs Fund (UK):** government paid employers £150/week (~$250/€200) for six months plus a couple thousand pounds upfront for a supposedly additional community-benefit job; Harford notes restrictions like "would not otherwise have existed" mean little ("Brooklyn Bridge") — many were sacked after six months[^harford-ch8].
- Evaluation (peer-reviewed, autumn 2012, >1 year after the Fund was shut early 2011; no full randomized trial but fairly solid comparison with otherwise identical nonparticipants): two years after starting, participants +11 percentage points more likely to be in proper nonsubsidized jobs, –7 points less likely to claim welfare; benefits persisted well beyond the six-month program[^harford-ch8].
- Estimated net benefits ~£18,000 per participant split roughly evenly among participants, employers and society; net government cost ~£3,100 per participant — Harford calls it extraordinarily effective and laments closure before evaluation[^harford-ch8].
- Harford stresses subsidies need proper piloting — promising ideas often fail[^harford-ch8].

### 2. Subsidize matching agencies — mixed evidence

- Large French field experiment (team including Esther Duflo) randomly offered some eligible job seekers intensive private career counseling up to a year[^harford-ch8].
- Low uptake, small temporary effect, and crucially displacement: counseling gave recipients a small edge but made nonrecipient peers (randomly not counseled) less likely to find work as competition stiffened — no improvement in overall market functioning[^harford-ch8].

### 3. Make relocating easier

- Policies often discourage mobility: US mortgage-interest deduction subsidizes homeownership; subsidies to declining regions encourage staying rather than moving to vibrant areas where vacancies exist; result is regional mismatch — vacancies in one region, high unemployment in another[^harford-ch8].
- Harford acknowledges genuine attachment to friends and family but notes rootedness has costs as well as benefits[^harford-ch8].

### 4. Pitch unemployment benefits at humane but not excessive level

- More generous out-of-work benefits reduce search urgency and can raise wages firms must pay to motivate (efficiency-wage channel), pushing the Beveridge curve right for any vacancy level; extended US unemployment insurance is one possible explanation for the post-2008 outward shift, alongside housing-market traps where falling prices prevent selling and moving[^harford-ch8].
- Possible offsetting argument: generous benefits allow breathing space to find a better skill-matched lasting job, but Harford presents the main case for them as humane — a price worth paying even if it raises unemployment[^harford-ch8].

## Cross-country regimes

- **Germany** (historically low structural): elaborate apprenticeships/training for youth plus subsidies for self-employment to keep people working and skilling[^harford-ch8].
- **United States** (also low): ultra-flexible — easy dismissal is unpleasant but enables easy hiring, getting a foot on the ladder[^harford-ch8].
- **Mediterranean (Spain, Italy, Greece):** little help for young plus extravagant protection for permanent contracts — Spain's standard contract 45 days' severance per year of service (eight years = one year's pay) creates sclerotic caution in hiring and high unemployment[^harford-ch8].
- **Spain's two-tier partial reform:** new temporary contracts with very limited rights for young workers — arguably worsened matters: in recession employers sack temporary first regardless of capability; temporaries get less training; insiders become immobile to preserve accrued security; combines rigid and flexible disadvantages; youth unemployment ~50% during euro crisis with no improvement at time of writing; policymakers "trapped with one foot on either side of a widening crevasse"[^harford-ch8].

## Policy caveats

- No single answer; different systems trade advantages and disadvantages[^harford-ch8].
- Timing matters: structural reforms that ease sacking may improve long-run hiring confidence and youth chances but depress demand if done mid-recession before the upside arrives (echoes the output-gap chapter's timing trap)[^harford-ch8].
