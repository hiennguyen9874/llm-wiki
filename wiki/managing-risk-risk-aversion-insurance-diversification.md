# Managing Risk — Risk Aversion, Insurance, Diversification, and the Trade-off between Risk and Return

Risk-averse people dislike uncertainty more than they like comparable gains because utility rises with wealth but at a diminishing rate, so a \$1,000 loss reduces utility more than a \$1,000 gain raises it[^mankiw-ch14-045]. Finance reduces risk not by eliminating it but by spreading or pricing it: insurance pools risk across many shareholders, diversification replaces one large firm-specific bet with many small ones, and portfolio choice trades higher average return for higher volatility — all limited by adverse selection, moral hazard, and undiversifiable market risk[^mankiw-ch14-045].

## 14-2 Managing Risk

Most people are risk averse; the rational response is not to avoid all risk but to take it into account[^mankiw-ch14-045].

## 14-2a Risk Aversion

- **risk aversion:** a dislike of uncertainty[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- More than disliking bad outcomes: for the risk-averse, pain of a loss exceeds pleasure from an equal gain — e.g., a 50/50 win \$1,000 / lose \$1,000 coin toss is rejected[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- **Utility foundation:** utility is subjective satisfaction from wealth; utility function is concave — flatter as wealth increases — reflecting **diminishing marginal utility** (each additional dollar yields less utility)[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Figure 1 (utility vs wealth): because of concavity, utility lost from −\$1,000 exceeds utility gained from +\$1,000, explaining rejection of the fair gamble[^mankiw-ch14-045].

Risk aversion explains demand for insurance, diversification, and the risk-return trade-off[^mankiw-ch14-045].

## 14-2b The Markets for Insurance

- General contract: person facing risk pays a fee (premium) to an insurance company, which agrees to accept all or part of the risk — car, fire, health, life, and longevity annuities[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- From economy's perspective, insurance does not eliminate risk but **spreads it more efficiently**: 10,000 shareholders each bearing 1/10,000 of fire risk is easier than one person bearing all, given risk aversion[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Gamble nature: in most years premium is paid and nothing returned except peace of mind; insurer relies on most policyholders not claiming to pay the unlucky few[^mankiw-ch14-045].

Two impediments to perfect risk spreading[^mankiw-ch14-045]:

- **adverse selection:** high-risk persons more likely to apply for insurance than low-risk because they benefit more; insurer cannot perfectly distinguish types[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- **moral hazard:** after buying insurance, insured has less incentive to be careful because insurer covers losses; insurer cannot monitor all behavior[^mankiw-ch14-045].

Result: price of insurance reflects post-purchase actual risks; high price causes low-risk persons to self-insure and endure uncertainty[^mankiw-ch14-045].

## 14-2c Diversification of Firm-Specific Risk

Folk wisdom "Don't put all your eggs in one basket" becomes diversification science[^mankiw-ch14-045].

- **Enron 2002 example:** bankruptcy amid fraud; employees had two-thirds of retirement in Enron stock → lost jobs and savings[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- **Insurance as diversification:** 10,000 homeowners each as shareholder + policyholder — each bears 1/10,000 of 10,000 fire risks rather than entire own-home risk; unless whole town burns, downside is much smaller[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- **Stock diversification:** buying one company's stock is a risky bet on its profitability (Microsoft vs Enron examples); spreading savings across many small bets reduces risk[^mankiw-ch14-045].

**Figure 2 — portfolio risk vs number of stocks** (standard deviation of return, equal weight; Source: Statman 1987)[^mankiw-ch14-045]:

- 1 stock: standard deviation ≈49%
- 1→10 stocks: eliminates about half the risk
- 10→20: another ~10% reduction
- Beyond 20–30: small additional reductions
- Risk never eliminated: diversification can eliminate **firm-specific risk** (uncertainty tied to specific company) but not **market risk** (uncertainty affecting all companies, e.g., recession)[^mankiw-ch14-045].

Definitions[^mankiw-ch14-045]:

- **firm-specific risk:** risk affecting only a single company.
- **market risk:** risk affecting all companies in the stock market.

## 14-2d The Trade-off between Risk and Return

Ten Principles reminder: people face trade-offs; the financial trade-off is risk vs return[^mankiw-ch14-045].

- Historically (past two centuries): stocks average **real return ~8%**, short-term government bonds ~3% — compensation for risk[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Example choice: diversified risky stocks 8% mean, standard deviation 20% (95% within ±2 sd: −32% to +48%) vs safe asset 3% mean, sd 0% (bank savings or government bond)[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- **Figure 3**: each point is an allocation between risky stocks and safe asset; more in stocks → higher expected return **and** higher risk[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Which point to choose depends on individual risk aversion (preferences); key insight is that higher average stock return comes at price of higher risk[^mankiw-ch14-045].

QuickQuiz prompt in source: "Describe three ways that a risk-averse person might reduce the risk she faces." — Answer: buy insurance, diversify holdings, choose lower-risk/lower-return portfolio[^mankiw-ch14-045].
