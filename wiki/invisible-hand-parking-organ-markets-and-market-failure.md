# The Invisible Hand Can Park Your Car — SFpark, Kidney Price Ceilings, and Market Failure

A benevolent social planner would need every buyer's willingness to pay and every seller's cost in thousands of markets to allocate resources efficiently — an informationally impossible task that explains why centrally planned economies work poorly — but the invisible hand decentralizes that information through prices, as shown by San Francisco's SFpark experiment which priced curbside parking to keep one–two spaces open per block and by the zero-price ceiling on kidneys which creates a persistent shortage that a paid market would eliminate, yet efficiency still fails when markets exhibit market power or externalities that drive price and quantity away from the welfare-maximizing equilibrium[^mankiw-028].

## The planner's information problem and the invisible hand

- Welfare-economics thought experiment imagines an all-knowing, all-powerful, well-intentioned dictator choosing an efficient allocation — a useful exercise but such characters are hard to come by; dictators are rarely benevolent, and even a virtuous one would lack crucial information[^mankiw-028].
- To choose efficiently on her own, the planner would need the value of a particular good to every potential consumer and the cost for every potential producer, not only in that market but in every one of the many thousands of markets in the economy — practically impossible[^mankiw-028].
- Partnering with Adam Smith's invisible hand solves the information problem: the marketplace takes all information about buyers and sellers into account and guides everyone to the best outcome as judged by economic efficiency — a remarkable feat for which economists often advocate free markets as the best way to organize activity[^mankiw-028].

This frames Chapter 7's conclusion after the proof that competitive equilibrium maximizes total surplus: the planner's job becomes easy once she relies on market forces[^mankiw-028].

## SFpark — a meter so expensive it creates parking spots

### The problem

- In many cities finding street parking seems like winning the lottery; as much as a third of traffic in some areas has been attributed to drivers circling for spaces, with costs in lost time, polluted air, and double-parked congestion[^mankiw-028].
- New York Times case (March 15, 2012) by Michael Cooper and Jo Craven McGinty — San Francisco's parking experiment is the latest attempt in a century-long saga of building highways, widening and narrowing streets, and alternately expanding and discouraging parking to keep downtowns accessible but not too congested[^mankiw-028].

### The experiment

- With a federal grant, San Francisco installed parking sensors and new meters at roughly a quarter of its 26,800 metered spots to track when and where cars are parked, and beginning summer 2011 began tweaking prices every two months — raising up to 25 cents per hour or lowering by as much as 50 cents — to leave each block with at least one available spot[^mankiw-028].
- City also cut prices at many of the garages and lots it manages to lure cars off the street[^mankiw-028].
- Most expensive spots rose from $3.50 to $4.50 per hour during phase-in, with potential to reach $6[^mankiw-028].
- Widely watched by other cities as the biggest test of Donald Shoup, UCLA urban-planning professor, whose 2005 book *The High Cost of Free Parking* (cult following, "Shoupistas" Facebook group >1,000 members) argued: get the price right at the lowest price that still leaves one–two vacant spaces per block[^mankiw-028].

### Evidence on Drumm Street

- Stretch of Drumm Street downtown near the Embarcadero and Ferry Building restaurants: last summer nearly impossible to find spots; after raising to $4.50 from $3.50, sensors showed spots available a little more often[^mankiw-028].
- Anecdote: Victor Chew, 48, salesman for a commercial dishwasher company, found a welcome space for his silver Toyota Corolla — "There are more spots available now. Now I don't have to walk half a mile"[^mankiw-028].
- Midday occupancy on one block fell to 86% from 98% after the price increase[^mankiw-028].

### Political economy and equity

- Raising prices is rarely popular. Shoup's book opens with George Costanza (*Seinfeld*) quote: "My father didn't pay for parking, my mother, my brother, nobody. It's like going to a prostitute. Why should I pay when, if I apply myself, maybe I can get it for free?"[^mankiw-028].
- Some San Francisco neighborhoods objected to installing meters where parking was free; raising prices in the most desirable areas raised concerns about accessibility for the poor[^mankiw-028].
- On Drumm Street: Edward Saldate, 55, hairstylist, paid nearly $17 for close to four hours and called it "a big rip-off"; Tom Randlett, 69, accountant, pleased to find a spot for the first time but acknowledged "complicated on the social equity level"[^mankiw-028].
- Officials noted rates are cut as often as they are raised; Shoup argued program would benefit many poor people including non-car-owning San Franciscans because all revenues fund mass transit and reduced traffic speeds buses[^mankiw-028].
- Shoup's vision: drivers would no longer attribute good spots to luck or karma but take availability for granted, "the way you take it for granted that when you go to a store you can get fresh bananas or apples"[^mankiw-028].

## Should there be a market for organs? — price ceiling of zero

### The Stephens case

- *Boston Globe* front-page case: Susan Stephens's son needed a kidney transplant; her kidney was incompatible, so doctor proposed she donate to a stranger and her son moves to top of waiting list — accepted, and two patients got transplants[^mankiw-028].
- Ingenuity and nobility not doubted, but raises policy questions: if a kidney-for-kidney trade is allowed, should she be allowed to trade a kidney for expensive experimental cancer treatment, for free tuition at the hospital's medical school, or for cash to trade a Chevy for a Lexus?[^mankiw-028].

### Current policy as a price ceiling

- As public policy, selling organs is illegal — in essence, the government has imposed a price ceiling of zero on the organ market[^mankiw-028].
- As with any binding price ceiling, result is a shortage of the good; the Stephens deal did not violate the prohibition because no cash changed hands[^mankiw-028].

### Economists' efficiency argument

- People are born with two kidneys but usually need only one; some suffer illnesses leaving no working kidney — obvious gains from trade are blocked[^mankiw-028].
- Typical patient waits several years; thousands die annually because a compatible kidney cannot be found[^mankiw-028].
- If needing patients could buy from those with two, price would rise to balance supply and demand: sellers better off with extra cash, buyers better off with life-saving organ, shortage would disappear — an efficient allocation[^mankiw-028].
- IGM Economic Experts Panel (March 11, 2014) asked: "A market that allows payment for human kidneys should be established on a trial basis to help extend lives" — source includes graphic 000182 not visually inspected; text frames it as expert polling on the proposal[^mankiw-028].

### Fairness critique

- Critics worry such a market would benefit the rich at the expense of the poor because organs would be allocated to those most willing and able to pay[^mankiw-028].
- Counter-question on current system's fairness: most people walk around with an extra unneeded organ while fellow citizens die trying to get one — is that fair?[^mankiw-028].
- Chapter frames this as the classic efficiency versus equality tension introduced in Chapter 7; source presents both sides without resolving[^mankiw-028].

## 7-4 Conclusion: Market efficiency and market failure — two critical assumptions

Chapter 7 used consumer and producer surplus to evaluate free markets and showed supply and demand allocate resources efficiently: even though each participant cares only about own welfare, together they are guided by an invisible hand to a surplus-maximizing equilibrium[^mankiw-028].

Warning: efficiency rests on assumptions; when they fail, equilibrium may not be efficient[^mankiw-028].

1. **Perfect competition.** Analysis assumed markets are perfectly competitive. In reality competition is sometimes far from perfect — a single buyer or seller (or small group) may control market prices. This ability to influence prices is called **market power**, which keeps price and quantity away from supply-demand equilibrium and causes inefficiency[^mankiw-028].

2. **No externalities.** Analysis assumed outcome matters only to participants in that market. Yet buyers' and sellers' decisions sometimes affect non-participants — side effects called **externalities**. Classic example: agricultural pesticides affect not only manufacturers and farmers but also many others who breathe contaminated air or drink contaminated water. Welfare then depends on more than buyer value and seller cost; ignoring side effects makes equilibrium inefficient for society as a whole[^mankiw-028].

- **Market failure** — the inability of some unregulated markets to allocate resources efficiently; market power and externalities are examples of this general phenomenon[^mankiw-028].
- When markets fail, public policy can potentially remedy the problem and increase efficiency; microeconomists devote much effort to studying when failures are likely and how best to correct them, using the welfare tools developed in this chapter[^mankiw-028].
- Despite possibility of failure, the invisible hand is extraordinarily important: in many markets the assumptions work well and efficiency applies directly, and welfare analysis sheds light on effects of government policies like taxation and international trade (subjects of the next two chapters)[^mankiw-028].
