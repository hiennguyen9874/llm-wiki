# Short-Run Economic Fluctuations — Three Facts and the Aggregate Demand–Aggregate Supply Model

Short-run economic fluctuations are year-to-year deviations of output and employment from long-run growth trends, described by three empirical regularities and analyzed with the model of aggregate demand and aggregate supply that abandons the classical separation of real and nominal variables in the short run[^mankiw-059][^mankiw-060]. In the short run a fall in the price level reduces quantity supplied, while over time wages, prices and perceptions adjust so the positive price–output relationship is only temporary[^mankiw-060].

## 20–1 Three key facts about economic fluctuations

**Fact 1 — Irregular and unpredictable.** Fluctuations are often called the business cycle, but the term is misleading: recessions do not arrive at regular intervals, are close together in some periods (e.g., 1980 and 1982) and absent for many years in others — the 1991–2001 expansion was the longest U.S. period without recession at the time — and are almost impossible to predict with much accuracy[^mankiw-059]. Figure 1 (U.S. since 1972, Commerce and Labor data, shaded recessions) illustrates irregular timing[^mankiw-059].

**Fact 2 — Most macroeconomic quantities fluctuate together.** Real GDP is the most comprehensive measure of activity and of total (inflation-adjusted) income, but income, spending, and production measures co-move closely in a recession[^mankiw-059]. When real GDP falls, so do personal income, corporate profits, consumer spending, investment spending, industrial production, retail sales, home sales, and auto sales — recessions are economy-wide phenomena visible in many data sources[^mankiw-059]. Magnitudes differ: investment is much more volatile. Even though investment averages about one-sixth of GDP, declines in investment account for about two-thirds of declines in GDP during recessions (e.g., Figure 1 panel b)[^mankiw-059].

**Fact 3 — As output falls, unemployment rises.** Changes in output and labor utilization are strongly correlated: when firms produce less they lay off workers, expanding the unemployed pool[^mankiw-059]. Figure 1 panel (c) shows unemployment rising substantially in each recession since 1972 and gradually declining in expansions, fluctuating around a natural rate of about 5 or 6 percent and never approaching zero because some workers are always between jobs[^mankiw-059].

## 20–2 Explaining short-run fluctuations — classical assumptions vs. short-run reality

Previous chapters (12–17) explained long-run determinants: Chapter 12 productivity and real GDP growth; 13–14 financial system and real interest rate balancing saving and investment; 15 natural unemployment; 16–17 monetary system, money supply, price level, inflation rate, and nominal interest rate — all based on the classical dichotomy (separation of real variables measuring quantities or relative prices and nominal variables measured in money) and monetary neutrality (changes in money affect nominal but not real variables)[^mankiw-059]. In the classical view, money is a veil: doubling money doubles all prices and incomes nominally but leaves real jobs and quantities unchanged; understanding real variables requires looking behind the veil[^mankiw-059].

Most economists believe classical theory describes the economy in the long run but not in the short run[^mankiw-059]. Beyond several years, money affects prices and other nominal variables without affecting real GDP, unemployment, and other real variables; year-to-year, monetary neutrality fails — real and nominal variables are intertwined and changes in money can temporarily push real GDP away from its long-run trend[^mankiw-059]. Even David Hume in 18th-century England noted that after gold discoveries expanded money, prices took time to rise and employment and production rose in the interim[^mankiw-059].

The short-run framework must therefore abandon the classical dichotomy and neutrality: real variables such as output and employment cannot be studied separately from nominal variables such as money and the price level[^mankiw-059].

## 20–2c Model of aggregate demand and aggregate supply

- **Two variables:** economy's output of goods and services measured by real GDP (real variable) and the average price level measured by CPI or GDP deflator (nominal variable) — focusing on their relationship departs from the classical separation[^mankiw-059].
- **Definitions:** model of aggregate demand and aggregate supply is the model most economists use to explain short-run fluctuations around long-run trend[^mankiw-059]; aggregate-demand curve shows quantity of goods and services households, firms, government, and foreign customers want to buy at each price level; aggregate-supply curve shows quantity firms produce and sell at each price level[^mankiw-059].
- **Axes and equilibrium:** Figure 2: vertical axis overall price level, horizontal axis total output of goods and services; price level and quantity adjust to intersection of AD and AS[^mankiw-059].
- **Not a large micro supply-demand model:** In a single market (e.g., ice cream) buyers substitute to other products and sellers hire workers from other markets when price rises; for the economy as a whole this substitution is impossible because real GDP measures total production in all markets — explaining AD and AS requires a macroeconomic theory[^mankiw-059].

## 20–3 The aggregate-demand curve

The AD curve tells the quantity of all goods and services demanded at any given price level; holding other things equal, a decrease in the price level (P1 → P2) raises quantity demanded (Y1 → Y2) — the curve slopes downward (Figure 3)[^mankiw-059].

### 20–3a Why AD slopes downward — three effects

Recall GDP identity Y = C + I + G + NX[^mankiw-059]. Government purchases G are assumed fixed by policy for this analysis; consumption, investment, and net exports depend on economic conditions and especially the price level[^mankiw-059]. All explanations assume a fixed money supply; the curve is drawn for a given quantity of money[^mankiw-059].

1. **Wealth effect (price level and consumption):** Nominal money in wallet and bank is fixed, but real value is not. Lower price level raises real value of money and wealth, increasing ability to buy goods and services and stimulating consumption spending; higher price level lowers real wealth and depresses consumption[^mankiw-059].

2. **Interest-rate effect (price level and investment):** Price level is a determinant of money demand. Lower price level → households need less money for purchases and try to lend excess by buying interest-bearing bonds or depositing in savings, driving interest rates down (detailed in next chapter)[^mankiw-059]. Lower interest rates make borrowing cheaper, encouraging firms to invest in plants and equipment and households to invest in housing (and durables bought on credit), increasing quantity demanded; higher price level raises rates and depresses investment[^mankiw-059].

3. **Exchange-rate effect (price level and net exports):** Lower U.S. price level lowers U.S. interest rate, leading some investors to seek higher returns abroad (e.g., selling U.S. government bonds for German bonds), increasing supply of dollars in foreign-currency exchange, causing dollar depreciation versus the euro and a change in the real exchange rate (relative price of domestic and foreign goods)[^mankiw-059]. Depreciation makes foreign goods more expensive relative to domestic goods, reducing U.S. imports and stimulating U.S. exports, raising net exports (exports minus imports) and quantity demanded; appreciation has the opposite effect[^mankiw-059].

Summing up: fall in price level → wealth up → consumption up; interest rate down → investment up; currency depreciates → net exports up — all increase quantity demanded; rise in price level works in reverse[^mankiw-059]. Thought experiment: if all prices halved overnight, dollars are worth twice as much — extra money could be spent at a restaurant (C), lent out reducing rates (I), or invested abroad depreciating the dollar (NX): in any case quantity demanded rises — this is what the downward slope represents[^mankiw-059].

### 20–3b Why AD might shift

Downward slope holds other things equal; many other factors change quantity demanded at every price level and shift AD[^mankiw-059]. Categorized by component of spending most directly affected (Table 1 summary)[^mankiw-059]:

- **Changes in consumption:** Increased concern for retirement saving reduces consumption at any price level → AD shifts left; stock-market boom making people wealthier shifts AD right[^mankiw-059]. Tax cuts encourage spending → right; tax hikes → left[^mankiw-059].

- **Changes in investment:** Faster computers leading firms to invest more at any price level → AD right; pessimism about future → left[^mankiw-059]. Investment tax credit (rebate tied to investment spending) shifts AD right, repeal shifts left[^mankiw-059]. Money supply is also policy shifter: increase lowers short-run interest rate, makes borrowing cheaper, stimulates investment → AD right; decrease raises rate → AD left — historically an important source of AD shifts throughout U.S. history[^mankiw-059].

- **Changes in government purchases:** Most direct policy shifter[^mankiw-059]. Congress reducing weapons purchases → AD left; state governments building more highways → AD right[^mankiw-059].

- **Changes in net exports:** Europe entering recession and buying fewer U.S. goods → AD left for the U.S.; recovery → AD right[^mankiw-059]. International speculation moving exchange rates: speculators shifting wealth into U.S., bidding up dollar → appreciation makes U.S. goods expensive, depresses net exports → AD left; depreciation stimulates NX → AD right[^mankiw-059].

**Table 1 — Why AD slopes downward and why it might shift (Mankiw Ch.20)** summarizes the three slope reasons (wealth, interest-rate, exchange-rate) and four shift categories above[^mankiw-059].

## 20–4 The aggregate-supply curve

The AS curve tells total quantity of goods and services firms produce and sell at any given price level; unlike AD which always slopes downward, relationship depends crucially on time horizon: vertical in the long run, upward-sloping in the short run[^mankiw-059].

### 20–4a Why long-run AS is vertical

In the long run, production (real GDP) depends on supplies of labor, capital, and natural resources and on available technology for turning factors into goods and services — forces studied in the growth analysis, requiring no reference to overall price level, which is determined by quantity of money[^mankiw-059]. Two identical economies except one has twice the money have twice the price level but same output because money does not affect technology or factor supplies[^mankiw-059]. Hence price level does not affect long-run determinants of real GDP → long-run AS is vertical (Figure 4)[^mankiw-059]. This vertical curve is a graphical representation of the classical dichotomy and monetary neutrality — quantity of output (real) does not depend on level of prices (nominal) — believed to work well over many years but not year-to-year, so the curve is vertical only in the long run[^mankiw-059].

### 20–4b Why long-run AS might shift — natural level of output

Long-run production is called potential output or full-employment output; more precisely the natural level of output — what the economy produces when unemployment is at its natural (normal) rate, toward which it gravitates in the long run[^mankiw-059]. Any change that alters natural level shifts long-run AS; categorized by the four classical determinants[^mankiw-059]:

- **Labor:** Increased immigration → more workers → quantity supplied up → LRAS right; many workers leaving → left[^mankiw-059]. Position also depends on natural rate of unemployment: raising minimum wage substantially raises natural rate → smaller quantity supplied → LRAS left; reform of unemployment insurance encouraging harder search lowers natural rate → LRAS right[^mankiw-059].

- **Capital:** Increase in capital stock (physical machines/factories or human capital such as college degrees) raises productivity → quantity supplied up → LRAS right; decrease → left[^mankiw-059].

- **Natural resources:** Discovery of new mineral deposit → LRAS right; weather patterns making farming difficult → left[^mankiw-059]. Imported crucial resources: world oil market events historically an important source of AS shifts for the U.S. and other oil importers[^mankiw-059].

- **Technological knowledge:** Most important reason economy produces more today than a generation ago; invention of the computer allows more production from given factors → LRAS right[^mankiw-059]. Other events act like technology: opening international trade allows specialization in higher-productivity industries → LRAS right; new regulations preventing production methods for safety or environment → LRAS left[^mankiw-059].

Summing up: any policy or event that raised real GDP in earlier chapters now described as increasing quantity supplied and shifting LRAS right; any that lowered real GDP shifts LRAS left[^mankiw-059].

### 20–4c Using AD and AS to depict long-run growth and inflation

Introducing AD and LRAS gives a new way to describe long-run trends (Figure 5)[^mankiw-059]. Both curves shift over decades; most important forces in practice are technology and monetary policy: technological progress shifts LRAS right (increasing Y), Fed increasing money supply shifts AD right (increasing P)[^mankiw-059]. Result is continuing growth in output and continuing inflation — another representation of classical growth and inflation analysis from earlier chapters[^mankiw-059]. Purpose of AD-AS, however, is not to relabel long-run conclusions but to provide a framework for short-run analysis; for simplicity continuing shifts in Figure 5 are then omitted, but short-run fluctuations should be viewed as deviations from long-run growth and inflation trends[^mankiw-059].

### 20–4d Why short-run AS slopes upward — three theories

In the short run (a year or two) an increase in the overall price level tends to raise quantity supplied and a decrease tends to reduce it — SRAS slopes upward (Figure 6: P1→P2 fall reduces Y1→Y2)[^mankiw-060]. Each theory shares a common theme: quantity supplied deviates from its long-run natural level when the actual price level deviates from the price level that people expected to prevail — above expected → output above natural, below expected → output below natural — due to a market imperfection that is temporary[^mankiw-060].

**Common equation:**

`Quantity of output supplied = Natural level of output + a × (Actual price level − Expected price level)`

where *a* determines responsiveness to unexpected price changes[^mankiw-060]. Over time wages, prices and perceptions adjust, so the positive relationship is only temporary and long-run AS is vertical[^mankiw-060].

1. **Sticky-wage theory** — emphasized in the book as simplest and possibly most important[^mankiw-060]. Nominal wages are slow to adjust — fixed by 1- to 3-year contracts and by slowly changing social norms/fairness[^mankiw-060]. Example: firm expects P=100 and contracts $20/hour; if P actually 95, product revenue 5% below expected but labor cost stuck at $20 → less profitable → hires fewer workers, output below natural; if P=105, revenue up 5% with same wage → hires more, output above natural; eventually contracts expire and wages renegotiate lower/higher but meantime employment and production remain away from long-run levels[^mankiw-060]. Hence SRAS slopes upward because wages based on expected prices do not respond immediately to actual price surprises[^mankiw-060].

2. **Sticky-price theory** — some goods/services prices adjust sluggishly due to menu costs (printing/distributing catalogs, changing price tags, time to change tags)[^mankiw-060]. Firms announce prices in advance based on expected conditions; after an unexpected money-supply contraction lowering long-run price level, some firms cut quickly but many lag to avoid menu costs, leaving prices too high → sales decline → cut production and employment; with money-supply expansion, laggards keep prices too low → attract customers → increase employment and production; during lag period overall price and output are positively associated[^mankiw-060].

3. **Misperceptions theory** — changes in overall price level temporarily mislead suppliers about relative prices in their own markets[^mankiw-060]. If overall price falls below expected, wheat farmers seeing wheat price fall before noticing other goods' price falls may infer relative price of wheat is low and cut wheat supply; workers seeing nominal wage fall before seeing goods prices fall may infer real wage low and reduce labor supply; both lower quantity supplied; opposite when price above expected — suppliers mistakenly think relative price rose and increase supply until misperceptions are corrected[^mankiw-060].

Economists debate which theory is correct; each may contain truth. For the book's purposes similarities matter more than differences — all imply the equation above — and each imperfection is temporary: wages/prices become unstuck and misperceptions corrected, so long-run AS is flexible and vertical[^mankiw-060].

### 20–4e Why the short-run AS might shift

SRAS tells quantity supplied at any given price level; like LRAS it shifts with labor, capital, natural resources, and technology — but has an additional shifter: the **expected price level**[^mankiw-060].

LRAS shifters (same for SRAS):
- **Labor:** more labor (fall in natural unemployment) → right; less labor (rise in natural unemployment) → left[^mankiw-060].
- **Capital:** increase in physical/human capital → right; decrease → left[^mankiw-060].
- **Natural resources:** greater availability → right; reduced availability → left[^mankiw-060].
- **Technology:** advance → right; regulatory reduction in usable technology → left[^mankiw-060].

**Expected price level** — influences stuck wages, stuck prices, and flawed perceptions which are set based on expected price[^mankiw-060]. 
- When expected price rises, workers/bargains set higher nominal wages, costs rise, firms supply less at any given actual price → SRAS shifts left[^mankiw-060].
- When expected price falls, wages lower, costs fall, firms supply more at any actual price → SRAS shifts right[^mankiw-060].
This plays a key role in the transition from short to long run: in short run expectations fixed at AD–SRAS intersection; in long run if people observe price different from expected, expectations adjust and SRAS shifts to bring economy to AD–LRAS intersection[^mankiw-060].

**Table 2 summary — Short-run AS:**
*Why upward?* (1) sticky wage: unexpectedly low P raises real wage → firms hire fewer → less output; (2) sticky price: unexpectedly low P leaves some firms with higher-than-desired prices → depressed sales → cut production; (3) misperceptions: unexpectedly low P makes suppliers think relative prices fell → cut production[^mankiw-060].
*Why shift?* (1) labor/capital/resources/technology as above; (2) expected price level — decrease shifts SRAS right, increase shifts SRAS left[^mankiw-060].

## 20–5 Two causes of economic fluctuations

Begin from long-run equilibrium (Figure 7): AD crosses LRAS at point A where output at natural level; expected price equals actual price so SRAS also passes through point A[^mankiw-060]. AD-AS analysis uses four steps (Table 3): (1) whether event shifts AD or AS (or both), (2) direction of shift, (3) impact on output and price in short run using diagram, (4) transition from new short-run to new long-run equilibrium[^mankiw-060].

### 20–5a Effects of a shift in aggregate demand — pessimism example

Assume wave of pessimism (White House scandal, stock crash, war): households cut spending/delay purchases, firms postpone equipment — shifts AD left from AD1 to AD2[^mankiw-060].
- **Short run:** move along initial SRAS1 from A to B: output Y1→Y2 falls (recession) and price P1→P2 falls; firms cut employment — pessimism partly self-fulfilling via falling incomes and rising unemployment[^mankiw-060].
- **Transition to long run:** price P2 below pre-shock expected P1; over time expectations catch up and expected price falls; via sticky wages (e.g., lower nominal wage bargains), sticky prices and perceptions, SRAS shifts right from AS1 to AS2, approaching point C where new AD2 crosses LRAS[^mankiw-060].
- **New long-run equilibrium C:** output back to natural Y1, price lower at P3; without policy action economy self-corrects — shift fully reflected in price (nominal change) not output (real)[^mankiw-060].
- **Policy alternative:** policymakers could raise AD (increase G or money supply) to shift AD back to AD1 and return to A, reducing length/severity of depressed output/employment if timed precisely (detail in next chapter)[^mankiw-060].

**Three lessons:** (1) in short run AD shifts cause output fluctuations; (2) in long run AD shifts affect price level not output; (3) because policymakers influence AD they can potentially mitigate fluctuations[^mankiw-060].

**Monetary neutrality revisited (FYI):** Fed reduction in money supply shifts AD left just as Figure 8: short run output and price fall (recession), long run expected price falls and SRAS shifts right back to LRAS — illustration that money matters for real variables short run (A→B) but is neutral long run (A→C): "Money is a veil, but when the veil flutters, real output sputters"[^mankiw-060].

**U.S. history Figure 9 (1900 onward, % change real GDP over previous 3 years, average ~10% per 3 years ≈3%/year):** two standout episodes attributable to AD shifts[^mankiw-060]:
- **Great Depression early 1930s:** real GDP −27% 1929–33, unemployment 3%→25%, price −22%; most explanations large AD contraction; primary blame money supply −28% 1929–33 — banking problems (household withdrawals, bankers holding greater reserves) reversed fractional-reserve money creation, Fed failed to offset with open-market operations; alternative contributors stock prices −90% depressing wealth/consumption, banking problems blocking firm financing for investment — possibly acting together[^mankiw-060].
- **WWII boom early 1940s:** government purchases ×5 1939–44, AD expansion doubled production, price +20% (with controls limiting rise), unemployment 17%→~1% in 1944, lowest in U.S. history[^mankiw-060].

### 20–5 case study — Great Recession of 2008–2009

Worst macro event in >50 years: real GDP −4.2% Q4 2007–Q2 2009, unemployment 4.4% May 2007→10.0% Oct 2009[^mankiw-060].
- **Origins:** housing boom fueled by low rates after 2001 recession and mortgage-market developments — securitization (originator bundles loans into mortgage-backed securities sold via investment banks to banks/insurers who underestimated risk), lax regulation/misguided homeownership policy, easier subprime loans; housing prices more than doubled 1995–2006 then fell ~30% 2006–09[^mankiw-060].
- **Two repercussions causing AD contraction:**
  1. Mortgage defaults/foreclosures — borrowed with minimal down payments, then underwater (owed > home value), stopped payments, foreclosure sales increased housing supply exacerbating price spiral, construction spending collapsed[^mankiw-060].
  2. Financial institutions owning MBS suffered huge losses — had leveraged bets on rising prices, near bankruptcy, lacked funds to lend, impairing intermediation; even creditworthy borrowers could not get loans, investment collapsed[^mankiw-060].
- **Policy response (aimed to return AD):**
  1. Fed cut federal funds target 5.25% Sep 2007 → ~0 Dec 2008 plus buying MBS/private loans to provide banks funds[^mankiw-060].
  2. Treasury rescue Oct 2008 — Congress $700B for equity injections into banks (government became part-owner) to stem Wall Street crisis and restore lending[^mankiw-060].
  3. Fiscal stimulus Feb 17 2009 — President Obama $787B increase in government spending after brief congressional debate[^mankiw-060].
- **Recovery began Jun 2009 but meager:** Q2 2009–Q4 2015 real GDP growth averaged 2.1%/yr vs ~3% historical; unemployment fell to 5.0% end-2015 but partly due to labor-force exit — employment-to-population only +1.3pp from trough and >3pp below pre-downturn; which policies most important remains debated among macroeconomic historians[^mankiw-060].

**Olivier Blanchard — Five lessons from the crisis (WSJ Apr 1 2013, LSE forum for Mervyn King):**
1. **Humility** — Great Moderation 1987–2007 convinced many large-economy financial/banking crises were past except emerging markets; history repeats[^mankiw-060].
2. **Financial system matters — plumbing matters** — unlike 1970s oil shocks that could be treated as aggregate shocks without plumbing details, financial system myriad distortions/small shocks build into very bad outcomes; Fed choosing one rate plus expectations hypothesis with small premia (view of Wall Street doing arbitrage) was too simple; aggregates interacting in simple models reach limits when plumbing fails[^mankiw-060].
3. **Interconnectedness matters** — crisis spread U.S.→world in days/weeks; cross-border creditor-debtor claims and risk-on/risk-off capital flows, safe havens, trade collapse 2009 (output down, trade collapsed even for non-trade-exposed countries) greater than models implied[^mankiw-060].
4. **We don't know if macro-prudential tools work** — traditional monetary+fisical insufficient for financial-sector problems; macro-prudential (e.g., raising minimum down payment → lower loan-to-value, cyclical bank capital ratios, capital controls) can target sectors without economy-wide rate hike but experiments (Brazil loan-to-value, capital ratios) "work but not great" — evaded and create distortions elsewhere[^mankiw-060].
5. **Central bank independence not designed for now** — two-way interaction monetary policy↔macro-prudential (QE near-zero rates encourages risk-taking; macro-prudential slowing housing lowers AD/output); question of organizing tools under central bank roof vs democratic deficit; independence given for clear mandate (inflation, observable) and tool (short-term rate) now larger responsibilities/tools require rethinking independence vs political supervision[^mankiw-060].

### 20–5b Effects of a shift in aggregate supply — stagflation and accommodation

**Adverse supply shift — Figure 10 stagflation.** When an event raises firms' costs, selling at any given price is less profitable so quantity supplied falls at every price level; short-run aggregate supply shifts left from AS1 to AS2[^mankiw-061]. Long-run AS might also shift depending on the event, but the analysis assumes it does not to keep things simple[^mankiw-061]. Comparing equilibria: short-run move along existing AD from point A to point B, output falls Y1→Y2 while price level rises P1→P2 — stagnation (falling output) plus inflation (rising prices), called **stagflation**[^mankiw-061].

**Transition to long-run equilibrium.** Whether stagflation persists depends on nominal wages via sticky-wage theory[^mankiw-061]. At first firms and workers may respond to higher prices by raising expected price level and nominal wages; firms' costs rise again and SRAS shifts farther left, worsening stagflation — a **wage-price spiral** where higher prices → higher wages → even higher prices[^mankiw-061]. Eventually the spiral slows: low output and employment put downward pressure on wages because workers have less bargaining power when unemployment is high; as nominal wages fall, production becomes more profitable and SRAS shifts right back toward AS1, price level falls and output approaches its natural level; in the long run with AD held constant the economy returns to point A where AD crosses LRAS[^mankiw-061]. This self-correction assumes AD is held constant; in practice monetary and fiscal policymakers may not hold it constant[^mankiw-061].

**Accommodating policy — Figure 11.** Policymakers who can shift AD (monetary and fiscal policy) may try to offset the AS shift by shifting AD right from AD1 to AD2 — just enough to prevent output from falling[^mankiw-061]. Economy moves directly A→C: output remains at natural level, but price level rises permanently P1→P3[^mankiw-061]. Policy is said to **accommodate** the supply shift — accepting a permanently higher price level to maintain output and employment[^mankiw-061].

**Two lessons from supply shifts:** (1) shifts in aggregate supply can cause stagflation — recession plus inflation; (2) policymakers who influence AD can mitigate the adverse output effect but only at the cost of exacerbating inflation[^mankiw-061].

**Case study — Oil and the economy.** Some of the largest U.S. fluctuations since 1970 originated in Middle Eastern oil fields: crude oil is a key input for gasoline, tires, and many products, much from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and other Middle Eastern countries; political supply cuts raise world oil prices, raise U.S. firms' costs, shift AS left, leading to stagflation[^mankiw-061].
- **1973–75 OPEC I:** OPEC as cartel restricting production to raise price — oil approximately doubled 1973–75; oil importers experienced simultaneous inflation and recession; U.S. CPI inflation >10% first time in decades, unemployment 4.9% (1973)→8.5% (1975)[^mankiw-061].
- **1978–81 OPEC II:** Again restricted supply, price more than doubled; inflation again >10% after having subsided; Fed was not willing to accommodate such large inflation, recession followed; unemployment ~6% (1978–79)→~10% few years later[^mankiw-061].
- **1986 favorable supply shift:** OPEC squabbling, members reneged on production agreements, world crude price fell by about half; reduced costs made supplying goods more profitable at any price level, AS shifts right (favorable shift — mirror image of Figure 10)[^mankiw-061].

## Context — growth, recession, and scope

On average over the past half century, U.S. real GDP has grown by about 3 percent per year because labor force, capital stock, and technological knowledge grow, allowing higher living standards[^mankiw-059]. In some years the economy contracts: firms unable to sell goods cut production, workers laid off, unemployment widespread, factories idle, real GDP and incomes decline — called recession if relatively mild and depression if more severe[^mankiw-059]. Example: Q4 2007 to Q2 2009 U.S. real GDP fell by 4.2 percent; unemployment rose from 4.4 percent in May 2007 to 10.0 percent in October 2009, highest in more than a quarter century[^mankiw-059].

Variables studied are largely those seen in previous chapters — GDP, unemployment, interest rates, price level — and policy instruments government spending, taxes, and money supply; what differs is time horizon: previous aim was to explain long-run levels and trends, now aim is to explain short-run deviations from trends, i.e., fluctuations from year to year rather than growth from generation to generation[^mankiw-059]. Debate exists among economists about best way to analyze short-run fluctuations, but most use AD-AS[^mankiw-059].

> **QuickQuiz answers:** Long-run AS vertical because output determined by factor supplies and technology, not price level, embodying classical dichotomy/neutrality[^mankiw-059]. Three theories SRAS upward: sticky-wage (unexpectedly low P raises real wage → less hiring/production), sticky-price (unexpectedly low P leaves firms with higher-than-desired prices → depressed sales → cut production), misperceptions (unexpectedly low P makes suppliers think relative prices fell → cut production)[^mankiw-060]. Variables shifting both LRAS and SRAS: labor, capital, natural resources, technology[^mankiw-060]; variable shifting SRAS but not LRAS: expected price level (fall shifts SRAS right, rise shifts left)[^mankiw-060]. Economy differs short vs long because classical dichotomy/neutrality fail short run; AD-AS variables are overall price level (vertical) and total output/real GDP (horizontal)[^mankiw-059].

## 20-6 Origins of the AD–AS model — Keynes and the Great Depression

The AD–AS framework is to a large extent a by-product of the Great Depression of the 1930s: economists and policymakers were puzzled about its cause and how to respond[^mankiw-062]. In 1936 John Maynard Keynes published *The General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money*, attempting to explain short-run fluctuations in general and the Depression in particular; his primary message was that recessions and depressions can occur because of inadequate aggregate demand for goods and services[^mankiw-062].

Keynes had long criticized classical economics — the long-run theory examined earlier in the book — because it could explain only the long-run effects of policies[^mankiw-062]. A few years before the *General Theory* he wrote:

> The long run is a misleading guide to current affairs. In the long run we are all dead. Economists set themselves too easy, too useless a task if in tempestuous seasons they can only tell us when the storm is long past, the ocean will be flat.[^mankiw-062]

His message was aimed at policymakers as well as economists: with high unemployment, he advocated policies to increase aggregate demand, including government spending on public works[^mankiw-062]. The analysis in this chapter and the next — how monetary and fiscal policy tools influence aggregate demand — owes much to that Keynesian legacy[^mankiw-062]. QuickQuiz application: a sudden rise in confidence after the election of a popular candidate (households expecting better future income, firms expecting higher profits) raises consumption and investment at any price level, shifting AD right — short-run output and price rise along SRAS; over time expected price adjusts and SRAS shifts left, leaving higher price with output back to natural unless policy intervenes[^mankiw-062].

## Decoupling from oil since the 1970s

The favorable-supply mirror of Figure 10 appeared when AD and SRAS shifted right together: output grew rapidly, unemployment fell, and inflation reached its lowest level in many years — the opposite of stagflation[^mankiw-062]. In recent years the world oil market has become a less important source of U.S. fluctuations: conservation, technology change, and alternative energy have reduced oil intensity by more than 50 percent per unit of real GDP since the OPEC shocks of the 1970s, so a given oil-price change now has a much smaller AS impact than in the 1970s — though for oil-exporting nations price swings remain crucial[^mankiw-062]. ## 20-6 Conclusion — chapter goals

The chapter achieves two goals: (1) documenting facts about short-run fluctuations around the long-run trend and (2) introducing the AD–AS model to explain them; the next chapter extends the model to what causes fluctuations and how monetary and fiscal policy might respond[^mankiw-062]. Summary synthesis repeated with provenance: fluctuations irregular/unpredictable with GDP, income, spending, production falling and unemployment rising in recessions; classical dichotomy/neutrality holds long run but not short run; AD slopes via wealth, interest-rate, and exchange-rate effects and shifts with C/I/G/NX; LRAS vertical determined by labor, capital, resources, technology; SRAS upward via sticky-wage, sticky-price, and misperceptions when P ≠ P^e (Y = Y* + a(P − P^e)); SRAS shifts with factor endowments plus expected price; AD left shift → short-run Y and P fall then SRAS right returns Y to natural at lower P; AS left shift → stagflation (Y down, P up) then SRAS returns to original with AD constant[^mankiw-062].
