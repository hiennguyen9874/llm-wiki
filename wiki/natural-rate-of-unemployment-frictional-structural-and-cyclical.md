# Natural rate of unemployment — frictional, structural, and cyclical components

Zero measured unemployment is not normally feasible: ongoing job creation, destruction, voluntary separation, and search create frictional unemployment, while persistent labor-market surpluses create structural unemployment. Together they form the natural rate; actual unemployment additionally varies with the business cycle.[^krugman-ch8-045]

## The decomposition

- **Frictional unemployment** is time spent in **job search**. It persists even when the number of job seekers equals the number of available jobs, because workers and jobs are heterogeneous and matching takes time.[^krugman-ch8-045]
- **Structural unemployment** occurs when job seekers exceed jobs available in a particular labor market at the current wage, including skill- and geographic-mismatch cases.[^krugman-ch8-045]
- The **natural rate of unemployment** is the normal rate generated by frictional plus structural unemployment. **Cyclical unemployment** is the difference between actual and natural unemployment associated with business-cycle downturns:[^krugman-ch8-045]

$$\text{natural unemployment} = \text{frictional unemployment} + \text{structural unemployment}$$

$$\text{actual unemployment} = \text{natural unemployment} + \text{cyclical unemployment}$$

The term *natural* does not mean fixed or immune to policy. The source says that it varies with labor-force characteristics, institutions, and public policy.[^krugman-ch8-045]

The natural rate is also the labor-market benchmark for potential output: at potential output, actual unemployment equals the natural rate; cyclical unemployment is above zero with a negative output gap and below zero with a positive output gap.[^krugman-ch16-088]

In the long-run Phillips-curve framing, the same benchmark is the **nonaccelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU)**: the unemployment rate at which inflation does not change because actual and expected inflation match. This identity is a model relation, not a fixed numerical estimate; Krugman and Wells report a CBO estimate of 4.7% for the U.S. natural rate in 2017, while their 6% NAIRU diagram is explicitly illustrative.[^krugman-ch16-089]

## Frictional unemployment and labor-market flows

In the source's December 2019 U.S. illustration—a low-unemployment month—1.53 million workers moved from employment to unemployment and 1.69 million moved in the reverse direction; flows also connected employment and nonparticipation (4.58 million and 4.41 million respectively) and unemployment and nonparticipation (1.48 million and 1.53 million). These flows support the claim that some transition-time unemployment is routine rather than evidence of a general labor surplus.[^krugman-ch8-045]

In February 2020, 35.1% of unemployed workers had been unemployed less than five weeks and 66.5% less than 15 weeks, consistent with a substantial short-duration component in a low-unemployment period. Long-term unemployment (27 weeks or more) was 19.2%; its share rose to about 45% after the Great Recession before gradually declining.[^krugman-ch8-045]

Better job-matching technology can reduce search time and thus frictional unemployment. **Synthesis:** renewed job search by people who had stopped looking increases measured unemployment initially because active search is a condition for being counted as unemployed; this is a measurement effect, not necessarily worsening job prospects.[^krugman-ch8-044][^krugman-ch8-045]

The source presents TaskRabbit as an app-based service that makes job matching less time- and effort-intensive than street-corner search, door-to-door canvassing, classified ads, or other online search. Its growth therefore illustrates a mechanism that can reduce frictional unemployment; it cautions that, as of 2019, there was not much evidence of a major shift in the overall nature of employment toward a gig economy.[^krugman-ch8-046]

## Sources of structural unemployment

A wage held persistently above the market-clearing level produces a labor surplus. The source identifies several possible channels:

- a **binding minimum wage**;
- union bargaining for higher wages and benefits;
- employer-chosen **efficiency wages** intended to improve effort, retention, or productivity;
- unemployment-benefit design that weakens urgency to accept a job; and
- skill or geographic mismatch during structural economic change.[^krugman-ch8-045]

This is a textbook mechanism, not a claim that every minimum-wage increase reduces employment. The source notes disagreement and evidence that moderate increases from a low U.S. minimum wage may not be associated with employment declines, including where employers have hiring power; it maintains that a sufficiently high binding minimum wage can create structural unemployment.[^krugman-ch8-045]

## Why the natural rate changes

The source highlights three categories of shifts:

1. **Labor-force composition.** Less-experienced workers tend to have higher unemployment. The source associates the rise in the U.S. natural rate in the 1970s with entry of baby boomers and more women, and its later decline partly with a more experienced workforce.[^krugman-ch8-045]
2. **Institutions and technology.** Unionization, temporary-help agencies, gig platforms, and technological change can alter matching, wage setting, and skill demand. Technology can raise structural unemployment if it shifts demand toward skilled labor while wages for surplus unskilled workers cannot adjust.[^krugman-ch8-045]
3. **Government policy.** Job training and employment subsidies may improve matching; high wage floors and more generous unemployment benefits may raise structural or frictional unemployment according to the source's account.[^krugman-ch8-045]

The historical estimates reported in the source illustrate variation rather than a constant benchmark: the Congressional Budget Office estimate for the U.S. natural rate was 5.3% in 1950, 6.3% in the late 1970s, and 4.4% by 2020.[^krugman-ch8-045]
