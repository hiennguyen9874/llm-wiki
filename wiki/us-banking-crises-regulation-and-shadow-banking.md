# U.S. banking crises, regulation, and shadow banking

The source presents U.S. banking regulation as repeated adaptation to fragility: decentralized banking and recurring runs prompted the Federal Reserve; Depression-era failures produced deposit insurance and activity separation; later deregulation and the 2008 shadow-banking panic revealed that protections and risks can migrate outside the traditional bank perimeter[^krugman-ch14-079].

## From national banks to the Federal Reserve

- From 1864 to 1913, federally regulated national banks alone could issue uniform federally printed currency, with issue volume tied to capital. The system nevertheless suffered frequent failures and major crises because currency could not move quickly enough to meet local demand[^krugman-ch14-079].
- Rumors of inadequate cash could create a bank run and contagion. Local clearinghouses sometimes jointly guaranteed member liabilities, and some states offered deposit insurance, but neither solved the national vulnerability[^krugman-ch14-079].
- The 1907 panic followed the failure of Knickerbocker Trust after speculative losses. The source describes trusts as bank-like, lightly regulated institutions with lower reserves that were outside the New York Clearinghouse; J. P. Morgan coordinated private support to arrest the panic[^krugman-ch14-079].
- In 1913, the Federal Reserve centralized reserve control, required deposit-taking institutions to maintain reserves and submit to inspection, and received sole currency-issue authority. These changes aimed to make the money supply more responsive and banking more stable, not to eliminate runs outright[^krugman-ch14-079].

## Depression-era response and later deregulation

- Run waves in 1930–1933 showed that Fed creation alone was insufficient. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC, 1932) lent to banks; the 1933 bank holiday, RFC recapitalization powers, expanded Federal Reserve borrowing, and federal deposit insurance formed a more active stabilization regime[^krugman-ch14-079].
- The Glass-Steagall Act of 1933 separated insured deposit-taking commercial banks from nondepository investment banks that trade financial assets. The source identifies deposit insurance as the central anti-run measure, while also describing Regulation Q and lighter investment-bank regulation[^krugman-ch14-079].
- The source attributes a long period of relative stability to the 1930s framework, then notes its erosion: Regulation Q ended in 1980 and Glass-Steagall had been weakened substantially by 1999[^krugman-ch14-079]. This is a historical narrative, not an assessment of the current legal regime.

## Savings-and-loan crisis

Savings and loans (thrifts) were insured deposit-taking institutions specialized in long-term home mortgages[^krugman-ch14-079]. Inflation and competition for deposits in the 1970s hurt their low-yield mortgage portfolios. Congress then permitted riskier investments without matching oversight; risky real-estate lending, some corruption, and delayed closure of insolvent institutions contributed to failures. From 1986 through 1995, the federal government closed more than 1,000 S&Ls, at a reported taxpayer cost above $124 billion[^krugman-ch14-079]. The 1989 response strengthened oversight and expanded Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's mortgage role[^krugman-ch14-079].

## Shadow banking and run-like vulnerability

**Shadow banking** comprises banking-like activity by nondepository intermediaries — such as investment banks, insurers, hedge funds, and money-market funds — without conventional deposit insurance or equivalent regulation[^krugman-ch14-079]. The source's mechanism is maturity and liquidity transformation: firms borrow short term, often overnight, and hold comparatively illiquid collateral-backed assets. It can function while lenders roll over funding, but loss of confidence can force repayment demands that resemble a bank run[^krugman-ch14-079]. It treats the 2007 housing-bubble collapse and subsequent 2008 financial crisis as that vulnerability becoming systemic[^krugman-ch14-079].

### Mortgage securitization and the 2008 crisis

**Subprime lending** is mortgage lending to borrowers who do not meet ordinary loan-qualification criteria. During the 2003–2006 housing boom, rising house prices made this lending appear safe because borrowers unable to pay could supposedly sell; the source says this belief helped subprime lending expand.[^krugman-ch14-080] Loan originators commonly sold the mortgages rather than holding them. Through **securitization**, pools of loans were assembled and claims on their income sold to investors, spreading exposure across shadow-banking institutions and some depository banks.[^krugman-ch14-080]

When house prices began falling in late 2006, borrowers who could neither make payments nor sell for enough to repay defaulted; investors in subprime-backed securities suffered losses.[^krugman-ch14-080] The source links the resulting loss of confidence to a sharp contraction in asset-backed commercial paper, a key short-term shadow-banking funding instrument: its figure peaks near $1.2 trillion in 2007 before falling to roughly $0.5 trillion in 2009.[^krugman-ch14-080] These are the textbook's causal account and descriptive series, not an independent attribution or current measure.

### Crisis response and Dodd-Frank

The source reports that from August 2007 the Fed supplied liquidity to a wide range of institutions and bought commercial paper; the Fed and Treasury also supported Bear Stearns and AIG. After Lehman Brothers was allowed to fail in September 2008, panic spread, leading the Treasury to inject bank capital in return for shares and the Fed to expand discount-window lending and buy long-term Treasury and Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac debt.[^krugman-ch14-080] It reports financial-system stabilization by fall 2010, while unemployment peaked at 10% in October 2009 and did not return to its pre-recession level until May 2016.[^krugman-ch14-080]

The 2010 Dodd-Frank Act sought to extend bank-style safeguards to the broader financial system. The source identifies: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau; designation and heightened capital/risk regulation of systemically important nondepository firms; federal resolution authority over troubled nondepository institutions; and public-exchange trading for most derivatives to improve transparency and reduce risk.[^krugman-ch14-080] It describes early evidence as relatively favorable but says effectiveness ultimately depends on performance in another period of turbulence; it also says the Trump administration curtailed CFPB independence and role after 2017.[^krugman-ch14-080] This is source-era assessment, not a current legal or policy status.
