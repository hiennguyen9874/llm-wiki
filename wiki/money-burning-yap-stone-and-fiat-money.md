# Money burning, Yap stones, and the nature of fiat money

For society as a whole money is not equivalent to things you can buy with it — an individual's purchasing power can be destroyed without destroying any real resources, as the KLF money-burning and the immovable stones of Yap both illustrate. Modern fiat notes function like Yap stones left in place: ownership is transferred by record-keeping and shared belief, not by moving intrinsic value[^harford-ch3].

## Burning a million pounds destroys only paper

- On 23 August 1994 Bill Drummond and Jimmy Cauty (The KLF) burned £1m in £50 notes — 20,000 notes in plastic bundles — on Jura, witnessed by a cameraman and *Observer* journalist Jim Reid; worth ~£1.5m/$2.5m today, said to be their emptied bank accounts[^harford-ch3].
- Public reaction: art-world rejection, *Observer* list of alternative uses (2,702 Rwanda kits feeding 810,810 people; B&B for 68 London families / 106 outside), hostile *Late Late Show* with Gay Byrne and studio audience jeering Drummond[^harford-ch3].
- Drummond's defence — "doesn't mean there's any less loaves of bread... any less apples... The only thing that's less, is a pile of paper" — is correct; Byrne's rejoinder that more bread could have been bought confuses individual and aggregate purchasing power[^harford-ch3].
- Replacement cost for 20,000 £50 notes is "a few pence" per note per Bank of England / U.S. Federal Reserve — no more than £2,000 (~$3,000) — so only ~$3,000 of paper was destroyed[^harford-ch3].
- Macro effect: in an economy where supply already meets demand, shrinking notes in circulation by £1m lowers average prices minutely — at the time £18bn in notes/coins (18,000× the burnt amount, fluctuating by hundreds of millions monthly), so £180 of goods falls ~1p on average; effectively a £1m gift via deflation to all pound-holders[^harford-ch3].
- In a demand-deficient economy (babysitting-co-op style) burning scrip would worsen the slump, but the central bank could reverse it at the same £2,000 printing cost[^harford-ch3].
- Lesson: microeconomics is about money you don't have; macroeconomics about money the government is out of (P. J. O'Rourke) — individual money equals purchasable things, social money does not[^harford-ch3].

## Yap stone money: ownership without movement

- On Yap (Micronesia, West Pacific) money is rai — limestone wheels with a hole, quarried 250 miles away on Palau by hundreds of men (a Victorian naturalist saw 400 Yap men, a tenth of adult males, at Palau quarries); some portable (hand-span, a couple of bags of sugar, 2ft buys a pig), largest 4.5 tons, >9ft diameter, moving it harder than moving the pig; especially valuable if someone died retrieving it[^harford-ch3].
- Innovation: ownership divorced from physical control — a transaction is publicly witnessed ("that stone second on the left behind your hut is now Tim's") without moving the stone — a collective-memory ledger[^harford-ch3].
- Seabed stone: a large new stone sank in a storm near Yap; the crew swam ashore and the chief's ownership persisted, transferable thereafter like any other stone — perfectly good money while out of sight and unreachable[^harford-ch3].

## From stones to gold to paper to fiat

- Western gold-standard system mirrored Yap: gold mined at cost/risk, stored in bank vaults, rarely moved; ownership tracked by goldsmiths' credit notes. Merchants rented vault space, received notes, then found it easier to pass notes than shuttle gold[^harford-ch3].
- Banknotes descended from those notes. Paper money has longer history: Kublai Khan's thirteenth-century purely paper system astounded Marco Polo[^harford-ch3].
- U.S. dollar and pound sterling once promised to pay "the bearer on demand" in gold; most countries broke the gold standard in the early 1930s[^harford-ch3].
- Modern £ notes retain the phrase as a relic — now only means exchanging a £10 for two £5s; Bank of England says "Public trust in the pound is now maintained by the operation of monetary policy" — fiat is a claim on nothing in particular and therefore on anything at all[^harford-ch3].
- Harford's contrast: Yap's stone is good money even at the bottom of the sea; modern money is good money even though the metal isn't there at all — the paper circulates on nods to former gold claims[^harford-ch3].

## Intrinsic value is not required

- Gold and rai were valued as beautiful/rare; salt as tasty and essential — but many intrinsically valuable items make poor money: a Ferrari is valuable but not divisible (cannot trade a wheel for a holiday)[^harford-ch3].
- Systems anchored to commodity value proved the commodity unnecessary: £1m in paper trades for £1m of goods though paper cost ~£2,000. All that is necessary for money to have value is for everyone to believe it has value[^harford-ch3].
