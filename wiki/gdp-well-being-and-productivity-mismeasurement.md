# GDP as well-being measure and productivity mismeasurement — Silicon Valley critique

Mankiw presents GDP as the single best — yet imperfect — measure of economic well-being: GDP per person tracks average income and expenditure, but omits leisure, nonmarket production, environmental quality and distribution, while official productivity (output per hour worked) appears to have stagnated for a decade even as Silicon Valley reports an explosion of free, quality-improving digital tools that GDP struggles to value[^mankiw-035].

## 1. The productivity slump and Silicon Valley's measurement rebuttal

- **Official definition:** productivity = economic output per hour worked; federal figures show barely any growth for a decade[^mankiw-035]. At a 2% annual growth rate the standard of living doubles in 35 years; at 1% it takes 70 — so low productivity slows the economy and holds down wages[^mankiw-035].
- **Silicon Valley contrarian take:** Google chief economist Hal Varian (68, purple hoodie, Mountain View, Calif.) — "evangelist for Silicon Valley's contrarian take" — argues the US has a measurement problem, not a productivity problem: "There is a lack of appreciation for what's happening in Silicon Valley because we don't have a good way to measure it"[^mankiw-035]. His demonstration: a search for "What is productivity?" shows most people do not know what it even means, yet his definition — giving people and companies tools to do things better and faster — points to an explosion of gadgets, apps and digital geegaws[^mankiw-035].
- **Why mismeasured:**
  - *Free or nearly free output.* Park meetup app that tracks locations for friends also tracks workers in warehouses/offices/malls; Internet search cuts time to learn to grow geraniums or find the best Mexican restaurant; taxi-hailing apps, airport car-rental sharing, instant translation, organ-donor matching — all provide uncounted value at home and work[^mankiw-035].
  - *Quality improvement.* A newer car that breaks down less often at the same cost creates consumer gain lost in the ether; standard price adjustments miss much of this[^mankiw-035].
  - *1930s design.* GDP was conceived when economists worried about steel and grain — output easy to measure compared with digital goods and services[^mankiw-035].
- **Statistical attempts and lag:** The Labor Department has tried to update GDP to include intangibles, e.g., periodically adjusting computer-chip productivity for faster speeds to account for higher quality, but critics say the process lags badly[^mankiw-035].
- **Historical parallel and Varian's forecast:** Indoor plumbing and household appliances drastically increased efficiency of domestic chores — time saved hauling water or washing clothes — but domestic labor is not counted in GDP so the gain did not show up in productivity numbers; those timesaving technologies eventually helped flood women into the workforce from the 1960s, sending measured US output soaring[^mankiw-035]. Varian is convinced a similar catch-up will happen: "To be fair, as we adopt technologies that save time in these nonmarket activities, that frees up time for market-based activities which will show up in GDP"[^mankiw-035].

> Source article: Timothy Aeppel, "Silicon Valley Doesn't Believe U.S. Productivity Is Down," *The Wall Street Journal*, July 17, 2015, as reprinted in Mankiw Ch.10[^mankiw-035].

## 2. Is GDP a good measure of economic well-being? (Mankiw 10-5)

Textbooks first call GDP per person a natural well-being proxy because GDP measures both total income and total expenditure — most people prefer higher income and higher expenditure[^mankiw-035].

### Kennedy's critique

Senator Robert Kennedy, 1968 campaign:

> "[Gross domestic product] does not allow for the health of our children, the quality of their education, or the joy of their play. It does not include the beauty of our poetry or the strength of our marriages, the intelligence of our public debate or the integrity of our public officials. It measures neither our courage, nor our wisdom, nor our devotion to our country. It measures everything, in short, except that which makes life worthwhile, and it can tell us everything about America except why we are proud that we are Americans."[^mankiw-035]

### Mankiw's reply — why we still care

A large GDP helps lead good lives not by directly measuring worthwhile things but by measuring ability to obtain inputs for them: nations with larger GDP can afford better healthcare, better education, broader literacy to enjoy poetry; courage, wisdom and devotion are easier to foster when material necessities are less pressing[^mankiw-035].

### What GDP leaves out

- **Leisure.** If everyone worked seven days a week, GDP would rise but well-being would fall; the loss from reduced leisure offsets the gain from greater production[^mankiw-035].
- **Nonmarket activity.** GDP uses market prices, so it excludes almost all outside-market activity: chef's meal sold at restaurant counts, same meal cooked for family does not; day-care centre child care counts, parental care does not; volunteer work is uncounted[^mankiw-035].
- **Environmental quality.** Eliminating all environmental regulations might let firms produce without considering pollution and raise GDP, but well-being would likely fall as air and water quality deterioration more than offsets production gains[^mankiw-035].
- **Income distribution.** Two societies each with GDP $5 million and GDP per person $50,000 look identical in per-capita terms — one with 100 people at $50,000 each, another with 10 at $500,000 and 90 at zero — but few would call them equivalent; GDP per person reports the average, hiding distributional variation[^mankiw-035].

Mankiw notes a textbook prompt embedded in the case: *Define real GDP and nominal GDP. Which is a better measure of economic well-being? Why?* — pointing to real GDP (base-year prices) as the quantity-focused answer, as developed in Ch.10[^mankiw-035].

## 3. International differences — GDP correlates with quality of life (Table 3)

Examining twelve large nations ranked by real GDP per person (2014, 2011 dollars) shows a clear pattern: rich-country citizens live ~10 years longer, have ~2× schooling, and rate life satisfaction ~2 points higher (0–10 scale)[^mankiw-035]. Source: UN Human Development Report 2015; average years of schooling among adults 25+[^mankiw-035].

| Country | Real GDP p.c. | Life expectancy | Avg schooling | Life satisfaction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| United States | $52,947 | 79 | 13 | 7.2 |
| Germany | $43,919 | 81 | 13 | 7.0 |
| Japan | $36,927 | 83 | 12 | 5.9 |
| Russia | $22,352 | 70 | 12 | 6.0 |
| Mexico | $16,056 | 77 | 9 | 6.7 |
| Brazil | $15,175 | 74 | 8 | 7.0 |
| China | $12,547 | 76 | 8 | 5.2 |
| Indonesia | $9,788 | 69 | 8 | 5.6 |
| India | $5,497 | 68 | 5 | 4.4 |
| Nigeria | $5,341 | 53 | 6 | 4.8 |
| Pakistan | $4,866 | 66 | 5 | 5.4 |
| Bangladesh | $3,191 | 72 | 5 | 4.6 |

The correlation extends to low birth weight, infant/maternal mortality, malnutrition, electricity, paved roads, clean water, school enrolment, pupil-teacher ratios, illiteracy, televisions/telephones/Internet access — leaving "no doubt that a nation's GDP per person is closely associated with its citizens' standard of living"[^mankiw-035].

Conclusion: GDP is a good measure of economic well-being for most — but not all — purposes; it is important to keep in mind what it includes and leaves out[^mankiw-035].

Krugman and Wells make the boundary explicit: real GDP per capita is average aggregate output per person and higher productive capacity expands what society *can* do, not what it necessarily does to improve living standards. Their 2019 cross-country illustration associates higher GDP per capita with higher average life satisfaction but shows diminishing apparent gains at higher income and exceptions such as Costa Rica's higher life satisfaction than Japan's despite lower GDP per capita.[^krugman-ch7-041]

## 4. Synthesis and limits

- GDP remains useful as ability-to-pay proxy despite omissions, and as the output base for productivity; the Silicon Valley debate highlights a measurement gap for free/quality-adjusted digital services rather than a refutation of GDP's welfare relevance[^mankiw-035].
