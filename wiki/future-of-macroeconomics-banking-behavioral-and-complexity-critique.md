# Future of macroeconomics — why macro isolated itself and three missing perspectives

Macroeconomics after 2008 looks like an empirically-driven but intellectually narrowed discipline: its most cited models prized logical consistency over realism, omitted banking, and largely ignored behavioral and complexity insights, leaving it without authoritative guidance when a banking crisis forced a choice between demand-side stimulus and supply-side austerity[^harford-ch15].

## Forecasting is not the job: the dentistry test

- Economics is stereotyped as bad forecasting because “chief economists” opine on TV about shares, but most academics do not try to forecast complex systems[^harford-ch15].
- Keynes’s line: if economists could be seen as “humble, competent people, on a level with dentists, that would be splendid!”[^harford-ch15]. You do not ask a dentist to forecast tooth-decay patterns, but to give practical advice and intervene when problems occur — the right demand for economists[^harford-ch15].
- Several years after the banking crisis, that practical performance does not feel impressive; the most exciting innovative work is from microeconomists, not macroeconomists[^harford-ch15].

## Why micro looks like dentistry right now

- Al Roth: market-design algorithms allocating children to schools, young doctors to hospital jobs, kidney donors to patients[^harford-ch15].
- Paul Milgrom, Hal Varian, Paul Klemperer: auction design from Google Ads to spectrum auctions to banking-system support mechanisms[^harford-ch15].
- John List, Esther Duflo and others: field experiments revealing human behavior[^harford-ch15]. These are engineer/doctor-like problem solvers[^harford-ch15].

## Why macro is hard — and why it isolated itself

- Scale and complexity: ten billion product varieties, seven billion people, countless unobservable transactions, plus psychology, history, culture, unforeseeable technologies, geology, climate, and microsecond computer trades[^harford-ch15].
- Second reason: response to complexity was narrowing, not broadening. Over the past forty years macro confined itself to a limited range of assumptions and modeling techniques regarded as publishable in top journals — elegant, logically sophisticated, but disconnected from reality[^harford-ch15].
- The premise: get logical consistency first, realism later. Not entirely ridiculous after Robert Lucas’s critique of the Phillips curve and the stagflation of the 1970s showed that raw empirical correlations could break[^harford-ch15].
- Four decades after the rational-expectations revolution, Harford argues macro is failing to incorporate important perspectives — a turn away from Keynes’s “master-economist” who must be “mathematician, historian, statesman, philosopher… understand symbols and speak in words… contemplate the particular in terms of the general, and touch abstract and concrete… study the present in light of the past for purposes of the future. No part of man’s nature or his institutions must lie entirely outside his regard”[^harford-ch15].

## Three neglected frontiers

### 1. Banking — omitted from both Keynesian and classical models

- For decades both sides omitted banks: banks are extremely complicated but their product looks simple — bridging savers and borrowers — so modelers waved a wand and assumed the job is done well[^harford-ch15].
- Warning sign ignored: the Great Depression was triggered by a banking crisis. It was discounted because deposit insurance seemed to fix fragility and the Depression became distant — worrying about banking felt like worrying about cavalry charges[^harford-ch15]. The cavalry charge happened anyway as a “massacre”[^harford-ch15].
- Responsibility caveat: keeping the banking system safe was not macroeconomists’ job alone — it fell to politicians, bankers, lawyers, accountants and microeconomists, and ideally safety engineers and organizational-behavior experts[^harford-ch15].
- Consequence for macro: mainstream had no good models of economic fallout — hard to say authoritatively whether banks should get central-bank cushioning or whether government should choose stimulus or austerity, or whether the shock was Keynesian demand, classical supply, or outside the apparatus entirely[^harford-ch15].
- Even after the crisis, many microeconomists argue macro’s high priests were slow to admit banking into models or to acknowledge that an intellectual response was needed, though Harford notes the research project itself is difficult[^harford-ch15].
- Epigraph framing: “Ultimately macro is an empirical subject. [It cannot indefinitely remain] impervious to the facts.” — Hyun Song Shin[^harford-ch15].

### 2. Behavioral economics — embraced by micro, ignored by macro

- Behavioral economics (fusion of economics and psychology) made big inroads in the past fifteen years: Daniel Kahneman (psychologist, Nobel Memorial Prize in economics), George Akerlof, Robert Shiller (bubble finance), Matthew Rabin (John Bates Clark Medal, often a Nobel precursor), Richard Thaler (*Nudge*, regular *Journal of Economic Perspectives* feature)[^harford-ch15]. Microeconomists paid attention, embracing or criticizing; macroeconomists largely did not show up — Shiller notes microeconomists argued at his behavioral-finance seminars, macroeconomists did not attend at all[^harford-ch15].
- Field limitations acknowledged: artificial labs, small samples, grab-bag of surprises without workable theory — but far-fetched to claim actual human behavior has no economy-wide relevance[^harford-ch15].
- Three behavioral questions already forced themselves in despite resistance, each central to macro:

  - **Sticky wages and prices:** fairness norms about a “just” price and confusion of nominal with real changes help explain why wages/prices stick — central to modern Keynesian recessions[^harford-ch15].
  - **Efficiency wages:** Ford’s five-dollar day and similar cases show employers may profitably pay above market wages; psychology gives intuitive accounts for why efficiency wages raise profits, central to unemployment[^harford-ch15].
  - **Expectations formation:** Lucas’s rational-expectations turn replaced careless/ad hoc expectations with a game-theoretic vision, more logical than predecessors. Thomas Sargent (Nobel laureate) began with rational expectations but broadened to decision-makers with far more limited knowledge. Whether rational expectations is best remains fundamental for monetary and fiscal impact[^harford-ch15].
- In each case macro tried to give psychology as small a role as possible; Harford argues it deserves at least a rebuttal and probably much more[^harford-ch15].
- Open behavioral channels for prices and investment: Keynes’s “animal spirits” — emotional, intuitive sense of whether to take risks, because the future is imponderable — vs. rational calculation of future rewards for consumer spending and business investment. Akerlof and Shiller’s book *Animal Spirits* aimed to put psychology back into macro, with limited traction so far, partly because applying low-stakes lab lessons to major financial bets is hard[^harford-ch15].

### 3. Complexity theory — many stupid agents, emergent dynamics

- Pushes opposite way to behavioral: expand from one or two “representative” agents to thousands or millions of interacting units; models are computer simulations like ant colonies — no two runs identical[^harford-ch15].
- Trade-off: individual agents tend to be extremely simple (“stupid”); understanding markets becomes like understanding sand piles or ice freezing. Hope is to gain system-dynamics understanding at the cost of psychological realism[^harford-ch15]. Complexity means different things to different people[^harford-ch15].

## Bill Phillips as the counter-model

- Phillips might have been expected to join the 1970s debates over the Phillips curve’s collapse and the Lucas critique, which pushed macro toward a highly mathematical trajectory nervous about empirical correlations[^harford-ch15].
- Instead he turned to China: having learned Chinese in a wartime prison camp well enough to become an instructor and read classic novels, he saw China by the mid-1960s as one of the planet’s poorest places that could not be ignored[^harford-ch15]. In 1967 he moved to the Australian National University in Canberra on condition he could spend half his time on China; a debilitating stroke in 1969 and fatal stroke in 1975 ended his work at age 60[^harford-ch15].
- Telling at a time when macro was becoming abstract about an idealized economy at no particular time or place, Phillips stayed fascinated by a vast, underdeveloped economy with rich culture, and by system dynamics — how economies oscillate and might be stabilized[^harford-ch15]. He could solve differential equations but never lost sight that economics is about people[^harford-ch15].
- Phillips the engineer-tinkerer: mechanic, hydraulic engineer, constructor of covert electronics, sociologist, economist, computing pioneer, crocodile hunter and wartime hero — always interested in how things work and borrowing from other fields, wanting to solve practical problems rather than retreat to an ivory tower[^harford-ch15].

## The wobbly bridge: why theory alone is not enough

- Millennium Bridge (first new Thames crossing in over a century, between Tate Modern and St Paul’s): closed after two days for alarming lateral wobble — like a horizontal ripple along a Slinky[^harford-ch15].
- Cause: emergent synchronization. Slight wobble made pedestrians adjust gait, walking like ice skaters pushing feet sideways in sync to keep balance, which amplified the wobble; bridge and crowd synchronized in feedback[^harford-ch15].
- Lesson 1 — pure theory is insufficient for real-world problems. Engineering enjoys physics laws yet is still caught out: Malpasset Dam in southern France cracked in 1959 from inadequate geological modeling (>400 deaths); Kemper Arena roof in Kansas City collapsed in 1979 just 24 hours after hosting the American Institute of Architects convention, with no fatalities[^harford-ch15]. If structural engineers can be surprised by snowdrifts, clay seams and synchronizing pedestrians, macroeconomists should not be wholly blamed for struggling with an unruly economy[^harford-ch15].
- Lesson 2 — eclectic engineering matters. Cambridge lecturer Allan McRobie, who diagnosed the bridge wobble, is the same person who painstakingly rebuilt Cambridge’s version of Bill Phillips’s MONIAC hydraulic computer from museum curiosity into a fully functioning machine[^harford-ch15]. Both Phillips and McRobie exemplify engineers with eclectic interests as a model for macro: borrowing tools, respecting detail, aiming at practical stabilization[^harford-ch15].
