# Goods-market equilibrium, Keynesian cross, and multiplier

In the textbook's short-run closed-economy model, firms meet demand at a given price level and output adjusts until production equals demand. With consumption responsive to disposable income but less than one-for-one, a change in autonomous spending changes equilibrium output by the multiplier $1/(1-c_1)$; the same equilibrium can be stated as investment equaling private plus public saving.[^blanchard-010][^blanchard-011]

## Model and equilibrium

The model abstracts from trade ($X=IM=0$), takes investment $\bar I$, government purchases $G$, and net taxes $T$ as exogenous, and uses the linear consumption function:

$$C=c_0+c_1(Y-T), \quad 0<c_1<1$$

Here $c_0$ is consumption independent of current disposable income—also a shift term for confidence, credit access, or expected future conditions—and $c_1$ is the marginal propensity to consume. Goods demand is consequently:

$$Z=C+I+G=(c_0+\bar I+G-c_1T)+c_1Y$$

The parenthesized term is **autonomous spending**. Goods-market equilibrium requires $Y=Z$, yielding:

$$Y=\frac{1}{1-c_1}[c_0+\bar I+G-c_1T]$$

The Keynesian-cross diagram represents production/income as a 45-degree line and demand as a line with slope $c_1$; their intersection is equilibrium. The cited figures were visually inspected.[^blanchard-010]

## Multiplier and adjustment

Because $0<c_1<1$, the multiplier $1/(1-c_1)$ exceeds one. A rise in autonomous demand raises production and therefore income; induced consumption raises demand again. The total is the convergent geometric series $1+c_1+c_1^2+\ldots=1/(1-c_1)$. The same logic applies in reverse to a fall in consumption confidence, investment, government purchases, or net exports once the closed-economy simplification is relaxed.[^blanchard-010]

The static model treats production and consumption adjustment as instantaneous. With inventories and production schedules, sales can temporarily differ from production: unexpectedly high sales reduce inventories, firms raise later production, and the multiplier adjustment occurs over time rather than at once.[^blanchard-010]

## Saving-investment equivalence and the paradox of thrift

In the closed model, private saving is $S=Y-T-C$, public saving is $T-G$, and goods-market equilibrium is equivalently:

$$I=S+(T-G)$$

Thus, given fixed investment, taxes, and government purchases, a fall in $c_0$—an attempt to save more at each income level—reduces equilibrium output enough that actual private saving is unchanged. This is the **paradox of thrift**. It is a short-run result conditional on the model's fixed-investment and closed-economy assumptions, not a claim that higher saving cannot raise income or capital accumulation in the medium or long run.[^blanchard-011]

## Scope and policy limits

The model isolates demand determination in the short run. It omits, among other mechanisms, monetary-policy reactions, imports, endogenous investment, expectations about whether fiscal changes persist, implementation lags, inflationary constraints, and public-debt consequences. It therefore establishes that fiscal choices can affect short-run demand and output, not that governments can precisely select any desired output level.[^blanchard-010][^blanchard-011]
