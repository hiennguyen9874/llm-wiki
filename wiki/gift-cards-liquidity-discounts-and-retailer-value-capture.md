# Gift cards as restricted purchasing power, liquidity discounts, and retailer value capture

A gift card is purchasing power restricted to a merchant or network rather than generally spendable cash. The source explains that this lower liquidity makes some holders willing to sell cards below face value, while retailers benefit when balances go unused, when spending exceeds the card balance, and when prepaid sales supply cash before goods or services are delivered.[^krugman-ch14-080]

## Why cards sell at a discount

Gift-card holders may prefer cash because it is more flexible: they can use it wherever and whenever they choose. A secondary-market buyer can therefore pay less than face value, retain a margin when reselling the card, and still offer a discount to a purchaser who plans to use that retailer.[^krugman-ch14-080] The size of the discount can vary by retailer, consistent with differing usefulness or resale demand; the source provides retailer-specific examples but does not establish a general causal estimate.[^krugman-ch14-080]

## How retailers capture value

- **Breakage:** an issued-card balance may never be redeemed because the card is lost or forgotten, a small remainder is not spent, or the retailer fails.[^krugman-ch14-080]
- **Incremental spending:** a recipient trying to use the full balance may buy more than the balance covers, potentially spending more than they otherwise would have.[^krugman-ch14-080]
- **Advance cash and loyalty:** during coronavirus shutdowns, the source reports restaurants selling gift cards, sometimes with a bonus, to obtain cash quickly and encourage customers to return later.[^krugman-ch14-080]

These mechanisms describe retailer incentives, not a claim that every card sale or promotion is profitable after fulfilment costs and defaults.
