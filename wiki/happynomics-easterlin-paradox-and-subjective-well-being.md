# Happynomics, Easterlin paradox, and measuring subjective well-being

Happiness economics asks people how they feel rather than counting market output, but life-satisfaction answers are sensitive to wording, scale, language and trivial context, produce an apparent paradox between individual-level income effects and societal stagnation, and diverge between evaluative judgments and moment-by-moment emotional experience — making data cheap to collect yet easy to oversell for policy[^harford-happynomics].

## Three ways to measure national well-being

Harford (following Kuznets) distinguishes[^harford-happynomics]:

1. **Tweak national accounts** — adjust GDP/NDP for resource depletion, traffic jams, unpaid labor, etc. (Kuznets' preferred approach; covered in [National income accounting and GDP as market-value output](national-income-accounting-and-gdp.md) and [GDP limitations and alternative welfare measures](gdp-limitations-and-welfare.md)).
2. **Grab-bag dashboard** — pull together disparate indicators: life expectancy, murder rate, inequality, depression prevalence, etc. Most civilized countries already collect such data at varying quality[^harford-happynomics].
3. **Ask people directly** — measure subjective well-being (SWB) via surveys. This is the focus of happynomics[^harford-happynomics].

Collection cost differs: adding a few SWB questions to an existing survey is cheap; richer methods such as the day-reconstruction method are costlier[^harford-happynomics]. Many countries already field official life-satisfaction questions; where they do not, Gallup does privately. Harford notes David Cameron's UK well-being measures, Nicolas Sarkozy's commission, Obama's appointments of Betsey Stevenson, Alan Krueger and Cass Sunstein, and Bhutan's long-standing "gross national happiness" rhetoric[^harford-happynomics].

## How life satisfaction is measured — and why it is fragile

The staple is an overall life-satisfaction question. Journalist Michael Blastland's spoof captures the gist: "When all's said and done, at the end of the day, Brian, taking the rough with the smooth and all that, rate your well-being on a scale of one to 10"[^harford-happynomics].

Historically: "Taken as a whole, how happy would you say you are these days? Very happy, fairly happy, or not very happy" scored 1–3; modern versions use 1–7 or 0–10[^harford-happynomics]. Despite the instruction to consider life "as a whole," answers are moved by ephemeral context:

- **Sunny-day effect and the dime experiment:** University of Michigan psychologist Norbert Schwarz asked office workers to photocopy the survey before completing it and left a dime on the copier for half the subjects; finding the dime just before the survey raised reported life satisfaction[^harford-happynomics].
- **Stock-market mood:** Angus Deaton found happiness in 2008–2010 closely correlated with stock-market moves — perhaps a common driver (expectations, weather) or simply mood being influenced by the morning news[^harford-happynomics].

A mechanical limitation is scale compression: the US can be (and is) a hundred times richer than Liberia, but cannot be a hundred times happier on a 1–3 or 1–7 scale. This is often swept under the carpet when comparing growth and happiness trends[^harford-happynomics].

## The Easterlin paradox

Richard Easterlin's 1974 paper "Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot?" is the most influential paper in the field and extensively uses these surveys[^harford-happynomics]. Findings:

- **Within a society:** richer people are robustly happier than poorer people. Other factors — divorce, unemployment, ill health — have far larger impacts than cash alone, but the income gradient is extremely robust[^harford-happynomics].
- **Across societies / over time:** richer societies are not happier than poorer societies. A 10% pay raise makes an individual happier; 10% economic growth does not make society happier. The tension is the *Easterlin paradox*[^harford-happynomics].

Easterlin is often misquoted as proving growth does not lead to happiness. Harford suggests a narrower reading given the 1–3 scale: in the 1950s almost half of Americans already said "very happy" and the average was ~2.5/3, so there was little room to rise — evidence that growth does not eliminate misery rather than that it does not raise happiness[^harford-happynomics].

Blanchard summarizes Stevenson and Wolfers's 2006 Gallup evidence for 131 countries as a positive relationship between PPP GDP per person and average life satisfaction, with the within-country income gradients usually pointing upward at a similar slope.[^blanchard-035] It supports their challenge to a hard income threshold, but the source explicitly retains uncertainty about life-satisfaction changes over time within a country and recognizes income distribution and other welfare dimensions.[^blanchard-035]

### Three candidate explanations

**1. The questions tell us little over time / across languages.**

Reasonable to compare people in the same society, language and period; doubtful across languages and decades. Easterlin's 1965 data: 53% of British "very happy" vs 20% West German vs 12% French at similar income per head; a later Eurobarometer: 64% Danes "very satisfied" vs 16% French. Linguists warn it is lazy to assume languages have a precise analogue for "happy"[^harford-happynomics].

The analogy: imagine measuring economic growth by asking "How rich would you say you are? Very rich, fairly rich or not at all rich?" After learning about Bill Gates, a respondent might downgrade from "fairly rich" to "not at all rich" without feeling poorer — simply revising views of what wealth is possible. Happiness reports may similarly adapt: we may feel happier than our parents but have raised our notion of how happy one may reasonably feel. Whether this adaptation occurs is hard to know[^harford-happynomics].

**2. The early data were flawed; properly measured, money does buy happiness for societies.**

Japan's happiness appeared to stagnate during its boom, but retranslations show questionnaires kept changing and raising the bar[^harford-happynomics]. Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers argue that with proper measurement the cross-country satisfaction-income relationship is "one of the highest correlations you'll ever see in a cross-country data set in the social sciences, ever" and mirrors the within-country relationship[^harford-happynomics]. Easterlin and others contest this with counter-analyses. Harford's conclusion: jury is out — the paradox may not exist at all, but it is too early to write it off[^harford-happynomics].

**3. Take the paradox at face value: people care about relative income / status.**

What matters is not absolute income but position in the pecking order — status is strongly correlated with income and is zero-sum: if you rise, someone else falls[^harford-happynomics]. Often cited is Sara Solnick and David Hemenway's survey: choose $50,000 when others have $25,000 versus $100,000 when others have $200,000 — many choose the first, preferring to be relatively rich. Harford notes a grain of salt: most respondents were Harvard students (particularly competitive); only a third of Harvard staff agreed. The relative-income hypothesis remains enormously plausible despite data difficulties and would take a lot to overturn[^harford-happynomics].

## Political economy of happiness metrics

Collecting happiness statistics does not guarantee happier citizens, and politicians select metrics that flatter them[^harford-happynomics]:

- **Bhutan:** venerated by naïve happiness advocates unaware of its human-rights record; Human Rights Watch reports stripping and harassment of the Nepali minority, many driven into refugee camps in Nepal. Driving out miserable minorities could mechanically raise average happiness; "gross national happiness" was announced by King Jigme Singye Wangchuck in a 1986 *Financial Times* interview when pressed on lack of economic progress — a defensive reflex[^harford-happynomics].
- **France:** President Sarkozy commissioned Joseph Stiglitz, Amartya Sen and Jean-Paul Fitoussi to consider GDP alternatives; one motive was that France's low work hours depress GDP but flatter alternative indices[^harford-happynomics].
- **Beyond:** politicians routinely hunt for measures that reflect well on them; happiness is no exception[^harford-happynomics].

## Kahneman's three happinesses and the day-reconstruction method

Daniel Kahneman (Nobel laureate, earlier encountered on sticky prices) argues "happiness" conflates three distinct concepts[^harford-happynomics]:

1. **Evaluative / remembered life satisfaction** — the Easterlin-type summary ("how satisfied are you with your life?").
2. **Experienced / emotional stream** — the ongoing sequence through a day: tired, laughing, stressed, joyful, aroused; whether life is enjoyable minute-by-minute.
3. **Objective welfare criteria** — external checklist: trouble sleeping, health problems, etc.

Lazy thinking lumps all three as "happiness," but they are not the same and "happiness" arguably exceeds any one of them[^harford-happynomics].

To measure (2), Kahneman with Norbert Schwarz and Alan Krueger developed the **day reconstruction method (DRM)**: respondents recall the previous day episode by episode and report the prevalent feeling (enthusiasm, boredom, joy, irritation). The aim is to track time spent in negative emotions (fear, anger, sadness)[^harford-happynomics].

Key results:

- **Income and mood diverge:** Kahneman and Deaton found higher income correlates with life satisfaction without limit, but beyond ~$75,000/year extra money does not improve mood as measured by DRM. Whether money buys happiness depends on which happiness you mean[^harford-happynomics].
- **France vs Ohio women:** American women were twice as likely to say they were "very satisfied" with life, yet French women spent more of their day in a good mood. Evaluative and experienced measures produce different rankings[^harford-happynomics].

### National time accounts

Kahneman and Krueger advocated publishing **time accounts** alongside national accounts, piggy-backing on the long-standing US time-use survey (how time is spent: commuting, praying, watching TV, lunch). Combining time-use with DRM yields time spent in an unpleasant mood and what people were doing during it[^harford-happynomics].

Findings: commuting and working most likely to induce bad mood; lunch, dinner and sex rarely unpleasant (though spouse choice matters)[^harford-happynomics]. A stripped-down DRM for telephone surveys was piloted by the Bureau of Labor Statistics; results can inform evaluations of projects reducing commuting (roads, high-speed rail) or providing amenities (parks, playgrounds, museums) without fully escaping interpersonal 6/10 vs 5/10 comparability problems, though DRM's time-in-bad-mood metric is arguably more objective[^harford-happynomics].

## Policy implications — and limits

Harford stresses collecting SWB data is worthwhile but not revolutionary[^harford-happynomics]:

- **If Easterlin's relative-income explanation is correct**, it implies redistribution raises happiness (taxing the rich makes others happier even before spending the revenue). But redistribution already exists; whether *more* is warranted is unclear[^harford-happynomics].
- **Unemployment:** life-satisfaction research confirms it is extremely depressing — an argument to reduce unemployment, which should already be on the agenda[^harford-happynomics].
- **Tort damages:** Andrew Oswald's work estimates how misfortunes (injury, bereavement) affect well-being and how much money might offset the emotional impact — useful for courts but not macro policy. Oswald also supplied the chapter's epigraph: "once a country has filled its larders there is no point in that nation becoming richer — the hippies...are being confirmed"[^harford-happynomics].
- **Time accounts:** the most promising policy tool is time accounting — quantifying unpleasant time by activity to evaluate transport and amenity investments — rather than maximizing a single happiness score[^harford-happynomics].

Overall: gathering evaluative SWB is low-cost and informative; DRM-based time accounts are costlier but potentially more policy-relevant. Either way, "happiness" must be reorganized before it can guide trade-offs, and the distinction between evaluative and experienced well-being must be taken seriously if happiness is to influence policy[^harford-happynomics].

## Contradictions

- **Does growth raise national happiness?** Stevenson/Wolfers (properly measured, cross-country satisfaction-income correlation is among the highest in social science; money buys happiness for societies as within them) vs Easterlin's paradox as originally stated (growth does not raise happiness; status/relative income dominates) — evidence contested, jury out[^harford-happynomics].
- **Which happiness tracks welfare for policy?** Life-satisfaction rankings (US women > French women) vs experienced-mood rankings (French women > US women) diverge; Kahneman's framework says they are different constructs, not interchangeable[^harford-happynomics].
