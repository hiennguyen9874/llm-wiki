# Purchasing-power-parity comparisons of cross-country living standards

For comparisons of material living standards across countries, output or consumption **per person** should be valued at purchasing-power-parity (PPP) prices rather than converted at current market exchange rates: exchange rates fluctuate for reasons that do not imply like-sized welfare changes, and basic goods and services are systematically cheaper in lower-income countries.[^blanchard-034] PPP constructs each country's quantities with a common price vector, so it is a comparison of purchasing power, not a claim that currencies trade at that rate.[^blanchard-035]

## Why current-exchange-rate GDP misleads

A currency conversion alone confounds relative quantities with relative prices. It can make a country's measured GDP per person change sharply when its exchange rate moves even though local consumption possibilities have not moved comparably.[^blanchard-034] It also understates the purchasing power of income where nontraded basics are cheaper. The source's 2018 illustration puts India at $2,016 per person at the current exchange rate versus $62,517 in the United States (a 31.3-fold ratio); at PPP prices, the ratio is 8. The figures are source-era illustrations, not current estimates.[^blanchard-034][^blanchard-035]

## Construction and interpretation

A PPP comparison values the quantities of goods and services consumed or produced in every country using one common set of prices. The example uses U.S. car and food prices: Russian consumption of 120,000 rubles converts to $2,000 at 60 rubles per dollar, but its quantities value to $10,700 at U.S. prices versus U.S. consumption of $20,000.[^blanchard-035] Operational PPP datasets use average cross-country **international-dollar** prices rather than privileging one country's prices; the Penn World Table provides such series for GDP and components across many countries.[^blanchard-035]

PPP therefore makes comparisons more meaningful, not exact. Matching products and quality across countries and choosing representative common prices are substantive measurement problems. Differences from current-exchange-rate comparisons are usually larger between rich and poor countries than among rich countries.[^blanchard-035]

## Choose the measure for the question

- **Living standards:** output per person is a practical summary; consumption per person is conceptually closer to welfare. The source expects similar country rankings when consumption-output ratios are similar, but neither measure captures all welfare dimensions.[^blanchard-035]
- **Productivity:** compare output per worker, or preferably output per hour when hours data are available. Output per person can differ because employment or hours per resident differ; the source attributes much of the United States–Germany per-person gap to hours per person rather than output per hour.[^blanchard-035]
- **Exchange-rate analysis:** a PPP *exchange rate* is a long-run relative-price benchmark. It is related to, but distinct from, the common-price PPP valuation used for international living-standard comparisons.[^blanchard-035]
