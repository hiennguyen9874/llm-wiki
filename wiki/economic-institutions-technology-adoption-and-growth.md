# Economic Institutions, Technology Adoption, and Growth

The source argues that poor institutions are a central reason countries may fail to invest, introduce, or adapt technologies already known elsewhere. Protection from expropriation, reliable courts, enforceable contracts and patents, competition policy, and limits on corruption make expected returns to investment and innovation more credible. The evidence presented is suggestive, not sufficient to treat institutions as the sole cause of income differences.[^blanchard-047][^blanchard-048]

## Mechanisms and causal caution

If producers expect the state, corrupt officials, or private actors to seize returns, they have weaker incentives to create firms, invest, or perform R&D. A well-functioning political and judicial system reduces those risks; patent protection supports R&D, and antitrust enforcement can preserve incentives to improve products and methods.[^blanchard-048]

The source plots a strong positive cross-country association between protection against expropriation and 1995 PPP GDP per person. It explicitly cautions that causality runs both ways: weak protection can depress income, while low income can limit the capacity to fund courts and policing. Geography, disease, and culture are possible additional explanations, so the association alone does not isolate institutional causation.[^blanchard-047][^blanchard-048]

## Korea as a comparative illustration

Before their separation, North and South Korea shared ethnic and linguistic background and had roughly similar reported PPP GDP per person. The source contrasts South Korea's capitalist organization—private ownership and legal protection for private producers, alongside substantial state intervention—with North Korea's central planning, nationalization, cooperatives, and absence of individual private-property rights. It reports that by 1998 South Korean PPP GDP per person was about ten times North Korea's; the chart shows the divergence after 1970.[^blanchard-048]

This comparison is a powerful historical illustration of divergent institutional arrangements, but it does not by itself identify every causal channel or establish that all differences between countries have the same explanation.

## China's transition: reforms, adoption, and unresolved explanation

The source describes China's move after the late 1970s from central planning toward market mechanisms: agricultural-market reforms, greater state-firm autonomy, space for private and collective enterprise, and incentives for foreign investment. It reports output-per-worker growth rising from 2.5% annually in 1952–77 to more than 9% afterward, attributing the high growth to both capital accumulation and technological progress.[^blanchard-049]

It contrasts China's transition with many Central and Eastern European transitions, where an initial collapse in the state sector was only partly offset by a slowly growing private sector. The source's proximate account is that China's more gradual state-sector decline was outweighed by private-sector growth. It records competing possible explanations—cultural norms, shorter experience of central planning, and continued Communist Party control that enabled experimentation and pacing—and does not resolve among them. It further argues that guarantees to foreign investors helped technology transfer and later domestic adaptation.[^blanchard-049]

The China discussion is source-era and descriptive. Its reported later-growth rate, institutional claims, and political assessment should not be read as current measurements or a general transition prescription.
