Heilbroner presents the continuity of society as a *social* problem of survival: humans survive only by cooperation, yet are strongly self-centered rather than equipped with inborn social instincts like ants, so the daily completion of thousands of interdependent tasks is fragile[^heilbroner-002].

# Synthesis

Society has found only three ways to guarantee its tasks get done and thereby endure. The *market system* — in which each does what is to his best monetary advantage under a single rule — is the most recent, paradoxical, and least understood, and its emergence constitutes an economic revolution that called the economists into being[^heilbroner-002].

# Three solutions to continuity

*   **Tradition.** Tasks handed down by custom and usage, son following father, preserved by religion and caste. In ancient Egypt every man was bound by religion to his father's occupation on pain of sacrilege; in India until recently occupations were assigned by caste; much of the unindustrialized world still assigns social tasks by birth[^heilbroner-002].
*   **Command (authoritarian rule).** Tasks ensured by edict and penalty of a supreme authority. Pyramids of Egypt and Soviet Five Year Plans alike were carried out not by custom or self-interest but by central command[^heilbroner-002].
*   **Market system.** Social continuity assured by allowing each individual to do as he sees fit, provided he follows the central rule: do what is to his best monetary advantage. The lure of gain, not the pull of tradition or whip of authority, steers people to tasks; interplay of individuals nevertheless results in necessary work getting done[^heilbroner-002].

For countless centuries only tradition or command operated, so no special field called economics was needed despite wide economic diversity (kings and commissars, dried codfish and stone money, communistic or ritualistic distribution). Theology, political theory, philosophy and history sufficed[^heilbroner-002].

# Why the market required a revolution

Heilbroner calls the shift to the market the most important revolution for shaping modern society — more disturbing than the French, American, or Russian Revolutions — because abandoning the security of custom and command for the dubious interplay of self-interest required centuries of convulsion, not a single event[^heilbroner-002].

Until the idea of widespread gain and the market mechanism itself gained acceptance, there was no puzzle for economists to explain. Unlike custom and command, it was not obvious that if everyone pursued only his own gain, society could endure or that dirty as well as plush jobs would be done[^heilbroner-002].

By about 1700, twenty-three years before Adam Smith's birth (1723), society had begun to heed new dicta — "Every man is naturally covetous of lucre," "No laws are prevalent against gaine," "Gaine is the Centre of the Circle of Commerce" — and the textbooks' "economic man" following his adding-machine brain had been born[^heilbroner-002].

> Direct quote: "The arrangement was called the 'market system,' and the rule was deceptively simple: each should do what was to his best monetary advantage."[^heilbroner-002]

# Why economists waited

Early social philosophy focused on politics — rights of kings, power temporal and spiritual — and treated the laboring substratum with indifference. Aristotle: "From the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule." Money and market matters were deemed too sweaty and vulgar for a gentleman scholar[^heilbroner-002]. Only when the struggle for riches became general, ubiquitous and vital did a general philosophy of riches become necessary. For two hundred years before Smith, pamphleteers spun contradictory models, until Smith's 1776 *Wealth of Nations* supplied the first true tableau of modern society and the vision through which generations saw themselves[^heilbroner-002][^heilbroner-003].
