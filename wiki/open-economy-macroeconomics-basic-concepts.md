# Open-economy macroeconomics — closed vs open economy, net exports and trade balance, and the increasing openness of the U.S. economy

An open economy interacts freely with other economies in world product markets (buying/selling goods and services) and world financial markets (buying/selling capital assets such as stocks and bonds); a closed economy does not interact with other economies[^mankiw-054]. Earlier macro chapters assume a closed economy to keep models simple — for the natural rate of unemployment or causes of inflation international effects can be safely ignored — but open-economy analysis introduces exports, imports, the trade balance, net capital outflow and exchange rates as core variables[^mankiw-054][^mankiw-055]. Benefits of trade carry over: like the Chapter 1 principle that trade can make everyone better off, openness lets countries specialize by comparative advantage and consume a wider variety of goods and services[^mankiw-054].

## 18-1 The international flows of goods and capital

### Definitions — flow of goods

- **Exports** — goods and services produced domestically and sold abroad (e.g., Boeing plane built in the U.S. and sold to Air France is a U.S. export and French import)[^mankiw-054].
- **Imports** — goods and services produced abroad and sold domestically (e.g., Volvo car made in Sweden and sold to a U.S. resident is a U.S. import and Swedish export)[^mankiw-054].
- **Net exports (NX)** — value of exports minus value of imports: `Net exports = Value of exports − Value of imports`[^mankiw-054]. Also called the **trade balance**[^mankiw-054].
- **Trade surplus** — NX > 0, exports > imports[^mankiw-054].
- **Trade deficit** — NX < 0, exports < imports[^mankiw-054]; cartoon caption frames a car purchase as "confronting this country's trade deficit with Japan"[^mankiw-054].
- **Balanced trade** — NX = 0[^mankiw-054].

### Six factors that influence exports, imports and net exports

Even before a formal trade-balance theory (developed next chapter), Mankiw lists[^mankiw-054]:

1. Tastes of consumers for domestic vs foreign goods
2. Prices of goods at home and abroad
3. Exchange rates — rates at which domestic currency buys foreign currency
4. Incomes of consumers at home and abroad
5. Cost of transporting goods country to country
6. Government policies toward international trade

As these variables change, so does the amount of international trade[^mankiw-054].

### The increasing openness of the U.S. economy (Case Study + Figure 1)

**Figure 1 — The Internationalization of the U.S. Economy** plots exports and imports as percent of GDP since 1950 (source: U.S. Department of Commerce)[^mankiw-054]. Both series track at 4–5% in the 1950s and fluctuate upward together, with imports (teal) slightly above exports (brown) from the mid-1970s onward, reaching roughly 13–15% for imports and 11–14% for exports in recent years (about three times the 1950s level), with a sharp dip around 2008–2009[^mankiw-054].

- In the 1950s imports and exports were typically between 4 and 5 percent of GDP; in recent years they have been about three times that level[^mankiw-054].
- As of 2015 the largest U.S. trading partner by combined imports+exports was China, followed by Canada, Mexico, Japan, Germany, South Korea, and the United Kingdom[^mankiw-054].

#### Why trade grew

1. **Transport improvements** — average merchant ship <10,000 tons in 1950 vs >100,000 tons today; long-distance jet 1958 and wide-body jet 1967 sharply cut air transport cost. Goods once necessarily local are now traded: cut flowers from Israel, summer fruits/vegetables from Southern Hemisphere in U.S. winter[^mankiw-054].
2. **Telecommunications** — first transatlantic telephone cable 1956; only 138 simultaneous North America–Europe conversations in 1966; today email makes contacting a customer abroad almost as easy as across town[^mankiw-054].
3. **Technology and composition** — goods with modern technology are often light and easy to transport (consumer electronics low weight per dollar value; film — Hollywood movie copies sent worldwide at almost zero cost and a major U.S. export); governments' trade policies also matter — economists have long believed free trade is mutually beneficial, and agreements such as NAFTA and GATT have gradually lowered tariffs, import quotas and other barriers; TPP with 11 Pacific Rim nations was under debate as book went to press[^mankiw-055].

### 18-1b The flow of financial resources: net capital outflow

Residents also participate in world financial markets. A U.S. resident with $25,000 could buy a car from Toyota (flow of goods) or buy stock in Toyota Corporation (flow of capital)[^mankiw-055].

- **Net capital outflow (NCO)** — also called **net foreign investment** — defined as[^mankiw-055]:

  ```
  NCO = Purchase of foreign assets by domestic residents − Purchase of domestic assets by foreigners
  ```

- Example: U.S. resident buys stock in Petrobras (Brazilian energy) increases first term → increases U.S. NCO; Japanese resident buys U.S. government bond increases second term → decreases U.S. NCO[^mankiw-055].
- **Two forms:**[^mankiw-055]
  - **Foreign direct investment** — actively managed (e.g., McDonald's opens fast-food outlet in Russia)
  - **Foreign portfolio investment** — more passive (e.g., American buys stock in a Russian corporation)
  - Both increase U.S. NCO because domestic resident buys foreign-located asset[^mankiw-055].
- **Sign:** NCO > 0 → domestic residents buying more foreign assets than foreigners buying domestic assets — capital flowing *out*; NCO < 0 → capital flowing *in* (capital inflow)[^mankiw-055].
- Theory of NCO determinants (developed next chapter) — preview of four variables[^mankiw-055]:
  - Real interest rates paid on foreign assets
  - Real interest rates paid on domestic assets
  - Perceived economic and political risks of holding assets abroad
  - Government policies that affect foreign ownership of domestic assets
- Illustration: U.S. investors comparing Mexican vs U.S. government bonds compare real interest rates but also default risk and restrictions Mexico imposes or might impose on foreign investors[^mankiw-055].

### 18-1c The equality of net exports and net capital outflow (NCO = NX)

Both NX and NCO measure an imbalance — NX in goods/services markets, NCO in asset markets — and for the economy as a whole they must always equal[^mankiw-055]:

```
NCO = NX
```

This is an accounting identity — true by definition and measurement — because every transaction affecting one side affects the other by exactly the same amount[^mankiw-055].

**Software-for-yen example (programmer sells software to Japan for ¥10,000 — U.S. export raises NX):**

- Stuff yen in mattress → domestic resident acquires foreign asset (Japanese currency) → NX rise matched by NCO rise[^mankiw-055].
- Use yen to buy Japanese stock or government bond → same: NCO rise exactly equals NX rise[^mankiw-055].
- Use yen to buy a Japanese good (Sony TV) → imports rise same amount as exports → balanced trade, NX unchanged, NCO unchanged — no foreign asset acquired[^mankiw-055].
- Exchange yen for dollars at bank → bank must do something with yen (buy Japanese asset → NCO, buy Japanese good → import, or sell yen to another American wanting such transaction) → in the end NX = NCO still holds[^mankiw-055].

**Generalization:**

- Walmart buys $50M clothing from China (U.S. import reduces NX): China could use $50M to buy U.S. government bonds → sale of bonds reduces U.S. NCO by same amount; or China could buy a Boeing plane → U.S. aircraft export balances clothing import, NX and NCO both unchanged[^mankiw-055].
- Trade surplus (NX > 0): selling more goods than buying, receiving foreign currency, must use it to buy foreign assets → capital flowing out (NCO > 0)[^mankiw-055].
- Trade deficit (NX < 0): buying more than selling, financing net purchase by selling assets abroad → capital flowing in (NCO < 0)[^mankiw-055].
- "The international flow of goods and services and the international flow of capital are two sides of the same coin"[^mankiw-055].

### 18-1d Saving, investment, and their relationship to the international flows

GDP components identity[^mankiw-055]:

```
Y = C + I + G + NX
```

National saving `S = Y − C − G` (income left after consumption and government purchases). Rearranged[^mankiw-055]:

```
Y − C − G = I + NX
S = I + NX
```

Since NX = NCO[^mankiw-055]:

```
S = I + NCO
Saving = Domestic investment + Net capital outflow
```

- In a closed economy NCO = 0 so S = I (as earlier in book)[^mankiw-055]; in an open economy saving has two uses: finance domestic capital or purchase foreign capital[^mankiw-055].
- Financial system stands between the two sides — e.g., Garcia family saves for retirement, deposits in mutual fund: fund buys General Motors stock → GM builds factory in Ohio (domestic investment); fund also buys Toyota stock → Toyota builds factory in Osaka (U.S. NCO)[^mankiw-055].
- When national saving exceeds domestic investment, NCO > 0 — nation uses saving to buy assets abroad; when investment exceeds saving, NCO < 0 — foreigners finance domestic investment by purchasing domestic assets[^mankiw-055].

#### Equivalent net-capital-inflow convention

Krugman and Wells define **net capital inflow** as foreign funds entering a country minus domestic funds leaving it: $NCI=IM-X=-NX$. Rearranging the GDP identity gives the equivalent open-economy identity $I=S_{National}+NCI$.[^krugman-ch10-053] It is the same accounting relationship as Mankiw's $S=I+NCO$, because $NCI=-NCO$; the sign convention states whether the cross-border balance is named as an inflow or an outflow.

Their government budget balance is $S_{Government}=T-G-TR$, where $TR$ is government transfers; national saving is $S_{National}=S_{Government}+S_{Private}$.[^krugman-ch10-053] This makes explicit the transfer adjustment that Mankiw incorporates by defining $T$ as taxes net of transfers.[^mankiw-ch13]

**2018 illustration.** The inspected figure reports U.S. investment spending of 21.0% of GDP, financed by private saving (24.1%) and a positive net capital inflow (2.4%), offset by a budget deficit (−5.7%). Germany's 21.8% investment spending was financed by private saving (27.2%) and a budget surplus (1.9%), offset by a capital outflow (NCI −7.3%). The source notes that rounded components need not exactly equal investment because of a statistical discrepancy.[^krugman-ch10-053]

### 18-1e Summing up — Table 1: three outcomes for an open economy

| | Trade Deficit | Balanced Trade | Trade Surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exports vs Imports | Exports < Imports | Exports = Imports | Exports > Imports |
| Net Exports | < 0 | = 0 | > 0 |
| Y vs C+I+G | Y < C+I+G | Y = C+I+G | Y > C+I+G |
| Saving vs Investment | Saving < Investment | Saving = Investment | Saving > Investment |
| Net Capital Outflow | < 0 | = 0 | > 0 |

[^mankiw-055].

Logic: surplus → Y > domestic spending → S > I → must send saving abroad → NCO > 0; deficit → Y < spending → S < I → must finance investment by selling assets abroad → NCO < 0; balanced trade → Y = spending, S = I, NCO = 0[^mankiw-055]. U.S. in recent years is the deficit case[^mankiw-055].

Krugman and Wells give the same high-level distinction: high investment spending relative to saving is associated with trade deficits, while low investment relative to saving is associated with surpluses. They caution that the balance alone has no simple relationship to an economy's productivity or the desirability of its products.[^krugman-ch6-038]

### Case Study: Is the U.S. trade deficit a national problem?

American press often called U.S. "world's largest debtor" after three decades of borrowing to finance large trade deficits[^mankiw-055].

**Figure 2 — National Saving, Domestic Investment, and Net Capital Outflow (% GDP since 1960)** — source: U.S. Department of Commerce[^mankiw-055]:

- Panel (a) saving and investment fluctuate; before 1980 they moved together, so NCO was typically small (−1 to 1% of GDP)[^mankiw-055].
- Since 1980 saving often fell well below investment, producing sizable trade deficits and substantial capital inflows (large negative NCO)[^mankiw-055].
- As identity requires, NCO always equals S − I[^mankiw-055]; fall in saving since 1980 reflected primarily in reduced NCO rather than reduced investment.

**Three historical episodes (not single cause):**

1. **Unbalanced fiscal policy (1980–1987):** capital inflow 0.5 → 3.0% GDP (Δ2.5pp) largely due to national saving fall of 2.7pp, explained by decline in public saving — government budget deficit from Reagan tax cuts + defense spending increase without nondefense cuts[^mankiw-055].
2. **Investment boom (1991–2000):** capital inflow 0.5 → 3.7% GDP (Δ3.2pp) with saving *rising* (budget switched to surplus) but investment 15.3 → 19.8% GDP on information-technology boom[^mankiw-055].
3. **Economic downturn and recovery (2000–2015):** capital inflow stayed large but masking big swings — 2000–2009 both saving and investment fell ~6pp (investment less profitable in tough times, saving fell via extraordinarily large budget deficits responding to downturn); 2009–2015 both rose ~3pp as economy recovered[^mankiw-055].

**Should we worry?** No easy answer — evaluate circumstances and alternatives[^mankiw-055]:

- Deficit induced by *fall in saving* (1980s): lower saving means less set aside for future — the deficit itself is not the problem; if saving fell without deficit, investment would have to fall, hurting capital stock, productivity and real wages; given saving decline, better to have foreigners invest than no one[^mankiw-055].
- Deficit induced by *investment boom* (1990s): borrowing to finance new capital that if it earns good return (higher production) economy can handle debt; if projects fail, debt looks less desirable in hindsight[^mankiw-055].
- Bottom line: like an individual, a nation can go into debt prudently or profligately; trade deficit is not a problem *per se* but can be a symptom[^mankiw-055].

#### Greece: capital inflows and an abrupt external adjustment

Krugman and Wells use Greece as an illustration rather than a general causal estimate. After adopting the euro in 1999, Greece ran large current-account deficits; the source reports a peak near 16% of GDP in the mid-2000s. It attributes the deficits and rapid expansion to optimistic foreign investors and capital inflows. After 2008, those inflows dried up; Greece could no longer sustain the large deficit, was near surplus by 2013, and suffered a severe recession.[^krugman-ch6-038]

The example reinforces that a changing trade/current-account balance can reflect changing saving, investment, and cross-border financing conditions, not a simple verdict on economic performance.[^krugman-ch6-038]

### Global loanable funds market — limiting case

If cross-border capital flows are sufficiently large and investors view foreign and domestic assets and liabilities as equivalent, flows narrow interest-rate differences and can equalize national rates: a **global loanable funds market**.[^krugman-ch10-054] In the source's two-country illustration, a 6% U.S. rate and 2% British rate induce lending from Britain to the United States; flows continue to a common 4%, where U.S. excess borrowing is a capital inflow and British excess lending is a capital outflow.[^krugman-ch10-054] Different currencies and expected exchange-rate movements make this a useful abstraction rather than a general description of actual financial markets.[^krugman-ch10-054]

## 18-2 The prices for international transactions: real and nominal exchange rates

International prices coordinate buyers/sellers just as domestic prices do. Two most important: nominal and real exchange rates[^mankiw-055].

### 18-2a Nominal exchange rates

- **Nominal exchange rate:** rate at which one currency trades for another — e.g., 80 yen per dollar (give bank $1 get ¥80)[^mankiw-055]. Bank actually posts slightly different buy/sell prices for profit; ignored here[^mankiw-055].
- Can be expressed both ways: 80 yen/dollar = 1/80 = 0.0125 dollar/yen; book always expresses as foreign currency per U.S. dollar[^mankiw-055].
- **Appreciation:** dollar buys more foreign currency (80→90 yen/dollar) — dollar appreciates, yen depreciates[^mankiw-055].
- **Depreciation:** dollar buys less foreign currency (80→70 yen/dollar) — dollar depreciates, yen appreciates[^mankiw-055].
- Media "strong/weak" dollar refers to recent changes in nominal rate: appreciating = strengthening[^mankiw-055].
- Many nominal rates (yen, pounds, pesos...); economists use indexes averaging many rates — like CPI for price level, exchange-rate index measures international value of currency; talk of dollar appreciating often refers to index[^mankiw-055].

### 18-2b Real exchange rates

- **Real exchange rate:** rate at which goods/services of one country trade for goods/services of another — e.g., if Swiss cheese twice as expensive as American cheese, real rate = ½ pound Swiss per pound American; like nominal, expressed as foreign item per domestic item, but item is good not currency[^mankiw-055].
- Real and nominal are closely related. Example: American rice $100/bushel, Japanese rice ¥16,000/bushel, nominal 80 yen/dollar → American rice = 8,000 yen/bushel → half as expensive → real rate = ½ bushel Japanese per bushel American[^mankiw-055].

**Formulas:**

```
Real exchange rate = (Nominal exchange rate × Domestic price) / Foreign price
Real exchange rate = (e × P) / P*   where P = U.S. price index (e.g., CPI basket), P* = foreign basket, e = nominal rate
```

Measures price of domestic basket relative to foreign basket[^mankiw-055].

- **Why it matters:** key determinant of exports/imports. Uncle Ben's choosing U.S. vs Japanese rice, or traveler choosing Miami vs Cancún hotel (dollar price vs peso price plus exchange rate) — both compare via real exchange rate[^mankiw-055].
- **Effect on net exports:** depreciation (fall) in U.S. real rate → U.S. goods cheaper relative to foreign → consumers at home and abroad buy more U.S. goods, fewer foreign goods → exports rise, imports fall → NX rises. Appreciation (rise) → U.S. goods more expensive → NX falls[^mankiw-055].

### FYI: The Euro

- During 1990s many European nations gave up francs, marks, lira etc. for common **euro** — circulating Jan 1, 2002 with 12 nations, 19 nations as of 2016; UK, Sweden, Denmark declined[^mankiw-055].
- Monetary policy for euro area set by **European Central Bank (ECB)** with representatives from all participants — issues euro and controls supply like Fed does dollars[^mankiw-055].
- **Benefit:** makes trade easier — avoids changing money crossing borders (analogy: if each of 50 U.S. states had its own currency, you'd constantly convert)[^mankiw-055].
- **Political motive:** reduce nationalistic feelings, foster shared history/destiny — single money for continent would help[^mankiw-055].
- **Cost:** only one monetary policy — if nations disagree about best policy they must agree rather than each going its own way; debate whether adoption was good[^mankiw-055].
- **2010–2015 crisis:** several nations had economic difficulties; Greece ran up large government debt, faced possible default, had to raise taxes and cut spending; some argued problems would have been easier with national monetary policy; possibility of Greece leaving euro and reintroducing own currency discussed but did not occur[^mankiw-055].

### ECB institutional analogy

Krugman/Wells dates the ECB's creation to January 1999, when 11 nations adopted the euro and delegated joint monetary policy; it reports Lithuania as the nineteenth euro adopter in 2015[^krugman-ch14-079]. It characterizes the ECB as analogous to the Fed's Board of Governors, with national central banks playing a role analogous to regional Federal Reserve Banks: national banks provide local services and conduct operations, while monetary-policy decisions sit with the ECB[^krugman-ch14-079]. Its Executive Board is compared with the Fed Board, and its Governing Council — Executive Board plus national-central-bank heads — with the FOMC[^krugman-ch14-079]. These are institutional comparisons and source-era membership facts, not a current governance directory.

## 18-3 A first theory of exchange-rate determination: purchasing-power parity

Exchange rates vary substantially: 1970 $1 = 3.65 German marks or 627 lira; 1998 $1 = 1.76 marks or 1,737 lira — dollar fell by more than half vs mark while more than doubling vs lira[^mankiw-055]. Many models emphasize different forces; simplest is **purchasing-power parity (PPP)** — a unit of any given currency should be able to buy same quantity of goods in all countries; believed to describe long-run forces[^mankiw-055].

### 18-3a Basic logic — law of one price and arbitrage

- **Law of one price:** a good must sell for same price in all locations, otherwise unexploited profit opportunities remain[^mankiw-055].
- Example: coffee $4/lb Seattle vs $5 Dallas → buy in Seattle sell in Dallas $1 profit — process of exploiting price differences is **arbitrage** — demand up in Seattle, supply up in Dallas, prices converge[^mankiw-055].
- Internationally: if dollar buys more coffee in U.S. than Japan, traders profit buying coffee in U.S. and selling in Japan — export drives U.S. price up and Japanese price down; opposite if dollar buys more in Japan → import drives opposite convergence[^mankiw-055].
- Leads to **purchasing-power parity:** currency must have same purchasing power in all countries — $1 buys same quantity in U.S. and Japan, ¥1 buys same in Japan and U.S.; parity = equality, purchasing power = value in terms of quantity of goods[^mankiw-055]; name describes theory well[^mankiw-055].

### 18-3b Implications of PPP — nominal rate reflects price levels and money

PPPP tells us the nominal exchange rate between two currencies depends on price levels in those countries. If a dollar buys the same quantity of goods in the U.S. (prices in dollars) as in Japan (prices in yen), then yen per dollar must reflect U.S. and Japanese prices. Example: coffee 500 yen in Japan and $5 in U.S. → nominal rate must be 100 yen per dollar (500 yen / $5)[^mankiw-056].

**Derivation:** Let P = U.S. price of basket (dollars), P* = Japanese basket (yen), e = nominal rate (yen per dollar)[^mankiw-056]:

- Purchasing power of $1 at home = 1/P[^mankiw-056]
- Purchasing power of $1 abroad: $1 → e yen → e/P* quantity[^mankiw-056]
- For equal purchasing power:

```
1/P = e / P*
1 = eP / P*
e = P* / P
```

- The middle expression `eP/P*` is the **real exchange rate**. If PPP always holds, purchasing power parity implies the real exchange rate is constant at 1 — the relative price of domestic and foreign goods cannot change[^mankiw-056].
- **Nominal rate = ratio of foreign price level to domestic price level** (foreign currency units per domestic currency unit)[^mankiw-056].

**Key implication:** nominal exchange rates change when price levels change. Price level adjusts to balance money supply and money demand (preceding chapter). Because nominal rate depends on price levels, it also depends on money supply and demand in each country. When a central bank increases money supply and raises the price level, that country's currency depreciates — money loses value both in goods it can buy and in foreign currency it can buy[^mankiw-056].

**Application — U.S. dollar vs German mark and Italian lira 1970–1998:** Question why dollar lost value vs mark but gained vs lira is answered by inflation differentials[^mankiw-056]:

- U.S. inflation 1970–1998: 5.3% per year[^mankiw-056]
- Germany: 3.5%[^mankiw-056]
- Italy: 9.6%[^mankiw-056]
- As U.S. prices rose relative to German prices, dollar fell vs mark; as U.S. prices fell relative to Italian prices, dollar rose vs lira[^mankiw-056].
- Germany and Italy now share the euro and a single monetary policy, so their inflation rates will be closely linked. Whether the dollar buys more or fewer euros 20 years hence depends on whether the European Central Bank produces more or less inflation than the Federal Reserve[^mankiw-056].

**CASE STUDY: The nominal exchange rate during a hyperinflation**

Macroeconomists rarely run controlled experiments — hyperinflations are natural experiments. Germany early 1920s hyperinflation shows money, prices, and nominal exchange rate moving together[^mankiw-056].

Figure 3 — Money, Prices, and Nominal Exchange Rate during German Hyperinflation Jan 1921–Dec 1924 (source: adapted from Thomas J. Sargent, "The End of Four Big Inflations," in Robert Hall, ed., *Inflation*, Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983, pp.41–93)[^mankiw-056]: money supply, price level, and nominal rate (U.S. cents per mark) move closely together — when money growth takes off, price level takes off and mark depreciates; when money supply stabilizes, price level and exchange rate stabilize[^mankiw-056].

Every hyperinflation shows same pattern: fundamental link among money, prices, and nominal exchange rate. Quantity theory explains money → price level; PPP explains price level → nominal exchange rate[^mankiw-056].

### 18-3c Limitations of PPP

PPP provides a simple model and works well for many long-term trends (U.S. dollar vs mark/lira) and major hyperinflation moves, but it is not completely accurate — exchange rates do not always move to keep a dollar's real value equal everywhere[^mankiw-056]. Two reasons:

1. **Many goods are not easily traded.** Example: haircuts more expensive in Paris than New York — travelers might avoid Paris haircuts and some haircutters might migrate, but arbitrage too limited to eliminate price differences; deviation persists, dollar buys less haircut in Paris[^mankiw-056].
2. **Even tradable goods are not always perfect substitutes.** Some consumers prefer German cars vs American cars; tastes can change. If German cars become more popular, demand drives German car price up relative to American car, but no profitable arbitrage arises because consumers do not view cars as equivalent[^mankiw-056].

Therefore both non-tradability and imperfect substitutability mean real exchange rates fluctuate over time. Nonetheless PPP is a useful first step: as real rate drifts from PPP level, incentive to move goods across borders grows; even if not completely fixing real rate, forces imply changes are most often small or temporary. Large and persistent nominal movements typically reflect changes in price levels at home and abroad[^mankiw-056].

Predicted and actual differ — international Big Mac arbitrage is not easy — yet they are usually in the same ballpark. PPP is not precise but often provides a reasonable first approximation[^mankiw-056].

QuickQuiz in text: Over past 20 years Mexico had high inflation and Japan low inflation — what happens to pesos per yen? PPP predicts Mexican price level rises faster → peso depreciates vs yen → number of pesos a yen buys rises[^mankiw-056].

### 18-4 Conclusion and chapter summary

The chapter's purpose was to develop basic open-economy concepts for the next chapter's model of what determines trade balance and exchange rates[^mankiw-056]:

- Trade balance relates to international capital flow; national saving can differ from domestic investment in an open economy — surplus must send capital abroad, deficit must experience capital inflow[^mankiw-056].
- Nominal exchange rate = relative price of currencies; real exchange rate = relative price of goods/services; implications and limitations of PPP as exchange-rate theory as above[^mankiw-056].
- Summary restated in book: net exports = exports − imports; net capital outflow = foreign assets domestic buys − domestic assets foreigners buy; every transaction exchanges asset for good/service, so NCO = NX always; saving = domestic investment + NCO; nominal depreciation/appreciation definitions vs real; PPP dollar should buy same quantity everywhere → nominal rate reflects price levels; high-inflation countries have depreciating currencies, low-inflation appreciating currencies[^mankiw-056].
- Key concepts glossary: closed economy, open economy, exports, imports, net exports, trade balance, trade surplus, trade deficit, balanced trade, net capital outflow, nominal exchange rate, appreciation, depreciation, real exchange rate, purchasing-power parity[^mankiw-056].
