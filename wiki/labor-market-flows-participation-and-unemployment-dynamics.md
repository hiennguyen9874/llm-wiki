# Labor-market flows, participation, and unemployment dynamics

The unemployment rate is a stock measure, so it alone cannot distinguish a dynamic labor market with many hires and separations from a stagnant one. U.S. CPS evidence in these sources shows large flows among employment, unemployment, and nonparticipation; it also shows that high unemployment coincides with both reduced job finding and greater job loss risk.[^blanchard-022][^blanchard-023]

## Stocks and rates

- The **civilian labor force** is people employed or actively looking for work; the **participation rate** is labor force divided by the noninstitutional civilian population. The **unemployment rate** is unemployed people divided by the labor force.[^blanchard-022]
- In the source's 2018 U.S. snapshot, 162.0 million people were in a 257.7-million noninstitutional civilian population (62% participation); 155.7 million were employed and 6.3 million unemployed (3.9% unemployment). The resulting employment-to-population ratio was 60.4%, not one minus unemployment because its denominator is the broader available population.[^blanchard-022][^blanchard-023]

## Flows reveal the labor market beneath the stocks

For the source's 1994–2018 monthly averages, the employment stock was 132.0 million and unemployment stock 8.6 million, while flows were still large: 1.8 million moved from employment to unemployment, 2.0 million from unemployment to employment, 3.7 million from employment to nonparticipation, 3.4 million in the reverse direction, and 1.8 million and 2.0 million respectively between unemployment and nonparticipation. An additional 3.0 million changed jobs within employment.[^blanchard-022]

About 3.8 million people left unemployment monthly (2.0 million to employment and 1.9 million to nonparticipation), roughly 44% of the unemployment stock in the source's average. Under a stable-stock approximation, average unemployment duration is the inverse of this monthly exit proportion, hence roughly two to three months. This average does not imply that every spell is short, and the source notes that duration rises when unemployment is high.[^blanchard-022]

Flows into and out of the labor force were likewise much larger than school-entry and retirement flows. The source reports 5.5 million monthly exits and 5.4 million entries, versus about 450,000 new entrants and 350,000 retirees, consistent with many nonparticipants moving between availability and nonparticipation rather than making a permanent exit.[^blanchard-023]

## Unemployment varies through both hazards

In downturns firms first tend to curb hiring and then, if necessary, lay off workers. Consequently, higher unemployment is associated with a lower probability that an unemployed person finds work and a higher probability that an employed person becomes unemployed.[^blanchard-023]

The source's 1996–2018 plots illustrate both: with unemployment near 10% in 2010, about 17% of unemployed workers became employed within a month, versus 28% in 2007; the monthly probability of employed workers becoming unemployed also rose markedly in high-unemployment periods.[^blanchard-023]

## Measurement boundary

Some people classified as outside the labor force report wanting a job and may accept one despite not actively searching. Thus the headline unemployment rate is not a complete estimate of people available for work; the employment rate supplies a complementary indicator. This is a classification limitation, not a basis for treating all nonparticipants as unemployed.[^blanchard-023]
