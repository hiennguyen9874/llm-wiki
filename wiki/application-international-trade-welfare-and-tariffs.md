# Application: International Trade — Welfare Analysis, Winners and Losers, Tariffs and Import Quotas

Free trade moves the domestic price to the **world price**; if domestic price before trade is below world price the country exports, if above it imports — a test of comparative advantage — and in either case total surplus rises by a triangle D because winners' gains exceed losers' losses, while a tariff on an importing country raises price by the tariff, transfers surplus to producers and government (revenue E), and creates deadweight loss D+F from overproduction and underconsumption, with import quotas producing identical welfare effects except that license-holder profit replaces government revenue[^mankiw-030].

## 9-1 Determinants of trade — Isoland textile market

- **Motivating question:** how does international trade affect well-being, who gains/loses, and how do gains compare to losses — illustrated by US textiles/clothing now largely imported after foreign low-cost competition closed domestic factories[^mankiw-030].
- **Tools:** supply, demand, equilibrium, consumer and producer surplus developed in prior chapters apply to trade[^mankiw-030]; Chapter 3 comparative advantage showed specialization benefits but not how markets achieve or distribute them[^mankiw-030].
- **Baseline without trade:** Isoland's textile market isolated by decree; price balances domestic supply and demand only; consumer + producer surplus at intersection measures total benefits[^mankiw-030].
- **Three policy questions posed to Isolandian economists:** (1) what happens to domestic price and quantity sold if trade allowed, (2) who gains/loses and whether gains exceed losses, (3) should a tariff be part of policy[^mankiw-030].

### World price and comparative advantage

- **World price** — price prevailing in world markets[^mankiw-030].
- If world price > domestic price before trade → country will export once trade permitted (producers seek higher foreign price); if world price < domestic price → country will import (consumers buy cheaper foreign good)[^mankiw-030].
- Domestic price before trade reflects **opportunity cost** of the good: low domestic price = low cost = comparative advantage in that good relative to world; high domestic price = foreign comparative advantage[^mankiw-030].
- Trade ultimately based on comparative advantage — each nation specializes in what it does best[^mankiw-030].

> QuickQuiz (Autarka): wool suit 3 oz gold in Autarka vs 2 oz in neighbors → if Autarka allows free trade it will **import** suits because world price is lower and neighbors have comparative advantage[^mankiw-030].

## 9-2 Winners and losers from trade — small-economy assumption

- **Small-economy assumption:** Isoland is small relative to world, so its trade policy does not affect world price; Isolandians are **price takers** — can buy/sell any quantity at world price; horizontal rest-of-world supply/demand at world price is perfectly elastic[^mankiw-030].
- Assumption simplifies but basic lessons unchanged for large economy[^mankiw-030]; explicitly justified by Chapter 2 modeling lesson on simplifying assumptions[^mankiw-030].

### 9-2a Exporting country — domestic price < world price

- Once trade allowed, domestic price rises to world price (no seller accepts less, no buyer pays more)[^mankiw-030].
- At world price: quantity supplied (supply curve) > quantity demanded (demand curve); difference = **exports**; rest-of-world demand viewed as horizontal at world price[^mankiw-030].

**Welfare changes (Figure 2):**

|  | Before trade | After trade | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A + B | A | −B |
| Producer surplus | C | B + C + D | +(B + D) |
| Total surplus | A + B + C | A + B + C + D | +D |

[^mankiw-030]

- Producers gain B + D, consumers lose B, net **+D** — trade raises total well-being[^mankiw-030].
- Conclusions: (1) when country becomes exporter, domestic producers better off, consumers worse off; (2) gains exceed losses so nation as a whole gains[^mankiw-030].

### 9-2b Importing country — domestic price > world price

- Domestic price falls to world price; quantity demanded > quantity supplied; difference = **imports**; horizontal rest-of-world supply at world price[^mankiw-030].

**Welfare changes (Figure 3):**

|  | Before trade | After trade | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A | A + B + D | +(B + D) |
| Producer surplus | B + C | C | −B |
| Total surplus | A + B + C | A + B + C + D | +D |

[^mankiw-030]

- Consumers gain B + D, producers lose B, net **+D**[^mankiw-030].
- Parallel conclusions: importer → consumers better off, producers worse off; total surplus rises by D[^mankiw-030].

### Trade can make everyone better off — with a political caveat

- Restates Ch.1 Ten Principles: **Trade can make everyone better off** — in either case gains exceed losses so winners could compensate losers and still be better off[^mankiw-030].
- In practice compensation is rare; opening to trade expands the pie but can leave some with smaller slice[^mankiw-030].
- Political implication: debate is contentious because policy creates winners and losers; losers often better organized and lobby for tariffs or quotas, so nations sometimes forgo gains[^mankiw-030].

## 9-2c Effects of a tariff

- **Tariff** — tax on imported goods; irrelevant if country is exporter (no imports to tax); analysis focuses on importer case[^mankiw-030].
- Tariff raises price of imported good above world price by amount of tariff; domestic sellers competing with imports can now sell at world price + tariff; both imported and domestic price rise, moving market toward no-trade equilibrium[^mankiw-030].
- Behavior: tariff reduces domestic quantity demanded Q₁ᴰ → Q₂ᴰ and raises domestic quantity supplied Q₁ˢ → Q₂ˢ; quantity of imports falls[^mankiw-030].

**Welfare with tariff (Figure 4):**

|  | Before tariff | After tariff | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer surplus | A + B + C + D + E + F | A + B | −(C + D + E + F) |
| Producer surplus | G | C + G | +C |
| Government revenue | None (0) | E (= tariff × after-tariff imports) | +E |
| Total surplus | A + B + C + D + E + F + G | A + B + C + E + G | −(D + F) |

[^mankiw-030]

- Total surplus falls by **D + F** — the **deadweight loss** of the tariff[^mankiw-030].
- Two distortions identified: **D** = overproduction — incremental units cost more to make domestically than buying at world price but tariff makes them profitable; **F** = underconsumption — tariff induces consumers to cut purchases even though they value incremental units above world price[^mankiw-030].
- Parallels tax analysis: like most taxes, tariff distorts incentives and pushes resource allocation from optimum[^mankiw-030].

### FYI: Import quotas — another way to restrict trade

- **Import quota** — limit on quantity that can be imported; conclusion stated: quotas much like tariffs — reduce imports, raise domestic price, decrease consumer welfare, increase producer welfare, cause deadweight losses[^mankiw-030].
- Only difference: tariff raises revenue for government, quota creates surplus for license holders — profit = domestic price − world price[^mankiw-030].
- If government charges license fee equal to price gap, profit is paid to government and quota works exactly like tariff (same consumer surplus, producer surplus, and government revenue)[^mankiw-030].
- In practice licenses rarely sold: example US pressuring Japan to "voluntarily" limit car exports — licenses allocated to Japanese firms, surplus accrues abroad; from US welfare view this quota is **worse than a tariff** because revenue goes to foreign producers rather than domestic government, though both cause same price increase and deadweight loss[^mankiw-030].

## 9-2d Lessons for trade policy — letter to the president

Economists' answers to the three questions[^mankiw-030]:

1. **Price:** once trade allowed, Isolandian price driven to world price. World price > domestic → price rises, consumption falls, production rises, country becomes exporter (has comparative advantage). World price < domestic → price falls, consumption rises, production falls, country becomes importer (others have comparative advantage)[^mankiw-030].
2. **Winners/losers:** depends on price direction — price rise → producers gain, consumers lose; price fall → consumers gain, producers lose; in both cases gains > losses so free trade raises total Isolandian welfare[^mankiw-030].
3. **Tariff:** matters only if importer; moves economy toward no-trade equilibrium, causes deadweight loss like most taxes, improves producer welfare and raises government revenue but losses to consumers more than offset; best policy for efficiency is free trade without tariff[^mankiw-030].

## 9-2e Other benefits of international trade

Standard surplus analysis understates gains; additional benefits[^mankiw-030][^mankiw-031]:

- **Increased variety:** goods not identical across countries (German vs American beer); free trade expands choice[^mankiw-030].
- **Lower costs through economies of scale:** some goods cheap only at large quantity; small domestic market prevents full scale; world markets allow realization of economies of scale[^mankiw-030].
- **Increased competition:** shielded firms may have market power and price above competitive levels (a market failure); opening trade fosters competition and lets invisible hand work better[^mankiw-030].
- **Enhanced flow of ideas / technology diffusion:** transfer of technological advances often linked to trading goods that embody them; poor agricultural nation learns about computer revolution best by buying computers abroad rather than making domestically[^mankiw-031].
- If those effects counted, Isoland economists' pro-trade advice would be even more forceful[^mankiw-031].
- See also [Trade as a Tool for Economic Development](trade-as-tool-for-economic-development-warhol-poverty-and-inequality.md) for Warholian poverty/inequality evidence reinforcing these benefits.

## 9-3 Arguments for restricting trade

Isoland narrative: president notes trade would make domestic price fall and hurt textile producers; industry urges protection[^mankiw-031]. Textbook evaluates five classic protectionist arguments; cartoon “You like protectionism as a ‘working man. How about as a consumer?” illustrates worker-versus-consumer interest[^mankiw-031] (image 000215 not inspected beyond caption).

### 9-3a The jobs argument

- **Claim:** free trade destroys domestic jobs — textiles price falls, quantity produced in Isoland falls, textile employment falls; opponents often skeptical that trade creates jobs and claim everything can be made cheaper abroad so no profitable employment remains[^mankiw-032].
- **Reply:** free trade creates jobs simultaneously as it destroys them — when Isolandians buy textiles abroad, those countries obtain resources to buy other Isoland goods; workers move to industries where Isoland has **comparative advantage**; transition may impose short-run hardship but raises living standards overall[^mankiw-032].
- **Comparative vs absolute advantage:** gains from trade rest on comparative, not absolute, advantage (Ch.3); even if one country is better at producing everything, each still gains and workers eventually find jobs in its comparative-advantage industry[^mankiw-032].
- This is the **job-counting fallacy** (seen-jobs vs unseen-jobs) — see [Bastiat's seen and unseen and macro policy fallacies](bastiat-seen-unseen-and-policy-fallacies.md).

### 9-3b The national-security argument

- **Claim:** industries vital to defense (e.g., steel for guns and tanks) must be protected; free trade creates dependence and risk of foreign supply cutoff in wartime[^mankiw-032].
- **Reply:** economists acknowledge legitimate security concerns but fear misuse by producers seeking rents[^mankiw-032].
- **Screening test:** be wary when claim comes from industry representatives rather than defense establishment; firms have incentive to exaggerate defense role to obtain protection at consumers’ expense; generals may see differently — when military is *consumer* of the industry’s output, cheaper imports let it stockpile weapons at lower cost[^mankiw-032].

### 9-3c The infant-industry argument

- **Claim:** new (or older adjusting) industries need **temporary** protection to get started/mature and later compete with foreign firms[^mankiw-032].
- **Skepticism — implementation:** government must pick which industries will eventually be profitable and whether consumer-cost of protection is justified — “picking winners” is extraordinarily difficult, made harder by political process awarding protection to politically powerful; “temporary” protection is often hard to remove once granted[^mankiw-032].
- **Skepticism — principle:** if long-run profitability is expected, owners should be willing to incur temporary losses to obtain eventual profits; protection is not necessary — start-up firms routinely incur losses and succeed without trade protection[^mankiw-032].

### 9-3d The unfair-competition argument

- **Claim:** free trade is desirable only if all countries play by same rules; different laws/subsidies make competition unfair — e.g., Neighborland subsidizes textiles with large tax breaks, so Isoland should protect its textile industry[^mankiw-032].
- **Reply:** Isoland is *not* hurt by buying at subsidized price — Isolandian producers suffer but consumers benefit more; gains of consumers from low price exceed losses of producers; the case for free trade is unchanged[^mankiw-032].
- Subsidy is bad policy for Neighborland but burden falls on Neighborland taxpayers; Isoland benefits from subsidized price and “perhaps should send Neighborland a thank-you note”[^mankiw-032].

### 9-3e The protection-as-a-bargaining-chip argument

- **Claim:** support free trade but threaten trade restrictions to bargain away foreign restrictions — e.g., Isoland threatens textile tariff unless Neighborland removes wheat tariff; if threat succeeds, result is freer trade[^mankiw-032].
- **Reply:** threat may not work, leaving a choice between two bad options: carry out threat and reduce own welfare, or back down and lose international prestige; country would likely wish it had never made threat[^mankiw-032].

### ASK THE EXPERTS — Labor and environmental standards as trade leverage

- **Expert consensus:** "Refusing to liberalize trade unless partner countries adopt new labor or environmental rules is a bad policy, because even if the new standards would reduce distortions on some dimensions, such a policy involves threatening to maintain large distortions in the form of restricted trade."[^mankiw-033]
- Implication: conditional liberalization trades one distortion for a larger one; consistent with textbook's view that economists favor free trade even when partners have different standards[^mankiw-033].

### Trade agreements and the World Trade Organization

- **Two approaches to free trade:** unilateral removal of restrictions on own initiative (Great Britain in 19th century, Chile and South Korea in recent years) vs multilateral reduction through bargaining while partners do same[^mankiw-032].
- **Multilateral examples:** **NAFTA** (1993, US–Mexico–Canada) lowering barriers among members; **GATT** — continuing series of negotiations among many countries to promote free trade (with US help)[^mankiw-032].
- **GATT to WTO:** GATT founded after World War II in response to high tariffs of the Great Depression of the 1930s (widely believed to have deepened worldwide hardship); GATT reduced average tariff among members from ~40% after WWII to ~5% today; rules now enforced by **World Trade Organization (WTO)**, established 1995, headquartered Geneva, Switzerland; as of 2015, 162 countries, >97% of world trade; functions: administer trade agreements, provide forum for negotiations, handle disputes[^mankiw-033].
> Coverage note: image 000220 (GATT/WTO graphic) not visually inspected; prose fully captured[^mankiw-033].

#### Pros and cons of the multilateral approach

- **Pro — freer trade potential:** multilateral can achieve freer trade than unilateral because it reduces restrictions abroad as well as at home[^mankiw-033].
- **Con — negotiation failure risk:** if international negotiations fail, result could be *more* restricted trade than under unilateral approach[^mankiw-033].
- **Pro — political economy advantage:** producers are fewer/better organized than consumers and wield greater influence; reducing Isolandian textile tariff alone is politically difficult (textile firms oppose, dispersed textile buyers hard to organize); if Neighborland simultaneously promises to cut its wheat tariff, powerful Isolandian wheat farmers back the agreement — multilateral logrolling wins support that unilateral cannot[^mankiw-033].

## IN THE NEWS — Should the Winners from Free Trade Compensate the Losers? (Landsburg)

- **Source:** Steven E. Landsburg, “What to Expect When You’re Free Trading,” *New York Times*, Jan. 16, 2008, reproduced as Mankiw Ch.9 In the News box[^mankiw-032].
- **Prompt:** politicians/pundits often say government should help workers hurt by trade by paying for retraining; Landsburg makes opposite case — *should* winners compensate losers?[^mankiw-032].
- **Economists’ agreed fact:** when American jobs are outsourced, Americans as a group are net winners — lower-wage loss more than offset by lower-price gains; winners can *more than afford* to compensate losers (the +D triangle logic)[^mankiw-032].
- **Landsburg’s moral answer — “Um, no.”** Even if you lost your job, it is churlish to blame the phenomenon that elevated you above subsistence since birth; if world owes you compensation for downside of trade, what do you owe world for enjoying upside? Everyone benefits from opportunity to trade freely — without it you would grow own food, make own clothes, rely on grandmother’s home remedies vs trained physician[^mankiw-032].
- **Isolated-instance framing:** someone might say consider only the *marginal* agreement that hurts particular workers and ask what is owed to them; Landsburg replies via moral-instinct analogy test[^mankiw-032].
- **Everyday analogies:** finding cheaper shampoo on the Web — do you owe your local pharmacist compensation? Move to cheaper apartment — compensate landlord? Eat at McDonald’s — compensate diner next door? Public policy should not advance moral instincts we reject daily[^mankiw-032].
- **Distinguishing displaced workers?** Possible distinction: pharmacists/landlords always faced cutthroat competition and knew what they were getting into, while tariffs/quotas led manufacturing workers to expect protection, develop skills, and now face rug pulled out[^mankiw-032].
- **Bullying rebuttal:** decades of schoolyard bullying was profitable and bullies built skills; if rules toughen to make bullying unprofitable, must we compensate bullies? Bullying and protectionism share use of force (directly or via law) to enrich someone at your involuntary expense — forced to pay $20/hour to American for goods you could buy from Mexican for $5 is extortion; a free-trade agreement that lets you buy from Mexican is liberation[^mankiw-032].
- **Link to welfare analysis:** Landsburg accepts standard surplus result (winners could compensate losers and remain better off) but denies it creates a moral mandate for taxpayer-subsidized retraining — compensability does not equal obligation; textbook juxtaposes this libertarian/moral view with standard political-economy observation that losers are organized and compensation is rare[^mankiw-030][^mankiw-032].

## 9-4 Conclusion — Economists vs the public and trade as technology

- **Public vs economists:** 2015 NBC News/*Wall Street Journal* poll — only 29% of Americans said free trade helped the US, 34% said it hurt (rest no difference/unsure); by contrast economists overwhelmingly support free trade as efficient allocation that raises living standards at home and abroad[^mankiw-033].
- **US interstate experiment:** United States has allowed unrestricted trade among states throughout its history — Florida oranges, Alaska oil, California wine illustrate specialization; Americans would not enjoy current living standards if confined to state-produced goods; world could similarly benefit from free trade among countries[^mankiw-033].
- **Isoland parable — trade as technology:** President ignores economists, stays in autarky; an inventor claims mysterious low-cost textile process using only wheat, little labor; living standards rise, displaced textile workers find farm/other jobs, displacement seen as normal technological progress; reporter discovers fraud — inventor smuggled wheat abroad for textiles, discovering only gains from trade; shutting the operation raises textile prices, lowers living standards; moral: trade is a technology — turning wheat into textiles via world markets — and the inventor "was just an economist"[^mankiw-033].
- Pedagogical point: parallels earlier claim that trade can make everyone better off; opposition reflects visible job loss vs invisible gains and political organization, not total-surplus arithmetic[^mankiw-030][^mankiw-033].

## Key Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| World price | Price prevailing in world markets; determines import vs export direction by comparison to pre-trade domestic price[^mankiw-030] |
| Small economy / price taker | Economy whose actions have little effect on world markets; takes world price as given; rest-of-world supply/demand perfectly elastic at world price[^mankiw-030] |
| Exports | Domestic quantity supplied − domestic quantity demanded at world price when world price > domestic price[^mankiw-030] |
| Imports | Domestic quantity demanded − domestic quantity supplied at world price when world price < domestic price[^mankiw-030] |
| Tariff | Tax on imported goods; raises domestic price by tariff amount, reduces imports, moves toward autarky, creates deadweight loss D+F[^mankiw-030] |
| Deadweight loss of tariff | Fall in total surplus D + F — D from overproduction, F from underconsumption[^mankiw-030] |
| Government revenue from tariff | E = tariff × quantity of after-tariff imports[^mankiw-030] |
| Import quota | Quantity limit on imports; welfare-equivalent to tariff except surplus goes to license holders rather than government unless license fee charged[^mankiw-030] |
| Total surplus | Consumer surplus + producer surplus (+ government revenue when tax/quota present); rises by D with free trade vs autarky, falls by D+F with tariff vs free trade[^mankiw-030] |

## Contradictions

No internal factual contradiction, but an explicit normative tension is preserved:
- **Standard welfare claim:** free trade raises total surplus (+D) so winners *could* compensate losers and remain better off; political-economy discussion notes compensation is rare and losers often organize to block gains[^mankiw-030].
- **Landsburg’s moral claim:** rejects that compensability creates a mandate to compensate — trade’s ubiquitous lifetime benefits undermine losers’ claim, everyday market-switch analogies (pharmacist/landlord/diner) show we do not normally compensate, and protectionism-as-extortion/bullying analogy reframes liberalization as ending coercion rather than inflicting harm[^mankiw-032].
- Textbook presents Landsburg as an opinion counterpoint, not as disproving surplus arithmetic; both views agree on net gains, diverge on moral obligation and policy (taxpayer retraining)[^mankiw-032]. Efficiency vs equality (pie size vs slice) tradeoff flagged in Ch.7 recurs here without new factual dispute.
