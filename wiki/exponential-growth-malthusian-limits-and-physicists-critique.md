# Exponential growth, Malthusian limits, and the physicists' critique

Physicists' warnings that any exponential economic growth must eventually hit physical impossibility rest on valid arithmetic but misapply it to GDP, repeating a classic economic error first formalized by Malthus[^harford-growth-limits].

## The exponential alarm

- **Compounding logic.** Any growth proportional to its current level — even 0.0001% per year — is exponential, like compound interest. Einstein is famously (but probably falsely) quoted as calling compound interest the most powerful force in the universe[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Rice on the chessboard.** Reward of 1 grain on square 1, 2 on square 2, 4 on square 3, doubling for 64 squares, sums to many times annual world rice production though early squares seem modest. Popularized by Albert Bartlett's "The Most Important Video You'll Ever See" (~5 million views) with the message "the greatest shortcoming of the human race is our inability to understand the exponential function"[^harford-growth-limits].
- **Tom Murphy's update.** Blog post "Exponential Economist Meets Finite Physicist": at 2.3% annual energy growth — below historical rates but enough for tenfold growth per century — waste heat alone (not greenhouse gases) would boil the planet in about four centuries, regardless of energy source (fossil, solar, or "fairy dust"), by thermodynamics. Like the chessboard, early growth feels benign until a sudden impossible square[^harford-growth-limits].

Harford notes economists understand this arithmetic perfectly; it is not new to economics[^harford-growth-limits].

## Malthus as first exponential economist

Reverend Thomas Malthus (d. ~1834), one of the first called an economist, argued in *An Essay on the Principle of Population* that population grows exponentially (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) while food production grows arithmetically (10, 20, 30, 40, 50), so the former inevitably overtakes the latter at any positive growth rate[^harford-growth-limits].

Malthus's math was incontrovertible but assumptions failed: technological progress outpaced population growth in the short term, and population growth has since slowed dramatically toward stabilization. Zero population growth is not obviously unsustainable, and economics has largely ridiculed Malthus for two centuries for timing and assumption errors[^harford-growth-limits]. Lesson: exponential arithmetic does not by itself predict when, whether, or how limits bind; assumptions about the exponent and substitutes matter.

## Why the physicists' inference about GDP fails

The critique looks at exponential growth in *physical* processes — heating, cooling, lighting, movement — which is understandable for physicists but conflates energy/material throughput with economic value. GDP measures what people are willing to pay for, not necessarily energy or resource use; the two have historically correlated since the industrial revolution but have no logical necessity to do so, and evidence suggests decoupling has begun (see [Decoupling economic growth from energy and material throughput](decoupling-economic-growth-from-energy-and-material-throughput.md))[^harford-growth-limits].

## Resource scarcity and environmental constraints

Krugman/Wells similarly reports that repeated resource-exhaustion alarms—from Jevons’s 1865 coal warning through the Club of Rome and early-2000s “peak oil”—had not materialized at the time of writing, in part because technology changed both supply and substitutes. It cites post-2005 fracking and falling wind- and solar-electricity costs. The source’s economic mechanism is that scarcity raises prices, which encourages conservation and substitutes; it uses US shifts toward fuel-efficient cars after the 1970s oil-price increases as an illustration.[^krugman-ch9-050]

This is not a claim that growth is environmentally costless. The text defines sustainable long-run growth as growth that continues despite limited natural resources and with less environmental harm, and distinguishes resource scarcity—which price incentives often address—from environmental problems, which it says require effective political action. It leaves the latter unresolved.[^krugman-ch9-050]
