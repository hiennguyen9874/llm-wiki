# Monitoring Inflation in the Internet Age — Billion Prices Project and Google Price Index

Web-scraped price indexes — the MIT Billion Prices Project (BPP) and Google Price Index (GPI) — offer daily, low-cost, web-basket alternatives to the Bureau of Labor Statistics' $234-million, clipboard-based monthly CPI, yet their close tracking of the CPI so far confirms rather than overturns the official measure's accuracy while adding higher frequency and micro-price insights[^mankiw-038-news].

## The BLS baseline — how CPI is collected

- At ~23,000 retailers and businesses in 90 U.S. cities, hundreds of government workers find and mark down prices on very precise products each month[^mankiw-038-news].
- Example specification for a motel room: occupancy — two adults; type — deluxe room; classification/location — ocean view, room 306; time — weekend; length — one night; bathroom — one full bathroom; kitchen — none; TV — one, includes free movie channel; telephone — one, free local calls; air-conditioned — yes; meals — breakfast; parking — free self parking; transportation to airport — no charge; recreation — indoor and outdoor pool, private beach, three tennis courts, exercise room[^mankiw-038-news].
- Panoply includes wine, takeaway meals, bedroom furniture, surgical procedures, pet dogs, college tuition, cigarettes, haircuts, funerals — forms collated, checked, input into spreadsheets, weighted by spending share (rent > cereal), adjusted for product improvements and spending-habit changes, distilled to one master number: the Consumer Price Index[^mankiw-038-news].
- Why it matters: inflation signals economic health — high inflation erodes savers' purchasing power, deflation encourages waiting and saving, volatile inflation impedes business/consumer decisions; government indexes Social Security benefits and TIPS bond interest to it[^mankiw-038-news].
- Costs and lags: ~$234 million per year; weekslong lag between checking and announcement; published only 12 times a year though prices change continuously; methodology described as clunky and archaic given online price availability and shift of shopping to the web[^mankiw-038-news].

## MIT Billion Prices Project (BPP)

- Started 2007 by economists Roberto Rigobon and Alberto Cavallo (MIT); unveiled the Billion Prices Project last month relative to the December 20, 2010 Slate article[^mankiw-038-news].
- Scale: 5 million items sold by 300 online retailers in 70 countries; for the United States about 500,000 prices[^mankiw-038-news].
- Methodology differences:
  - Basket = whatever can be bought on the web (books heavily represented online; cats not)[^mankiw-038-news].
  - Simple average of all prices culled online — no weighting by household spending share, unlike BLS[^mankiw-038-news].
- Results: has tracked the CPI closely[^mankiw-038-news].
- Advantages: daily frequency giving better sense of direction; allows minute, day-to-day price-change examination[^mankiw-038-news].
- Findings cited: Black Friday discounts had smaller effect on average prices in 2010 than in 2009, contrary to reports of deeper discounting; retailers change prices less often but by larger percentage amounts than economists previously thought[^mankiw-038-news].

## Google Price Index (GPI)

- Pet project of Google chief economist Hal Varian; idea came while searching for a pepper grinder online; reported by the Financial Times[^mankiw-038-news].
- Uses Google's vast database of Web prices to construct a constantly updated measure[^mankiw-038-news].
- Status at writing: Google had not decided whether to publish and had not released methodology[^mankiw-038-news].
- Preliminary finding: tracked CPI closely, though showed periods of deflation — prices actually falling — where CPI did not[^mankiw-038-news].

## So do we need to replace CPI?

- Question: should government update methods to account for new pricing trends, rejigger formula, update more frequently? Answer might be yes — economists have reformed CPI before[^mankiw-038-news].
- Defense of CPI: stable, tested, consistent over time since methodology doesn't change much[^mankiw-038-news].
- Remarkably, Google and BPP indices confirm accuracy of old-fashioned CPI, tracking it closely rather than showing it off-base[^mankiw-038-news].
- Argument for more measures, not just better/newer ones: BLS already publishes multiple inflation rates — notably "core inflation" (outside volatile food and energy) and dozens of other measures — web-based yardsticks provide additional alternatives and opportunities to check CPI accuracy and make new findings[^mankiw-038-news].
- Implication: detective-like government workers painstakingly checking prices on clipboards get to stay in work for now[^mankiw-038-news].

## Additional CPI measurement context in same source

- Section notes BLS had already adopted several technical changes in response to 1990s studies concluding CPI overstated inflation by ~1 percentage point per year; bias now believed about half as large[^mankiw-038-news].
- Government programs use CPI to adjust for overall price level; Social Security recipients get annual increases tied to CPI — some economists suggest reducing automatic benefit increases to correct measurement problems[^mankiw-038-news].

## Later textbook case — independent, faster estimates

Krugman/Wells describes the BPP as an independent check where official statistics cannot be trusted: for Argentina during 2007–2016, the source reports a mid-2015 BPP estimate of 27.18% annual inflation versus an official 14.85%. In its U.S. comparison, the respective reported rates were −0.60% and 0.17%.[^krugman-ch7-042] These are historical illustrations, not current estimates or a general verdict on any statistical agency.

The source says PriceStats (owned by State Street Bank) sold BPP-based, real-time estimates to business subscribers a few weeks before official CPI releases. Its distinction is speed, not proof that web prices can replace a representative, spending-weighted CPI: online prices can miss goods, services, and weights in household expenditure.[^krugman-ch7-042]
