# Asset Valuation and Market Efficiency — Fundamental Analysis, Efficient Markets Hypothesis, and Market Irrationality

Having built the two tools of finance — time (present value) and risk — the value of a share of stock is the present value of the cash flows its owner will receive (dividends plus final sale price)[^mankiw-ch14-045]. **Fundamental analysis** estimates that present value from business determinants (demand, competition, capital, labor, regulation)[^mankiw-ch14-045]; the **efficient markets hypothesis** holds that equilibrium prices already reflect all publicly available information, so price equals the best available estimate of value and changes follow a **random walk** driven only by unpredictable news[^mankiw-ch14-045]. Evidence — stock returns' near-zero serial correlation and the fact that 81% of active mutual funds underperformed a broad index by 0.89pp per year over the 15 years ending February 2016 — supports the hypothesis, though Keynesian "animal spirits," Greenspan's "irrational exuberance," and speculative bubbles raise a psychological counterview that remains debated[^mankiw-ch14-045].

## 14-3 Asset Valuation

Introductory framing: supply and demand determine stock price, but willingness to pay depends on perceived value[^mankiw-ch14-045].

- Value of a stock = present value of stream of dividend payments plus final sale price; both depend on company's ability to earn profits[^mankiw-ch14-045].

## 14-3a Fundamental Analysis

- **fundamental analysis:** detailed analysis of a company to estimate its value[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Stock classification: **undervalued** (price < value → bargain), **overvalued** (price > value), **fairly valued** (price = value)[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Profitability factors analysts consider: demand for product, competition, capital in place, unionization, customer loyalty, government regulations and taxes, and so on[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Three ways to implement: do research yourself via annual reports; rely on Wall Street analysts' advice; buy shares in a mutual fund whose manager conducts fundamental analysis for you[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Portfolio-choice illustration: with 60% in stock and targeting 20-stock diversification, how to pick 20 among thousands listed is the selection problem[^mankiw-ch14-045].

## 14-3b The Efficient Markets Hypothesis

- **efficient markets hypothesis:** the theory that asset prices reflect all publicly available information about the value of an asset[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- **informational efficiency:** asset prices rationally reflect all available information[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- **random walk:** the path of a variable whose changes are impossible to predict[^mankiw-ch14-045].

Mechanism[^mankiw-ch14-045]:

1. Each major listed company is followed closely by many money managers/mutual-fund managers who monitor news daily and conduct fundamental analysis to buy when price < value and sell when price > value.
2. Equilibrium supply/demand sets price where number of shares offered equals number demanded → at market price, overvalued judgments exactly balance undervalued judgments; as judged by the typical person in the market, all stocks are fairly valued all the time.

Implications[^mankiw-ch14-045]:

- Market price is always the best guess of value given available information; prices change only when information changes — good public news raises value and price, bad news lowers both.
- Changes must be unpredictable because news itself is unpredictable — otherwise it would already be incorporated. Thus stock prices should follow a random walk.
- If a 10% rise tomorrow were predictable from public information today, the market would have already moved today — contradiction.

**Random walks and index funds — evidence**[^mankiw-ch14-045]:

- Stock prices are very close to a random walk: buying recent winners or losers fails to outperform; correlation between one year's return and next year's is about zero.
- **Index fund:** mutual fund buying all stocks in a given index — buys all stocks rather than picking best. Active funds pick alleged best stocks but trade more frequently (higher costs) and charge higher fees.
- Over 15-year period ending February 2016: **81% of stock mutual funds performed worse than a broadly based index fund holding all U.S. stocks**; average annual return of stock funds fell short by **0.89 percentage points**[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Of the 19% that beat the market, studies show superior performance usually does not persist — consistent with luck (analogy: 5,000 people flipping coins 10 times, ~5 get ten heads and may claim skill but cannot replicate)[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Conclusion: even if not exact, efficient markets contains a large element of truth; best individual strategy is a **diversified portfolio — ideally a low-cost index fund**[^mankiw-ch14-045].

IAN: IGM-style insert in source: "In general, absent any inside information, an equity investor can expect to do better by choosing a well-diversified, low-cost index fund than by picking a few stocks."[^mankiw-ch14-045]

QuickQuiz: Fortune's "most respected" companies — according to efficient markets, restricting portfolio to them will **not** earn better-than-average return because respect is public information already in price[^mankiw-ch14-045].

## 14-3c Market Irrationality

Counter-tradition: fluctuations partly psychological[^mankiw-ch14-045].

- **Keynes (1930s):** asset markets driven by **"animal spirits"** — irrational waves of optimism and pessimism.
- **Greenspan (1990s):** questioned whether boom reflected **"irrational exuberance"**; prices did fall thereafter, but whether exuberance was irrational given information at the time remains debatable[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- **Speculative bubble:** when price of an asset rises above its apparent fundamental value[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Why bubbles possible: stock value depends not only on dividends but also on final sale price — willingness to pay more than worth today if expecting another person to pay even more tomorrow → circular expectations[^mankiw-ch14-045].

**Debate**[^mankiw-ch14-045]:

- Irrationality believers: market often moves in ways hard to explain by news altering rational valuation.
- Efficiency believers: impossible to know correct rational valuation, so don't quickly call any valuation irrational; moreover a rational person should be able to profit from irrationality, yet beating the market is nearly impossible (see index-fund evidence above).

## 14-4 Conclusion and Chapter Integration

- Present value reminds that a dollar next year is worth less than a dollar today and provides comparison method across time[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Risk management reminds that future is uncertain and risk-averse people can guard via insurance/diversification/lower-risk portfolios[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Stock price should reflect expected future profitability (present value of cash flows)[^mankiw-ch14-045].
- Validity of efficient markets / rationality remains controversial; nevertheless large stock-price movements have important macroeconomic implications, often coinciding with broader economic fluctuations — revisited when studying economic fluctuations[^mankiw-ch14-045].
