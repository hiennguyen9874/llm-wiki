Heilbroner's "The End of the Worldly Philosophy?" asks whether economics is ending and what its purpose should be, taking "end" as both *termination* and *purpose*[^heilbroner-019]. Economics is defined not as daily figures or supply-demand diagrams but as an *explanation system* for the workings, problems and prospects of the economy[^heilbroner-019]. Looking for a common structural core rather than surface variety — from Mercantilist monarch to Marshallian clerk, Smithian Society of Perfect Liberty to Veblenian sabotage — Heilbroner argues that core is *capitalism*, defined by three historic innovations whose fading from economists' vision and replacement by *Science* marks the possible termination of the worldly philosophy, while its proper purpose is to help steer *some* capitalisms through ecological, military and globalization strains by broadening motivation, flexibility and social responsibility[^heilbroner-019].

# Synthesis

The chapter returns to Chapter II's survival problem: for 99% of human presence tradition governed hunting and gathering; from the fourth–third millennia B.C. command built cities, irrigation and pyramids, but neither order needed "economics" — there were no prices for pharaohs' blocks[^heilbroner-019]. Only the slow displacement of medieval tradition and feudal command by capitalism — with legitimated acquisitiveness for everyone, market guidance of production and distribution, and bifurcated public/private authority — created an *economy* requiring *economics*; the worldly philosophy is the child of capitalism and could not exist without it[^heilbroner-019]. In recent decades economists have increasingly depicted buying and selling abstractly — Edgeworth's Felicific Calculus, von Thünen's "just wage," Marshall's diagrams, Keynes's algebra — but the deeper shift is not mathematization itself, which modern interdependent technology makes indispensable (GDP, Price Level), but the emergence of *Science* as the essence of economics and the disappearance of *Capitalism* as its object[^heilbroner-019]. Heilbroner challenges the science vision on grounds of volition (Marshall) and the inherently political nature of stratified distribution, and rejects a pseudoscientific objectivity about wealth, taxes, inheritance or sweatshops; scientific method belongs to data reporting but not to policy as if givens were natural[^heilbroner-019]. For the foreseeable future he expects no benign socialist rebirth given twentieth-century experience, so the purposeful end must be understanding the capitalist setting; that setting varies enough to produce a spectrum of socially versus economically successful capitalisms whose social adaptability — exemplified by Scandinavia/Europe versus the United States — will determine navigation of warming, nuclear/ethnic hatred, and supranational globalization[^heilbroner-019].

# Capitalism as the structural core

## Why tradition and command needed no economics

- Hunting-gathering tradition and command-built civilizations had complex rules but no market prices; command altered society spectacularly without creating a production-distribution organization requiring a new understanding called economics[^heilbroner-019].

## Three historic innovations of capitalism

1.  **Dependency on the acquisitive drive** as principal organizer of material production and distribution — unprecedented legitimation and celebration of wealth pursuit for everyone, not merely kings or adventurers[^heilbroner-019].
2.  **Consignment of guidance and distribution to the market** — provision of the stuff of life by competitive buying and selling, with no parallel in other orders[^heilbroner-019].
3.  **Bifurcated overall guidance** — public authority wielding force and law but not carrying on everyday production, and private profit-seeking individuals producing, hiring at offered wages and firing, but without dragooning labor or physical punishment as pyramid builders or feudal lords could[^heilbroner-019].

These define the common source of all great economists from Smith to Keynes and Schumpeter despite quickened responses after losing tradition's drag and command's arbitrariness[^heilbroner-019].

# Mathematization is not the crucial change

- Early abstract depictions: Edgeworth Felicific Calculus pleasure/pain, von Thünen formula for just wage (both in Ch. VII), Marshall diagrams, Keynes algebra[^heilbroner-019].
- Mass quantitative information now required by high-speed production and near-instantaneous communication; interdependency exceeds Smith's pin factory — GDP and Price Level cannot be computed without statistics and mathematics in various forms[^heilbroner-019].
- Econometric predictive accuracy is not notable, but there is no alternative for analytical purposes; still, no one confuses mathematics with economics itself[^heilbroner-019].

# The new vision: Science displacing Capitalism

## Evidence of disappearance

- N. Gregory Mankiw *Principles of Economics* Introduction: "Economists try to address their subject with a scientist's objectivity. They approach the subject of the economy in much the same way as a physicist approaches the study of matter and a biologist approaches the study of life: They devise theories, collect data, and then analyze these data in an attempt to verify their theories."[^heilbroner-019]
- Joseph Stiglitz *Economics* two volumes, 997 pages: the word *capitalism* does not appear — capitalism does not exist for introductory purposes[^heilbroner-019].
- Heilbroner invites skeptic to compare random *American Economic Review* or *Economic Journal* volumes pre-1950s versus last decade: pronounced increase in science-method references and precipitous decrease in *capitalism*[^heilbroner-019].

## Why science is attractive

- Students of economy, like students of nature, seek regularities as clues to "laws" — knowledge of gravity lets us explain/predict orbits or airplane trajectories[^heilbroner-019].
- Lawlike (not law) economic stimulus-response: price up → quantity bought usually down despite advertising; incomes vs consumer spending; interest rates vs business investment — generally predictable and marked by *bipolar* opposing movements by role (buyer vs seller) that make markets a stabilizing, self-balancing order akin to natural processes, unlike politics[^heilbroner-019].
- Promise: if economics became true science, prediction and policy-consequence foresight would increase, though not giving complete control any more than physics controls gravity[^heilbroner-019].

# Two objections to economics as science

## 1. Volition vs particles — Marshall's warning

> Marshall "although beguiled by the sciencelike aspects of economics, he warned that 'economics cannot be compared with the exact physical sciences) for it deals with the ever-changing and subtle forces of human nature.'"[^heilbroner-019]

- Light explained by electrons: no one supposes each electron "decided" where to move; price changes explained by buyers/sellers cannot be described without assuming each person *decided* to act — human behavior requires volition, the unpredictable capacity to change minds up to the last moment (aside from reflexes)[^heilbroner-019].
- Conflating the two uses of "behavior" equates conscious existence with particle motion; if economics were truly a science, humans would be robots no more capable of choosing response to price rise than iron to a magnet[^heilbroner-019].

## 2. Political nature of distribution — no natural givens

- All societies beyond hunting-gathering create privilege/disprivilege — aristocracy/slavery, class/caste, property/penury; capitalism is no exception[^heilbroner-019].
- Are wealth/income distributions, taxes, inheritance rights, sweatshops determined by social gravity or by mutable sociopolitical determinations?[^heilbroner-019]
- "Scientific objectivity" about inherited wealth or immiserating poverty cannot mean accepting arrangements as natural givens under telescope/microscope, nor that discounting private endorsements yields neutrality about a social not natural object[^heilbroner-019].
- Scientific method is ample for many analytic problems including scrupulous data reporting, but policy recommendations cannot be presented as unchallengeably stemming from givens of society — there are no such givens comparable to nature; applying language of nature to society is pseudoscience and would spell termination of worldly philosophy as such[^heilbroner-019].

# The purposeful end: understanding the capitalist setting

- Heilbroner notes personal stance — long endorsed democratic socialism — but judges benign socialist rebirth unlikely given twentieth-century experience; any near-term socialism, especially where most likely in less-developed areas, would again tend to political megalomania, bureaucratic inertia, ideological intolerance[^heilbroner-019].
- Yet capitalism faces equally threatening strains:
    - ecological dangers, foremost global warming — containing damage in poor nations and reducing emissions in rich source nations;
    - alarming spread of nuclear weaponry and ethnic/racial/religious hatreds;
    - fast-growing supranational globalized economy arising within individual capitalisms then escaping control and threatening sovereignty of even the wealthiest[^heilbroner-019].
- Little for economics to offer on political leadership, diplomatic skills, social inspiration, but worldly philosophy has unique potential for visionary guidance helping *some* capitalisms navigate — not all capitalisms are alike despite shared economy[^heilbroner-019].

# The variety of capitalisms — socially vs economically successful

- Every capitalism shares drive for capital, market guidance/constraints, and mixed-blessing bifurcation of power into two interpenetrative but independent sectors, plus a capacity for adaptation and innovation that yields a *spectrum* of performances visible in intensity of drive, degree of market freedom, and location of public/private boundary[^heilbroner-019].
- Contrast: socially if not always economically successful capitalisms of Scandinavia and Europe versus economically successful but socially disastrous capitalism of the United States:
    - executive compensation in top U.S. corporations is twice that of France or Germany;
    - upward mobility of American poor is half that of those countries and but a third that of Sweden[^heilbroner-019].
- Heilbroner reads first as culture of greed, second as social indifference — together hardly the institutional adaptability needed to minimize coming strains, let alone serve as world-leadership model[^heilbroner-019].
- Reborn worldly philosophy's most useful role: awareness of ways capitalist structure can broaden motivations, increase flexibility, develop social responsibility — to develop socially *as well as* economically successful capitalisms[^heilbroner-019].
- Requires prodigies of political leadership and draws on psychology, sociology, political science; economics alone will not guide a leaderless country, but leadership lacks clear directions without enlarged, deepened economics — incorporation of other social knowledge but above all expansion beyond the desiccated residue left today[^heilbroner-019].
- Closing dedication: bearing in mind two meanings of "end," to this hopeful vision of tomorrow's worldly philosophy[^heilbroner-019].

# Guide to Further Reading — bibliographic map

Heilbroner closes with a curated, opinionated reading list, noting much economics is dusty but some is alive and worth endurance; many titles available in paperback (marked * in original)[^heilbroner-019]:

- **Textbooks:** Paul A. Samuelson *Economics* (McGraw-Hill) — famous, bright, broad, demanding; simpler alternative his and Lester Thurow *Economics Explained* (Touchstone/Simon & Schuster, 1998)[^heilbroner-019].
- **History of doctrines:** Mark Blaug *Economic Theory in Retrospect* (Cambridge, 1978) superb but theory-heavy; Wesley Mitchell *Lecture Notes* as *Types of Economic Theory* (Augustus Kelley) marvelous but expensive and over-edited; Schumpeter *History of Economic Analysis* (Oxford, 1954) encyclopedic, brilliant, opinionated, slow for nonprofessionals; his own *Teachings from the Worldly Philosophy* (W.W. Norton, 1996) selections with commentary[^heilbroner-019].
- **Rise of capitalism:** Karl Polanyi *The Great Transformation* (Farrar & Rinehart, 1944) market imposition in 18th century; R.H. Tawney *Religion and the Rise of Capitalism* (1937, Harcourt Brace) profound, unsurpassable style; Max Weber *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (Allen & Unwin, 1930) classic but more demanding; his with William Milberg *The Making of Economic Society* (Prentice-Hall, 1998) less specialized overview[^heilbroner-019].
- **Historical background:** H. Pirenne *Economic and Social History of Medieval Europe* (Harcourt Brace, 1937); *Cambridge Economic History of Europe* 2 vols (Cambridge, 1952); David Landes *The Unbound Prometheus* (Cambridge, 1969) and *The Wealth and Poverty of Nations* (Norton, 1998); Paul Mantoux *The Industrial Revolution in the Eighteenth Century* (Harcourt Brace, 1928)[^heilbroner-019].
- **Pre-Smith:** Bernard Mandeville *The Fable of the Bees* (Penguin Classics, 1970); William Letwin *The Origins of Scientific Economics* (Doubleday, 1964); Ronald Meek *The Economics of Physiocracy* (Harvard, 1963); C.B. MacPherson *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism* (Oxford, 1962); Fernand Braudel multivolume masterpiece (Harper & Row, 1967–1979) a must[^heilbroner-019].
- **Adam Smith:** Glasgow Collected Works *Essays* (eds. Skinner/Wilson, Clarendon, 1975) for scholars; otherwise Modern Library *Wealth* or his *The Essential Adam Smith* (Norton, 1985) with *Theory of Moral Sentiments* excerpts[^heilbroner-019].
- **Malthus and Ricardo:** Keynes sketch of Malthus in *Essays in Biography* (Horizon, 1951); Mitchell on Ricardo in *Lecture Notes*; Sraffa *Works of David Ricardo* (Cambridge, 1951) multivolume, last vol. biographical, bruising abstract argument; Vol. 2 reproduces Malthus *Principles* with Ricardo's annihilatory comments; Malthus *On Population* (Modern Library, 1960) intro Gertrude Himmelfarb; Samuel Huntington 1,000-page *Malthus* (Toronto, 1997) for scholars[^heilbroner-019].
- **Utopians:** not worth reading directly; Frank Manuel *The Prophets of Paris* (Harvard, 1962); Alexander Gray *The Socialist Tradition* (Longmans, 1946) arch but biased pro-utopian; F. Podmore *Robert Owen* (Appleton, 1907) and G.D.H. Cole *Robert Owen* (E. Benn, 1925); Owen's own *The Life of Robert Owen* (Knopf, 1920)[^heilbroner-019].
- **J.S. Mill:** *Autobiography* (Columbia, 1944) classic but tedious; Michael Packe biography (Macmillan, 1954); Hayek *John Stuart Mill and Harriet Taylor* correspondence (Chicago, 1951); Gertrude Himmelfarb *On Liberty and Liberalism* (Knopf, 1974) on Mill/Taylor; Mill *Principles of Political Economy* (Toronto, 1965) beautifully written; Bantam *The Essential Works of John Stuart Mill* with *Autobiography* and *Essay on Liberty*[^heilbroner-019].
- **Marx:** David McLellan *Karl Marx* (Harper & Row, 1973) and shorter Modern Masters (Viking, 1975) best biographies; Edmund Wilson *To the Finland Station* (Harcourt Brace, 1940) novel-like; Marx *Capital* Vol. I Vintage (Random House, 1977); short *Grundrisse* ed. McLellan (Harper Torchbook, 1971); Robert Tucker reader (Norton, 1978); Paul Sweezy *The Theory of Capitalist Development* (Monthly Review); his own *Marxism, For And Against* (Norton, 1983)[^heilbroner-019].
- **Victorians:** No single volume; Alfred Marshall *Principles of Economics* (Macmillan, 1948) ponderous but not difficult; Keynes biographical bits on Marshall and Edgeworth in *Essays in Biography*[^heilbroner-019].
- **Underworld:** Henry George *Progress and Poverty* (Doubleday, 1926) dated but emotional journalistic; J.A. Hobson *Imperialism* (Allen & Unwin, 1938) still to the point, more so than Lenin pamphlet of same name[^heilbroner-019].
- **Veblen:** *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (Modern Library, 1934) best-known; *The Portable Veblen* (Viking, 1950) intro Max Lerner for clarity; Jack Diggins *The Bard of Savagery* (Seabury, 1978) penetrating study; Matthew Josephson *The Robber Barons* (Harcourt Brace, 1934) trenchant/rollicking on times[^heilbroner-019].
- **Keynes:** Roy Harrod *Life of John Maynard Keynes* (Harcourt Brace, 1951) comprehensive but pompous; Robert Skidelsky *John Maynard Keynes* (Viking, 1986) brilliant 2 of 3 vols out; Keynes's own *Economic Consequences of the Peace* (Harcourt Brace, 1920) and *Essays in Persuasion* (1951) sparkling introductions[^heilbroner-019].
- **Whither Capitalism/Economics:** Schumpeter *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (Harper, 1947) still top; his own *Nature and Logic of Capitalism*; Robert Loring Allen *Opening Doors* 2 vols (Transaction, 1991) for Schumpeter life[^heilbroner-019].
- **Nature of economics (final-chapter questions):** Deborah Redman *Economics and the Philosophy of Science* (Oxford, 1991) masterful rapprochement history; Philip Mirowski *More Heat than Light* (Cambridge, 1989) provocative critical economics as social science; Thomas Mayer *Truth versus Precision in Economics* (Elgar, 1993) best evenhanded critique; Norwegian Erik S. Reinert writings especially booklet "The Role of the State" (Center for Development and Environment, University of Oslo, P.O. Box 1116-Blindern, N-0317 Oslo, Norway) difficult to obtain but worthwhile[^heilbroner-019].

# Contradictions

- None within source as Heilbroner resolves to a prescriptive stance, but the chapter stages a core tension also present across wiki: economics as *science* seeking lawlike, objective regularities (Mankiw) versus economics as *worldly* political interpretation where volition and privilege make objectivity pseudoscientific. Both views acknowledge need for scientific method in data work while disputing scope for policy neutrality[^heilbroner-019].
- Heilbroner's own normative tension — long advocate of democratic socialism yet arguing capitalism will be the setting for foreseeable future and must be made socially successful — presented not as contradiction to hide but as reluctant judgment based on twentieth-century socialist experience[^heilbroner-019].
