# International capital flows — savings, investment opportunities, diversification, and tax distortion

In the textbook's loanable-funds account, **net** capital moves from countries where it would otherwise earn a lower interest rate to those where it would earn a higher rate.[^krugman-ch18-095] High saving or weak domestic investment opportunities tend to make a country a capital exporter; rapid growth, strong investment opportunities, low saving, or government deficits tend to make it a capital importer.[^krugman-ch18-095] This explains net positions, not all cross-border transactions: diversification, multinational production, and international banking generate large **gross** flows in both directions, while tax-motivated profit shifting can make recorded cross-border investment and GDP misleading.[^krugman-ch18-095]

## Net flows, saving, and investment opportunities

With otherwise comparable assets and easy cross-border lending, capital flows narrow interest-rate differences; the source's simplified two-country example has lending from 2%-rate Britain to 6%-rate United States until both reach 4%.[^krugman-ch18-095] The model therefore predicts direction from relative scarcity of loanable funds:

- Fast-growing economies generally offer more investment opportunities and therefore have higher demand for capital and returns; capital tends to flow to them from slower-growing economies.[^krugman-ch18-095]
- Differences in private saving and government saving alter the supply of funds. A government budget deficit reduces national saving and can lead to capital inflows.[^krugman-ch18-095]
- A country with high capital demand and/or low fund supply has a relatively high interest rate and receives net inflows; low demand and/or high supply produces lower rates and net outflows.[^krugman-ch18-095]

The source uses 1870–1914 Britain-to-New-World lending as an illustration: rapidly industrializing United States had high capital demand, while already-industrialized Britain had accumulated saving and slower growth.[^krugman-ch18-095]

## Current-account surplus patterns

For 2009–2018, the source's figure reports the largest average current-account surpluses as Germany ($257 billion), China ($197 billion), Japan ($135 billion), Netherlands ($75 billion), Switzerland ($65 billion), and South Korea ($64 billion).[^krugman-ch18-095] It attributes China’s inclusion partly to a policy of holding its currency weak for a time. For Germany, Japan, Netherlands, Switzerland, and South Korea, it emphasizes the combination of high saving and slow long-run growth; it also says euro depreciation increased German and Dutch manufacturing exports.[^krugman-ch18-095]

These are period-specific textbook explanations, not a current ranking or a complete causal account of any country’s external position.[^krugman-ch18-095]

## Why gross flows run both ways

Interest-rate differences explain the direction of **net** flows—the excess of inflows over outflows—but do not imply one-way investment.[^krugman-ch18-095] The source identifies three additional motives for two-way gross flows:

1. **Diversification:** investors hold assets in several countries to reduce country-specific risk.[^krugman-ch18-095]
2. **Business strategy:** firms build or acquire foreign operations to serve markets or organize production, even while foreign firms invest domestically.[^krugman-ch18-095]
3. **International banking centers:** global deposits can enter a country’s financial institutions and then be invested abroad.[^krugman-ch18-095]

Consequently, economies can simultaneously hold foreign assets and owe liabilities abroad. The source reports source-era 2016 U.S. foreign assets of $29.3 trillion and foreign-owned U.S. assets of $40.3 trillion.[^krugman-ch18-095]

### Growth in gross international positions

An accompanying figure shows both cross-border asset stocks growing sharply relative to rest-of-world GDP: foreign-owned assets in the United States rise from about 4% in 1980 to 28% in 2019, while U.S.-owned assets abroad rise from about 6% to 21%.[^krugman-ch18-098] **Synthesis:** this supports increased cross-border financial integration, but stock growth alone does not establish net capital-flow direction: both sides can rise simultaneously, and the figure does not separate transactions from valuation changes.

## Tax-driven reporting distortions

Different corporate-tax rates give multinational firms an incentive to report profits and investments in low-tax jurisdictions.[^krugman-ch18-095] Internal transfer prices and royalty payments need not reflect arm’s-length market prices, so recorded profits—and retained profits treated as investment—may not correspond to local real investment.[^krugman-ch18-095] The source illustrates this with Ireland’s reported 26% real-GDP growth in 2015 following multinationals’ accounting changes, calling the result “leprechaun economics”; it says the IMF estimates tax-avoidance-motivated “phantom” flows at about 40% of international corporate investment.[^krugman-ch18-095]

Those examples support a measurement warning, not an inference that all recorded foreign investment is artificial or that Ireland’s current statistics have the same distortion.[^krugman-ch18-095]
