# IS–LM model — joint short-run equilibrium, policy mix, and adjustment lags

In Blanchard's short-run, fixed-price IS–LM model, output and the interest rate must simultaneously clear goods and financial markets. Goods equilibrium produces a downward-sloping IS curve; when the central bank instead targets the interest rate, financial-market equilibrium is a horizontal LM curve. Fiscal policy shifts IS and monetary policy shifts LM, so their combination can hold output steady while changing the budget deficit; consequently, deficit reduction does **not** by itself determine whether investment rises or falls.[^blanchard-017][^blanchard-018]

## Relations and joint equilibrium

With investment responsive to activity and borrowing cost, goods-market equilibrium is:

$$Y=C(Y-T)+I(Y,i)+G, \qquad I_Y>0,\ I_i<0$$

Higher output/sales induces firms to invest more, while a higher interest rate discourages investment. At a given rate, demand must rise by less than output for the standard stable graphical equilibrium. A rate increase reduces investment and demand, then lowers output through consumption and investment feedback; the resulting IS curve therefore slopes downward in $(Y,i)$ space.[^blanchard-017]

For the traditional money-stock formulation, financial-market equilibrium is $M/P=Y L(i)$. This source instead represents contemporary central-bank practice as choosing an interest-rate target $\bar i$ and supplying whatever money is necessary to attain it. The corresponding LM relation is $i=\bar i$, a horizontal curve. The IS–LM intersection is the only point satisfying both markets' equilibrium conditions.[^blanchard-017]

## Shifts and policy transmission

- A tax increase, cut in government purchases, or fall in consumer confidence shifts IS left: at a given rate, demand and equilibrium output are lower. The reverse changes shift IS right.[^blanchard-017]
- With the policy rate held fixed, a fiscal contraction therefore lowers output and moves the economy **along** the horizontal LM curve; it does not shift LM.[^blanchard-017]
- A monetary expansion lowers the central bank's target rate and shifts horizontal LM down. The economy moves down **along** IS: lower borrowing cost raises investment, then demand and output. Monetary tightening reverses those effects.[^blanchard-017]
- Fiscal and monetary expansion can both raise output. Conversely, fiscal consolidation plus monetary expansion can reduce the deficit while preserving output, provided the central bank has scope to lower rates.[^blanchard-017][^blanchard-018]

This is a comparative-statics framework: the curve shift is distinct from the movement along the other curve.

## Deficit reduction and investment are conditional

The goods-market accounting identity is:

$$I=S+(T-G)$$

It is therefore incomplete to infer that a smaller deficit (higher $T-G$) mechanically produces more investment. If monetary policy does not lower the interest rate, fiscal contraction lowers output and income; private saving falls, in the source's account by more than public saving rises, so investment falls. If the central bank reduces the rate enough to keep output unchanged, lower borrowing cost unambiguously raises investment. Consumption can nevertheless differ depending on whether consolidation uses lower spending or higher income taxes.[^blanchard-018]

Thus, this conclusion concerns short-run demand and the monetary response, not the long-run loanable-funds comparative static in which a persistent deficit reduces national saving and crowds out investment.

## Adjustment lags and scope

The model's static diagrams do not imply instantaneous adjustment. Consumption, investment after sales or rate changes, and production all respond with lags. The source summarizes a U.S. 1960–1990 econometric study of a one-percentage-point federal-funds-rate increase: sales, output, and employment fall and unemployment rises, with the largest output effect around eight quarters; the price level changes little for roughly six quarters. Its plotted estimates include 60% confidence bands, so these are historical estimated responses rather than fixed policy timing rules.[^blanchard-018]

The fixed-price assumption is consequently framed as a short-run approximation. Once price adjustment becomes material, the basic IS–LM model is less reliable. The model also abstracts from differing private borrowing rates, the nominal–real rate distinction, open-economy channels, expectations, and a binding lower bound; in particular, a very low policy rate can restrict the monetary accommodation required for a consolidation without an output decline.[^blanchard-017][^blanchard-018]
