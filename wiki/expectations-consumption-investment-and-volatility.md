# Expectations, Consumption, Investment, and Volatility

Consumption and investment are forward-looking, but neither is determined by expected future income or profits alone. Consumers weigh total wealth—financial and housing assets plus the expected present value of after-tax labor income—alongside current after-tax income; firms weigh expected discounted profits alongside current profit or cash flow. Borrowing constraints, risk, and limited foresight give current flows a direct role. This shared dependence on expectations makes consumption and investment co-move, while discrete capital adjustment makes investment much more volatile.[^blanchard-062][^blanchard-063]

## Consumption: wealth, income, and smoothing

**Total wealth** combines **nonhuman wealth** (financial and housing wealth) with **human wealth**, the expected present value of current and future after-tax labor income. The benchmark foresighted consumer chooses consumption from total wealth and borrows or saves to smooth consumption over the remaining lifetime.[^blanchard-062]

A more realistic consumption function is:

$$C_t=C(\text{total wealth}_t, Y_{Lt}-T_t), \qquad (+,+)$$

Current after-tax labor income matters directly because consumers may prefer a rising lifetime consumption profile, use simple income-following rules, guard against adverse income or health outcomes, or be unable to borrow on the benchmark terms. Thus a household with little wealth and poor credit access may consume its current income even when it expects future income to recover.[^blanchard-062][^blanchard-063]

Expectations affect consumption directly through expected labor income, taxes, and real rates used to value human wealth; they also affect it indirectly through asset prices, whose values incorporate financial-market expectations. A perceived **transitory** income change therefore produces less than a one-for-one consumption response, whereas a perceived **permanent** change can support roughly a one-for-one adjustment. Consumption can also change without current income moving when expectations or asset values change.[^blanchard-063]

## Retirement evidence and its distributional limit

The source reports two U.S. Health and Retirement Study analyses for source-era data. For people aged 65–69 in 2008, mean total wealth was \$1.155 million for married couples and \$533,000 for single-person households (thousands of 2008 dollars); Social Security, employer pensions, personal retirement assets, other financial assets, home equity, and other equity were included.[^blanchard-063]

A separate study compared each household's actual wealth with a consumption-smoothing target. The source reports that more than 80% of households exceeded their target overall, more than 90% in the top half of the income distribution did so, but fewer than 70% in the bottom income quintile did. It interprets the high wealth of better-off households partly as potential bequests and observes that Social Security forms nearly all wealth for many low-wealth households; its conclusion that the program helps prevent retirement shortfalls is an interpretation of those source-era studies, not a current program evaluation.[^blanchard-063]

## Investment: expected profitability and internal finance

A firm compares the purchase cost of a capital good with the expected present value of the profits it will generate. With real profit per unit of capital $\Pi$, depreciation rate $\delta$, and a one-period installation lag, later expected profits are discounted by current and expected future **real** rates and reduced by accumulated depreciation. Investment rises with that expected present value.[^blanchard-063]

Under the deliberately restrictive assumption that expected future profit and real rates equal their current values:

$$V(\Pi_t^e)=\frac{\Pi_t}{r_t+\delta},\qquad I_t=I\!\left(\frac{\Pi_t}{r_t+\delta}\right)$$

The denominator $r_t+\delta$ is the **user cost** (or rental/shadow cost) of capital: the interest opportunity cost plus depreciation. This shortcut is useful but should not replace the forward-looking valuation when expectations differ from current conditions.[^blanchard-063]

Observed investment also depends on current profit/cash flow, beyond its informational value for future profit. Firms with low current profit may be reluctant or unable to borrow because lenders doubt the project or repayment capacity; firms with retained earnings can finance projects internally. The source accordingly specifies $I_t=I[V(\Pi_t^e),\Pi_t]$. Its cited 1986 oil-price episode finds a source-reported 10–20 cent reduction in non-oil investment for each \$1 cash-flow decline among affected firms; this is evidence within that study design, not a universal elasticity.[^blanchard-063]

Tobin's $q$ expresses the market value of installed corporate capital relative to replacement cost. A higher $q$ should encourage investment, and the source's U.S. figure shows a clear association between changes in lagged $q$ and investment; it does not imply firms mechanically follow stock prices, because both respond to expected profits and rates.[^blanchard-063]

## Output linkage and relative volatility

Profit per unit of capital increases with sales/output relative to capital:

$$\Pi_t=\Pi\!\left(\frac{Y_t}{K_t}\right),\qquad (+)$$

Consequently, higher current or expected output can raise current or expected profits and investment. A sales increase regarded as temporary has a small effect on the present value of profits. A permanent increase can require a rapid capital-stock adjustment, so investment initially rises by more than sales before returning toward normal once capital catches up.[^blanchard-063]

The source's U.S. annual series since 1960 show consumption and investment generally co-moving but investment much more volatile: deviations of investment growth from its mean range from about −29% to +24%, versus about −5% to +3% for consumption. Since investment is a much smaller GDP share (about 15% versus consumption near 70% in the source), changes in each can be of comparable aggregate importance.[^blanchard-063]
