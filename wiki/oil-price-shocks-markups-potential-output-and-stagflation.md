# Oil-price shocks, markups, potential output, and stagflation

In the textbook's IS–LM–PC framing, a persistent oil-price rise raises production costs and is represented as a higher markup. The higher markup lowers the real wage consistent with price setting, raises the natural unemployment rate, and reduces potential output. With the policy rate initially unchanged, inflation rises; returning inflation to target requires lower output, yielding a stagflationary adjustment.[^blanchard-032]

## Supply-side mechanism

The baseline labor-market model produces output using labor alone, so oil enters as a reduced-form increase in the price markup $m$. Price setting then implies $W/P=1/(1+m)$: a higher markup lowers the real wage firms can pay. The price-setting curve shifts down, and a higher unemployment rate is required for wage setting to be consistent with that lower real wage.[^blanchard-032]

If the oil-price rise persists and output per worker is unchanged, higher natural unemployment lowers natural employment and potential output. This is a model result, not a claim that every oil-price movement has the same lasting effect; a temporary price increase leaves the medium-run natural rate unaffected in this setup.[^blanchard-032]

## Inflation and policy trade-off

The fall in potential output shifts the PC curve up. If IS and the real policy rate do not initially change, output is unchanged but is now above its lower potential level, so inflation rises above target. To prevent expectations from de-anchoring, the model has the central bank raise the real rate, moving output down to the lower potential level and bringing inflation back to target.[^blanchard-032]

During that adjustment, output is lower while inflation is above target: **stagflation**. The visual model shows the initial upward PC shift, followed by monetary tightening and movement along the new PC curve to the lower-potential medium-run equilibrium.[^blanchard-032]

## Qualifications

- Oil can also reduce demand: firms may cancel or redirect investment, and income shifts from oil buyers to producers who may spend less on domestic goods. These channels can shift IS left, producing an immediate output decline as well as the supply effect.[^blanchard-032]
- A central bank's choice depends on whether the shock is temporary and on the risk that expectations de-anchor. Allowing temporary above-target inflation may avoid unnecessary output loss, but is risky if the shock persists.[^blanchard-032]
- The source attributes milder U.S. effects in the 2000s than the 1970s partly to weaker worker bargaining power and, importantly, more anchored inflation expectations; it presents these as plausible hypotheses rather than settled proof.[^blanchard-032]
