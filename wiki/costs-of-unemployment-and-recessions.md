# Costs of unemployment and recessions

Millions become jobless or trapped in disliked jobs during recessions, fearful to leave[^harford-ch4]. The costs extend well beyond lost income.

## Employment, household, and firm costs

The unemployment rate is a commonly used indicator of labor-market conditions: a high rate indicates scarce jobs, and a low rate easier job-finding. The source's 1988–2020 U.S. series shows it rising sharply during and after recessions and generally falling in expansions.[^krugman-ch6-037]

By cutting jobs and making replacement work difficult, recessions reduce household living standards. The source says they are usually associated with more poverty, mortgage-related home loss, and lower health-insurance coverage; firms also experience lower profits, and some small businesses fail.[^krugman-ch6-037]

## Psychological cost and misery weighting

Happiness economics finds unemployment among the most depressing experiences; Arthur Okun's "misery index" (unemployment rate + inflation rate) was a thought experiment, but research cited shows 1pp extra unemployment is about four times as grim as 1pp extra inflation[^harford-ch4].

## Hiring penalty for long-term unemployment

Rand Ghayad (Northeastern University, 2012) sent 4,800 computer-generated résumés to 600 advertised vacancies across US industries, varying only: relevant industry experience, job-hopping history, and >6 months unemployment[^harford-ch4]. Key result: candidates with wrong-industry experience but ≤14 weeks unemployment were >3× as likely to get a callback as candidates with right-industry experience but ≥6 months unemployment; employers prioritized shunning the long-term unemployed over relevant experience[^harford-ch4]. This creates hysteresis — a recession can drag good workers out of the market permanently[^harford-ch4].

## Long-run earnings scarring

Till Marco von Wachter (UCLA) finds people who lose jobs in mass layoffs or graduate in recessions suffer lasting earnings damage versus boom cohorts, partly because they accept jobs in the wrong field and accumulate misdirected skills, experience, and contacts; differences remain visible a decade after recession ends[^harford-ch4].

## Technology, churn, and job displacement

Technological progress is structurally disruptive: new products, firms, and skills replace old ones. The source calls this **creative destruction** and distinguishes local job loss from economy-wide unemployment. Its historical account finds no systematic positive association between decade-average US productivity growth and unemployment (outside the Great Depression); it also reports evidence that robots can reduce employment in the local labor markets where they are introduced, while whether offsetting jobs arise elsewhere is harder to establish.[^blanchard-051] Thus neither past aggregate employment nor local displacement settles the future effect of automation.

A mass layoff can impose persistent losses even when workers later find jobs. In the source's Social Security records study of long-tenured workers laid off from firms that cut at least 30% of their workforce in a quarter, relative earnings fell about 40 percentage points in the first post-layoff year in recessions and 25 points in expansions. Losses remained about 20 and 10 points, respectively, even 20 years later.[^blanchard-052] Firm- or industry-specific skills are one proposed reason; the source also reports associations with less stable employment, poorer health, and adverse outcomes for children. These results concern this defined mass-layoff population, not all job separations.

## Moral and social consequences

Benjamin Friedman (Harvard) argues downturns reduce charitable donations and raise intolerance — nepotism, racism, closed-mindedness — and anti-democratic forces; the Great Depression → Hitler and World War II is the stark example, but subtler effects occur in milder downturns[^harford-ch4].
