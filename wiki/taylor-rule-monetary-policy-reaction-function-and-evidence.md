# Taylor rule, monetary-policy reaction function, and evidence

A Taylor rule summarizes a central bank's interest-rate response to inflation and economic slack: it calls for a higher policy rate when inflation or activity is high and a lower rate when inflation is low or unemployment is high.[^krugman-ch15-084] Krugman and Wells presents it as a useful description of Fed behavior before the 2008 crisis, not as a mechanical mandate; the zero lower bound and financial-stability concerns can make actual policy diverge from it.[^krugman-ch15-084]

## Policy reaction to output and inflation

- The textbook's 1985–2020 comparison shows the federal funds rate generally rising as the output gap rises and falling as it falls. It reports exceptions when the rate was near zero in 2009–2016 and in 2020; this co-movement is descriptive, not proof that the rate caused the gap.[^krugman-ch15-084]
- It also reports that low inflation helped the Fed keep rates low through the late-1990s positive output gap, as well as during 2002–03 and after 2008.[^krugman-ch15-084]
- The conventional stabilization implication is countercyclical: expansionary policy addresses a recessionary gap, while contractionary policy addresses an inflationary gap. The transmission from the policy rate to aggregate demand is detailed in [The Influence of Monetary and Fiscal Policy on Aggregate Demand](influence-of-monetary-and-fiscal-policy-on-aggregate-demand.md).[^krugman-ch15-084]

## The rule

A **Taylor rule** sets the federal funds rate using the inflation rate and either the output gap or unemployment gap.[^krugman-ch15-084] The source gives a Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco estimated version for 1988–2008:

$$\text{federal funds rate}=2.07+1.28\times\text{core inflation rate}-1.95\times\text{unemployment gap}$$

Here core inflation is the prior year's change in consumer prices excluding food and energy, and the unemployment gap is actual unemployment minus the Congressional Budget Office estimate of the natural rate.[^krugman-ch15-084] This specification predicts a lower rate when unemployment exceeds its natural rate, and a higher rate when inflation rises.

The source's Figure 15-9 shows the actual funds rate tracking this particular rule fairly closely from 1985 through the end of 2008. Thereafter the rule prescribed substantially negative rates (about −6% in 2009–10 in the figure), while the actual rate remained near zero.[^krugman-ch15-084]

## Rules, targets, and discretion

- **Taylor-rule method:** reacts to past inflation and a realized output or unemployment gap; in that sense the source calls it backward-looking.[^krugman-ch15-084]
- **Inflation targeting:** central bank announces an intended inflation rate and sets policy to reach it, using a forecast of future inflation; the source calls it forward-looking. Its claimed benefits are transparent objectives and accountability against the stated target.[^krugman-ch15-084]
- The source's caution is that a fixed inflation objective can be too restrictive when financial-system stability or a weak recovery warrants priority. It describes the Fed's near-zero rate while the Taylor-rule estimate rose from late 2013 as motivated by concern that tightening could return the weak economy to recession.[^krugman-ch15-084]

See [Inflation targeting and the case for higher inflation (2% vs 3–4% and NGDP)](inflation-targeting-optimal-inflation-and-ngdp.md) for the 2% target and its rationale.

## Identifying whether monetary policy works

Observed high rates often coincide with strong economies, because the Fed normally changes rates *in response to* the business cycle. The source therefore reports Christina and David Romer's strategy of identifying FOMC episodes intended to create a recession to reduce inflation, rather than routine cyclical responses.[^krugman-ch15-084]

For five identified contractionary decisions between 1952 and 1984, the textbook reports that unemployment rose after a modest lag in four cases and by an average of 2 percentage points. This is source-reported historical evidence consistent with contractionary monetary policy causing weaker labor-market outcomes; it is not a general effect-size estimate for every policy move.[^krugman-ch15-084]
