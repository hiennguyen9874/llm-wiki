# Hyperinflation and the Brazilian URV ghost currency

Printing too much money causes broad-based price rises; hyperinflation (>50% a month) destroys money's three functions — medium, store, and unit — and Brazil's innovative separation of the unit of account from the medium of exchange via a ghost currency, the URV, supplied the stable reference point that allowed inflation to end overnight in 1994[^harford-ch3].

## Hyperinflation: definition and record cases

- Defined as >50% a month. At 50% monthly, a $1m mortgage becomes trifling within three years (monthly salary in billions, coffee >$1m), debts trivialized and savings/pensions not perfectly indexed rendered worthless; borrowers gain, savers and lenders lose[^harford-ch3].
- **Zimbabwe:** knocked three zeroes off then another ten zeroes (billions→millions→thousands, then again) — cumulatively turning $10 trillion into $1 — then issued 100-trillion notes (sextillion without revaluations: Z$1,000,000,000,000,000,000,000, >10 million × world annual output in dollars)[^harford-ch3].
- **Germany, October 1923:** ~30,000% a month, prices more than doubled every ~4 days; wheelbarrows for cash, cigarettes as currency, currency as firewood; Erich Maria Remarque's *The Black Obelisk* — Ludwig lights cigar with 10-mark bill and asks "Are we ruined or in clover?" Georg replies "I don't believe anyone in Germany knows that about himself" — no one knows where he stands[^harford-ch3].
- **Yugoslavia 1994:** monthly inflation topped 300 million percent; **Hungary 1946** world record 41,900,000,000,000,000% a month (prices triple daily, monthly salary won't buy coffee if you wait a week, 5% per hour; equivalent annual rate a 178-digit number) — salaries cease to be monthly at that pace[^harford-ch3].
- Why so damaging: banknotes cease to be handy medium (wheelbarrows), useless as store (saving/borrowing impossible), and useless as unit (need alternative currency — "Mars Bar before you can say Fintlewoodlewix")[^harford-ch3].

## Brazil's inflation trap

- By early 1990s prices rising 80% a month (comfortably hyperinflationary) — loaf at 1 cruzeiro in January → >3 in March → >100 in September → >1,000 next January; supermarkets employed a full-time price-label man walking aisles, customers raced ahead; wages had to be spent immediately; house sales required agreeing *when* price would be paid — each day's delay cheapened the deal[^harford-ch3].
- Earlier remedies failed:
  - President Sarney (mid-1980s) made raising prices illegal → sellers removed goods, beef farmers hid cows ("Brazil's a big country. You can hide cows"), only black-market sales at true prices[^harford-ch3].
  - Currency replacement tried repeatedly: cruzeiro → cruzado 1986 → revalued 1987 → new cruzado 1988 → cruzeiro 1990 → cruzeiro [new] 1992 — five new currencies in seven years without halting inflation, undermining belief a cure was possible[^harford-ch3].

## The URV ghost currency

- Four academic economists, former college drinking pals who had studied Brazilian inflation, reluctantly engaged after President Itamar Franco summoned Edmar Bacha ("Please tell your father to work fast for the benefit of the country" — autograph for Bacha's children)[^harford-ch3].
- Insight: separate money's functions. Previous reforms replaced medium, store, and unit together and failed. New plan: keep cruzeiro as medium of exchange and (poor) store, but change the unit of account[^harford-ch3].
- Every price and salary listed in URVs — *unidades real de valor* (units of real value) — which did not exist physically. Transactions settled in cruzeiros stuffed in wallets/registers; daily URV–cruzeiro exchange rate calculated by the central bank, published in newspapers and on shop walls[^harford-ch3].
- Cruzeiro lost value daily, but URV (initially pegged to U.S. dollar) held value. Bread stayed at, say, 1 URV — no need for the price-label man — while its cruzeiro price rose daily; workers paid 500 URVs monthly received more cruzeiros each month[^harford-ch3].
- Psychological fixed point: in a landscape where cruzeiros were a poor unit, minds scrabbled for a foothold; URV became the instinctive way Brazilians thought about value despite never taking physical form — *This American Life* called it "the lie that saved Brazil"[^harford-ch3].
- Complementary tightening: printing pressed turned off, budget balanced, wage inflation clamped — cruzeiro inflation already falling, but URV supplied the comprehensibility anchor[^harford-ch3].
- 1 July 1994 the cruzeiro was abolished and replaced by the long-stable URV, now called the *real*; the four promised inflation would end overnight — and it did[^harford-ch3].
