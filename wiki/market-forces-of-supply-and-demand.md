# The Market Forces of Supply and Demand — Competitive Markets, Demand and Supply, and Equilibrium

Supply and demand are the most-used words in economics because they determine in market economies the quantity of each good produced and the price at which it is sold — illustrated when a Florida cold snap raises orange-juice prices, warm New England summers lower Caribbean hotel prices, or a Middle East war raises U.S. gasoline prices while lowering used-Cadillac prices[^mankiw-021]. A market is a group of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service; buyers as a group determine demand, sellers as a group determine supply[^mankiw-021]. This chapter builds the perfectly competitive supply-and-demand model to show how buyers and sellers interact, how their curves determine price, and how prices allocate scarce resources[^mankiw-021].

## 4-1 Markets and competition

- **Market** — a group of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service[^mankiw-021]. Highly organized examples (agricultural commodities with auctioneers meeting at a time and place) contrast with less organized markets such as ice cream in a town — different locations, differentiated products, no auctioneer, each seller posting a price, yet still a market because buyers choose among sellers and sellers compete for the same buyers[^mankiw-021].
- **Competition** — the ice-cream market and most markets are highly competitive in that each buyer has several sellers to choose from and each seller offers a product similar to others; no single buyer or seller determines price and quantity alone — they are determined by all buyers and sellers interacting[^mankiw-021].
- **Competitive market** — a market with so many buyers and sellers that each has a negligible impact on the market price; each seller has limited control — little reason to charge below the going price and loses sales if charging above — and no single buyer can influence price because purchases are small[^mankiw-021].
- **Perfectly competitive market** — the highest form of competition requires (1) goods offered for sale are all exactly the same, and (2) buyers and sellers are so numerous that no single buyer or seller has any influence over the market price; participants are **price takers** who accept the market price and can buy or sell all they want at that price[^mankiw-021].
- **Beyond perfect competition:** wheat exemplifies near-perfect competition (thousands of farmers, millions of consumers)[^mankiw-021]; monopoly — one seller who sets price, e.g., local cable company — is the opposite extreme, with many markets falling between[^mankiw-021]. Assuming perfect competition is a useful simplification and natural starting point because it is easiest to analyze and many lessons apply even when competition is imperfect[^mankiw-021].

## 4-2 Demand

### 4-2a The demand curve and the law of demand

- **Quantity demanded** — amount of a good buyers are willing and able to purchase[^mankiw-021].
- **Law of demand** — other things being equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and when the price falls, quantity demanded rises — pervasive enough to be called a law[^mankiw-021].
- **Demand schedule** — table showing the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded holding everything else constant[^mankiw-021]. Example — Catherine's monthly ice-cream cones: $0.00→12, $0.50→10, $1.00→8, $1.50→6, $2.00→4, $2.50→2, $3.00→0[^mankiw-021].
- **Demand curve** — graph of the price–quantity demanded relationship (price on vertical axis, quantity on horizontal axis by convention); slopes downward because lower price means greater quantity demanded other things equal[^mankiw-021].

### 4-2b Market demand versus individual demand

- Market demand is the sum of all individual demands for the good[^mankiw-021].
- Figure 2: Catherine and Nicholas combined — at $0.00, 12+7=19; $0.50, 10+6=16; $1.00, 13; $1.50, 10; $2.00, 7 (4+3); $2.50, 4; $3.00, 1 — market demand curve is the **horizontal** sum of individual demand curves (add quantities at each price)[^mankiw-021]. Analysis of market function uses the market demand curve[^mankiw-021].

### 4-2c Shifts in the demand curve

The demand curve holds other things constant and need not be stable; a change that alters quantity demanded at any given price shifts the curve — e.g., an American Medical Association discovery that ice cream prolongs health would shift demand right[^mankiw-021].

- **Increase in demand** — any change that raises quantity demanded at every price shifts the curve to the right[^mankiw-021].
- **Decrease in demand** — any change that lowers quantity demanded at every price shifts left[^mankiw-021].

Key shift variables (Table 1)[^mankiw-021]:

| Variable | A change... |
|---|---|
| Price of the good itself | Movement **along** the demand curve |
| Income | Shifts the demand curve |
| Prices of related goods | Shifts the demand curve |
| Tastes | Shifts the demand curve |
| Expectations | Shifts the demand curve |
| Number of buyers | Shifts the demand curve |

- **Income:** if demand falls when income falls, the good is a **normal good**; if demand rises when income falls, it is an **inferior good** (e.g., bus rides)[^mankiw-021].
- **Prices of related goods:** a fall in price of one good reduces demand for another → **substitutes** (ice cream/frozen yogurt, hot dogs/hamburgers, sweaters/sweatshirts, cinema tickets/film streaming)[^mankiw-021]; a fall in price of one good raises demand for another → **complements** (ice cream/hot fudge, gasoline/automobiles, computers/software, peanut butter/jelly)[^mankiw-021].
- **Tastes:** most obvious determinant — if you like ice cream you buy more; economists take tastes as given but examine effects of taste changes[^mankiw-021].
- **Expectations:** about future income or future price affects current demand (expect higher next-month income → spend more now; expect ice-cream price to fall tomorrow → less willing to buy today)[^mankiw-021].
- **Number of buyers:** market demand depends on number of buyers; adding Peter to Catherine and Nicholas raises quantity demanded at every price[^mankiw-021].

**Rule for shift vs movement:** a curve shifts when a relevant variable *not* measured on either axis changes; price is on the vertical axis, so a change in price is a movement along the curve, while income, related-good prices, tastes, expectations, and number of buyers — not on either axis — shift the curve[^mankiw-021]. See also [Graphing Review — Types, Correlation, Slope, and Causality Pitfalls](graphing-review-correlation-slope-and-causality.md) for ceteris-paribus logic.

### Case: Two ways to reduce smoking

Public policymakers often want to reduce smoking; two distinct approaches illustrate shift vs movement[^mankiw-021].

- **Shift left:** public service announcements, mandatory health warnings, banning TV cigarette ads — at any price, quantity demanded falls; panel (a) shifts D₁→D₂, e.g., from 20 to 10 cigarettes per day at $4.00 per pack (point A→B)[^mankiw-021].
- **Movement along:** taxing cigarette manufacture, largely passed to consumers as higher price — panel (b): price $4.00→$8.00 reduces quantity demanded 20→12 (A→C) along the same curve, not a shift[^mankiw-021].

Empirical price sensitivity: 10% price increase → 4% quantity demanded reduction overall; teenagers more sensitive: 10% → 12% drop[^mankiw-021]. Tax opponents argue tobacco and marijuana are substitutes (higher cigarette prices encourage marijuana); gateway-drug view says complements. Studies find lower cigarette prices associated with greater marijuana use, consistent with complements rather than substitutes[^mankiw-021].

## 4-3 Supply

### 4-3a The supply curve and the law of supply

- **Quantity supplied** — amount sellers are willing and able to sell[^mankiw-021].
- **Law of supply** — other things being equal, when the price of a good rises, quantity supplied also rises; when price falls, quantity supplied falls[^mankiw-021]. High price makes selling profitable (longer hours, more machines, more workers); low price may cause shutdown and zero supply[^mankiw-021].
- **Supply schedule** — table showing price–quantity supplied holding everything else constant[^mankiw-021]. Ben's monthly cones: $0.00→0, $0.50→0, $1.00→1, $1.50→2, $2.00→3, $2.50→4, $3.00→5[^mankiw-021].
- **Supply curve** — graph of the relationship; slopes upward because higher price means greater quantity supplied[^mankiw-021].

### 4-3b Market supply versus individual supply

Market supply is the sum of supplies of all sellers[^mankiw-021]. Ben and Jerry combined: at $2.00, 3+4=7; totals: $0.00→0, $0.50→0, $1.00→1, $1.50→4, $2.00→7, $2.50→10, $3.00→13; market supply curve is the horizontal sum of individual supply curves[^mankiw-021].

### 4-3c Shifts in the supply curve

The supply curve is drawn holding other things constant; changes in other factors shift it. E.g., a fall in sugar price (an input) raises ice-cream profitability and shifts supply right — at any price sellers willing to produce more[^mankiw-021].

- **Increase in supply** — raises quantity supplied at every price, shifts right[^mankiw-021].
- **Decrease in supply** — lowers quantity supplied at every price, shifts left[^mankiw-021].

Key shift variables (Table 2)[^mankiw-021]:

| Variable | A change... |
|---|---|
| Price of the good itself | Movement **along** the supply curve |
| Input prices | Shifts the supply curve |
| Technology | Shifts the supply curve |
| Expectations | Shifts the supply curve |
| Number of sellers | Shifts the supply curve |

- **Input prices:** cream, sugar, flavoring, machines, buildings, labor — rise in input prices makes production less profitable and reduces supply (potentially to zero); supply is negatively related to input prices[^mankiw-021].
- **Technology:** invention of mechanized ice-cream machine reduced labor needed, lowered costs, raised supply[^mankiw-021].
- **Expectations:** if firm expects price to rise in future, it may store current production and supply less today[^mankiw-021].
- **Number of sellers:** market supply depends on count of sellers; retirement of Ben or Jerry reduces supply[^mankiw-021].

**Movement vs shift:** price is on vertical axis, so price change is a movement along; input prices, technology, expectations, number of sellers — off-axis — shift the curve[^mankiw-021].

## 4-4 Supply and demand together

### 4-4a Equilibrium

- **Equilibrium** — situation where market price has reached the level at which quantity supplied equals quantity demanded[^mankiw-021].
- **Equilibrium price (market-clearing price)** — the price that balances quantity supplied and quantity demanded; at this price everyone is satisfied — buyers have bought all they want, sellers sold all they want[^mankiw-021].
- **Equilibrium quantity** — quantity supplied and quantity demanded at the equilibrium price[^mankiw-021]. Figure 8: equilibrium at $2.00 per cone and 7 cones where curves intersect[^mankiw-021].

**Disequilibrium dynamics:**

- **Surplus (excess supply)** — quantity supplied greater than quantity demanded when price is above equilibrium; e.g., at $2.50, quantity supplied 10 > quantity demanded 4 (Figure 9a); sellers' freezers fill, they cut prices, quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls — movements along curves until equilibrium reached[^mankiw-021].
- **Shortage (excess demand)** — quantity demanded greater than quantity supplied when price is below equilibrium; e.g., at $1.50, quantity demanded 10 > quantity supplied 4 (Figure 9b); buyers wait in long lines, sellers raise prices without losing sales, quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises toward equilibrium[^mankiw-021].

The activities of many buyers and sellers automatically push price toward equilibrium regardless of whether it starts too high or too low; once at equilibrium there is no upward or downward pressure[^mankiw-021]. Speed of adjustment varies by market depending on how quickly prices adjust; in most free markets surpluses and shortages are only temporary[^mankiw-021].

- **Law of supply and demand** — the claim that the price of any good adjusts to bring quantity supplied and quantity demanded into balance — so pervasive it is called a law[^mankiw-021].

### 4-4b Three steps to analyzing changes in equilibrium

Equilibrium price and quantity depend on positions of supply and demand curves; an event shifting a curve changes equilibrium[^mankiw-021].

1. Decide whether the event shifts the supply curve, the demand curve, or both[^mankiw-021].
2. Decide whether the curve shifts to the right or left[^mankiw-021].
3. Use the supply-and-demand diagram to compare initial equilibrium with the new one to see how the shift changes price and quantity[^mankiw-021].

**Example — shift in demand:** very hot summer weather affects tastes for ice cream[^mankiw-021].
1. Demand curve affected (more desired at any price); supply unchanged because weather does not directly affect sellers[^mankiw-021].
2. Demand shifts right (D₁→D₂) — higher quantity demanded at every price[^mankiw-021].
3. At old $2.00 price there is now excess demand (shortage) inducing firms to raise price; new equilibrium $2.50 and 10 cones (up from $2.00 and 7) — both price and quantity rise[^mankiw-021].

**Shifts versus movements:** hot weather raises quantity supplied even though the supply curve did not move — this is an increase in "quantity supplied" but no change in "supply" itself[^mankiw-021]. Supply = position of curve; quantity supplied = amount sellers wish to sell at a given price[^mankiw-021]. A shift in the supply curve is a "change in supply"; a shift in the demand curve is a "change in demand"; a movement along a fixed curve is a "change in quantity supplied" or "change in quantity demanded" respectively[^mankiw-022]. When demand rises (D₁→D₂) with supply fixed, the shortage at the old price pushes price up; quantity supplied rises via movement *along* the supply curve[^mankiw-022].

### Comparative statics — three equilibrium shifts

**Increase in demand (Figure 10):** abnormally hot summer shifts demand right (D₁→D₂). At the old $2.00 price quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, price rises to $2.50, and equilibrium quantity rises from 7 to 10 cones. Supply curve does not shift[^mankiw-022].

**Decrease in supply (Figure 11):** hurricane destroys part of the sugarcane crop, raising sugar (input) prices. Three steps[^mankiw-022]:
1. Supply curve shifts — higher input costs reduce amount firms produce at any price; demand unchanged because input costs do not directly affect consumer desire[^mankiw-022].
2. Supply shifts left (S₁→S₂)[^mankiw-022].
3. At old $2.00 price excess demand causes price to rise; new equilibrium $2.50 and 4 cones (down from $2.00 and 7) — price up, quantity down[^mankiw-022].

**Both curves shift simultaneously (Figure 12):** hot weather (demand right) plus hurricane (supply left) occur in the same summer. Both curves shift — demand right, supply left — as previously analyzed[^mankiw-022]. Outcome depends on relative magnitude[^mankiw-022]:
- Panel (a): demand increase dominates — price $P₁→P₂$ rises, quantity $Q₁→Q₂$ rises[^mankiw-022].
- Panel (b): supply decrease dominates — price $P₁→P₂$ rises, quantity $Q₁→Q₂$ falls[^mankiw-022].
- In both cases price unambiguously rises; quantity change is ambiguous (could go either way)[^mankiw-022].

### Table 4 — What happens to price and quantity when supply or demand shifts?

Quick-quiz prediction matrix for any combination of shifts[^mankiw-022]:

| | No Change in Supply | An Increase in Supply | A Decrease in Supply |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No Change in Demand** | P same, Q same | P down, Q up | P up, Q down |
| **An Increase in Demand** | P up, Q up | P ambiguous, Q up | P up, Q ambiguous |
| **A Decrease in Demand** | P down, Q down | P down, Q ambiguous | P ambiguous, Q down |

Mankiw advises testing understanding by explaining entries with a supply-and-demand diagram[^mankiw-022]. Practice prompts: show what happens to pizza market if tomato price rises (input-cost supply shift left); on a separate diagram, show what happens if hamburger price falls (substitute — demand for pizza shifts left)[^mankiw-022].

### Check: simultaneous demand and supply shifts

The Krugman/Wells exercise supplies concrete comparative-statics checks: cheaper gasoline (a complement) shifts demand for large cars right, so both equilibrium price and quantity rise; a technological innovation shifts the supply of recycled paper right, so price falls and quantity rises; and cheaper on-demand films (a substitute) shift cinema demand left, so both price and attendance fall.[^krugman-ch3-099]

A new computer chip simultaneously shifts demand for computers using the older chip left and their supply right. The equilibrium price therefore falls, but the quantity effect is indeterminate: it falls when the demand decrease dominates and rises when the supply increase dominates. The two accompanying diagrams were inspected and show both cases.[^krugman-ch3-099]

### Application — Price increases after disasters and the price-gouging debate

Hurricanes and other disasters create the textbook conditions for upward price pressure — demand spikes (batteries, water, gasoline, generators, hotel rooms) or supply falls — illustrated by Hurricane Sandy reports of $4 for a can of Coke, $500/night for a Brooklyn hotel, and $6.99 for D-batteries[^mankiw-023]. The policy question is whether to let prices rise or suppress them with anti–price-gouging laws.

**Anti-gouging laws as price ceilings:** New Jersey law treats price increases of more than 10 percent during a declared emergency as excessive — a gas station paid a $50,000 fine for a 16 percent gasoline hike during Tropical Storm Irene — while New York's General Business Law prohibits increases on "essential items" like food, water, gas, generators, batteries, flashlights, and transportation during market disruptions, backed by AG warnings and consumer-report hotlines from Eric Schneiderman and Chris Christie[^mankiw-023]. These are political expressions of the conventional ethical view that profiting on neighbors' misery is wrong — "reverse looting"[^mankiw-023].

**The economic mechanism:** Price gouging can occur only when there is a shortage; without shortage, competition prevents spikes — a deli charging $4 for Pepsi when the next deli charges $1 simply loses customers[^mankiw-023]. When shortages arise, higher prices perform rationing: they discourage overconsumption and hoarding, so a limited stock (e.g., buying half as many batteries, water bottles, or gallons of gas) reaches more customers than under fixed prices[^mankiw-023]. Merchants are thus profiting from managing prices — a socially beneficial allocation service — not from the disaster itself[^mankiw-023]. This reflects the broader price-as-baton for the invisible hand: decentralized price and wage signals allocate workers, goods, and resources without central planning[^mankiw-023].

**Alternative allocation mechanisms and their limits:**
- **Lottery:** allocate rights to buy at old prices — winners may resell at elevated prices, so gouging migrates from merchant to lottery winner, and losers go without[^mankiw-023].
- **Bureaucratic rationing:** assign quantities by household need, as the U.S. did in World War II — requires an immense, timely information burden on available goods and needs, impractical as a hurricane approaches[^mankiw-023].
- **First-come, first-served (the de facto result of price ceilings):** encourages hoarding, empties shelves, and makes speed rather than willingness to conserve the rationing criterion — "speed seems a poor proxy for justice"[^mankiw-023].

**Equity objection and policy alternative:** The concern that free-floating prices let the wealthy buy everything while the poor are excluded is, in the Carney/Mankiw presentation, overrated — disaster price hikes typically strain budgets rather than put necessities wholly out of reach[^mankiw-023]. The preferred remedy for budget strain is after-the-fact transfer payments to poor households, preserving the allocative benefits of the price signal rather than controlling the price itself[^mankiw-023]. Conclusion: instead of cracking down on gougers, reform counterproductive laws and "hope for a bit more gouging and a lot fewer empty store shelves" next time[^mankiw-023].

### 4-5 Conclusion — how prices allocate resources

Markets harness supply and demand to allocate scarce resources; prices are the signals that guide that allocation[^mankiw-022]. Beachfront land is rationed by price — whoever is willing and able to pay gets it, price adjusting until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied[^mankiw-022]. Prices also determine who produces and how much (e.g., farming — need for food implies some must work the land, price signals guide that allocation)[^mankiw-022]. Discussion centered on ice cream but lessons apply to most markets — buying contributes to demand, job-seeking contributes to supply of labor[^mankiw-022].
