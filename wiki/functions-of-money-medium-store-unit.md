# Three functions of money as medium, store, and unit of account

Textbook money combines three separable roles — medium of exchange, store of value, and unit of account — the best money does all three; the unit-of-account role as a stable standard of value determines whether a volatile asset like gold or Bitcoin counts as money at all[^harford-ch3]. Mankiw defines money narrowly as the set of assets people regularly use to buy goods and services — cash in the wallet — not total wealth: owning Facebook stock makes Zuckerberg wealthy but not money until converted to cash[^mankiw-16-1].

## The three roles

- The textbook view lists: (1) medium of exchange, (2) store of value, (3) unit of account; each can in some circumstances be peeled away from the others[^harford-ch3].

### 1. Medium of exchange — moving purchasing power through space

- Mankiw's definition: an item buyers give to sellers when they purchase goods and services; sellers accept it because they trust others will accept it later — the convention allows roundabout trade (restaurant → chef → day care → teacher → lawn-mowing) without a double coincidence of wants, the unlikely requirement under barter that two people each want what the other has[^mankiw-16-1].
- Allows indirect exchange: laundry services for cash, cash for a computer, without needing a computer retailer who wants laundry[^harford-ch3].
- Can be understood as keeping track of valuable contributions: doing laundry earns a formal record (cash), buying the computer redeems it. In principle this could be a giant centralized database[^harford-ch3].
- Yap's rai illustrate the database without paper: the island's collective memory of who owns which stone is the ledger; paper money replaced that for anonymous urban societies, and is now partly giving way again to a computerized Yap — debit cards and Internet banking[^harford-ch3].

### 2. Store of value — moving purchasing power through time

- Mankiw: an item people can use to transfer purchasing power from present to future; seller accepts money today to become a buyer later[^mankiw-16-1]. Money is not the only store — stocks and bonds also transfer value — wealth is the total of all stores including nonmonetary assets[^mankiw-16-1]. When prices rise, the value of money falls; each dollar buys less — the link between price level and value of money that drives inflation analysis[^mankiw-16-1].
- A dairy farmer cannot save churns of milk; selling for cash and keeping cash (mattress or bank) preserves value[^harford-ch3].
- Stores and media are related but not identical: a house can be an excellent store but a lousy medium (hard to buy/sell); Yap rai were a very good store but the medium was the mental bookkeeping, not the stones themselves[^harford-ch3].

### 3. Unit of account — the stable standard of value

- Mankiw: the yardstick people use to post prices and record debts — a shirt $50 vs hamburger $5 is quoted in dollars, not in hamburgers; loans are measured in dollars, not goods; this measuring and recording function is the unit of account[^mankiw-16-1].
- The strangest and most important function: a reference point for what things are worth, like kilograms vs. pounds vs. bags of sugar for weight (Harford at 88kg / 194lb / 176 bags)[^harford-ch3].
- Example from Anthony Courakis (Harford's undergraduate tutor): $1m in mixed assets could be called £641,500 or €795,800 or 10,893 barrels of oil or 1,730 Apple shares at time of writing; over the prior year that $1m fluctuated between ~3,200 and ~1,500 Apple shares while remaining $1m — useful denomination is the one stable relative to what you plan to buy (dollars for Florida, sterling for Edinburgh, oil only if filling a hole with Brent Crude)[^harford-ch3].
- Good currencies are stable relative to things you buy; confusing salaries in Apple shares or apples signals a poor unit[^harford-ch3].
- Historical commodity monies succeeded as units because supply and demand were stable: salt's demand stable (everyone needs a bit, no one wants a lot) and supply stable (age-old techniques) → stable price; hence *salary* from salt and Roman soldiers plausibly paid in salt[^harford-ch3].

## When the unit of account fails — gold, Bitcoin, and substitute units

- A dollar is money only if it keeps reasonably stable value; otherwise people switch units[^harford-ch3].
- Tony Courakis as a boy in postwar Greece played Monopoly with worthless German marks and Greek drachmas; Greeks denominated long-term contracts in British gold sovereigns without handing sovereigns over; Massachusetts in the Revolutionary War promised soldiers cash to the value of 68 4/7 pounds of beef, 16 pounds leather, 5 bushels corn, 10 pounds sheep's wool because Continental Congress paper's post-war value was unknowable (payment still in cash, portfolio as comprehensible benchmark)[^harford-ch3].
- *Financial Times* journalist Nico Colchester noted the Mars Bar as a fantastically stable unit — many prices stable for decades when measured in Mars Bars — its non-adoption signals confidence in modern fiat stability, not its merit as currency[^harford-ch3].
- **Gold is not money now:** James Rickards tweeted April 2013 as gold collapsed: "Last week I had x ounces... Today I have x ounces... Dollar is volatile..." — absurd because hamburgers, suits, cars changed slowly in dollars but gyrated wildly in ounces; gold may be an investment but not a unit of account[^harford-ch3].
- **Bitcoin is not money:** decentralized electronic "currency" from 2008 by pseudonymous Satoshi Nakamoto, mined slowly like gold, with hard cap and government independence appealing to gold-like enthusiasts; but price fell 61% on 10 April 2013 — far too volatile to be a unit of account, though it may be a long-term investment[^harford-ch3].

## Liquidity and the store-of-value trade-off

- Liquidity is the ease with which an asset can be converted into the medium of exchange; money is the most liquid asset because it *is* the medium[^mankiw-16-1]. Stocks and bonds are relatively liquid (sold with small cost); a house, Rembrandt, or 1948 Joe DiMaggio card is illiquid (time and effort)[^mankiw-16-1].
- Households balance liquidity against usefulness as a store: money is most liquid but imperfect as a store when inflation erodes purchasing power[^mankiw-16-1].
