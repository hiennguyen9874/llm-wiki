# Policy expectations, credibility, and fiscal consolidation

Policy affects current output not only through today's government spending or policy rate, but through its effect on expected future output, taxes, and real rates. In the forward-looking IS account, this makes a monetary expansion more potent when it credibly changes expected future conditions, and makes the short-run effect of fiscal consolidation theoretically ambiguous—not evidence that consolidation is generally expansionary.[^blanchard-066][^blanchard-067]

## A forward-looking IS relation

Aggregate private spending depends on current and expected future variables:

$$Y=A(Y,T,r,Y'^{e},T'^{e},r'^{e})+G$$

It rises with current and expected future output and falls with current and expected future taxes and real rates. Expected income changes human wealth and expected profits; expected real rates affect the present values of labor income, dividends, and profits. Thus changes in those future expectations shift IS, while an isolated change in today's real policy rate produces only a small movement along a steep IS curve: it has limited effect on present values and a temporary income change has a small multiplier.[^blanchard-066]

## Monetary policy and expectation management

A central-bank rate cut lowers the current policy rate directly. If it also leads people and firms to expect lower future real rates and higher future output, both expectation changes shift IS right and amplify the rise in current output. If expectations do not change, the direct effect is limited.[^blanchard-066]

The textbook calls the practice of signaling future policy rates **forward guidance**. Its rational-expectations benchmark assumes people use available information as well as they can when inferring the policy's future effects; it is not an assertion that they know the future or never exhibit biases. The source retains rational expectations as a useful policy-evaluation benchmark while recognizing behavioral limits and episodes of excessive optimism or pessimism.[^blanchard-066]

## Fiscal consolidation: offsetting channels

A credible deficit-reduction program that cuts current and expected future government spending has opposing effects on current demand:

- Lower current $G$ shifts IS left.
- Higher expected future output and lower expected future real rates—anticipated consequences of higher future saving and investment—shift IS right.[^blanchard-066]

Without further information, their net effect is indeterminate. A program may be more likely to avoid an immediate contraction when it credibly places more of the cuts in the future, when its composition reduces distortions or credibly allows lower future taxes, when initially precarious public finances make restored control reassuring, and when monetary policy can lower rates. Backloading also weakens credibility if future cuts are not believed. These are conditional mechanisms, not a general prescription or an estimate of a fiscal multiplier.[^blanchard-066][^blanchard-067]

## Ireland and euro-area evidence: suggestive, then adverse

Ireland's two 1980s consolidations illustrate why fiscal-program composition and expectations were proposed as part of the explanation. The 1982 tax-focused effort began after the 1981 deficit reached 13% of GDP and debt 77%; output growth was low in 1982 and negative in 1983, unemployment rose from 9.5% in 1981 to 15% in 1984, and the actual deficit reduction through 1984 was 3.5% of GDP. The spending-cut and tax-reform-oriented program begun in 1987 followed a 1986 deficit of 10.7% and debt of 116% of GDP; GDP growth was 4.7%, 5.2%, and 5.8% in 1987–89, while the deficit fell from 10.7% to 1.8% of GDP.[^blanchard-067]

The household saving rate is consistent with a change in expectations—rising slightly amid the earlier weak-growth episode but falling from 15.7% in 1986 to 12.6% in 1989 during strong growth. It does not establish that fiscal policy caused the expansion: the source identifies faster productivity growth relative to wages, tax breaks, low labor costs, an educated workforce, and foreign-firm investment as material concurrent changes.[^blanchard-067]

The source reports the opposite broad outcome for euro-area austerity in 2010–11: countries with larger planned consolidations had larger negative growth forecast errors. Because forecasts had embedded small positive consolidation effects, it interprets the relationship as evidence that multipliers were positive and larger than assumed—expectation effects did not offset direct demand losses. This cross-country forecast-error evidence supports that source-era inference but is not by itself a controlled causal estimate.[^blanchard-067]

## Uncertainty can independently restrain spending

For a given expected outcome, greater risk can induce precautionary consumption and investment restraint. When the outcome or its probabilities are unclear and an investment is costly to reverse, delaying it can have an **option value of waiting**. The source links this mechanism to durable purchases after the 1929 crash, heightened financial-market uncertainty after Lehman's 2008 failure, and some firms' delayed investment amid source-era tariff uncertainty; these historical illustrations do not quantify the aggregate effect.[^blanchard-067]
