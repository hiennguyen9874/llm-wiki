# Beveridge curve and structural versus cyclical unemployment

Unemployment combines a cyclical component that rises and falls with recessions and a structural component that persists even in booms; the Beveridge curve helps separate them[^harford-ch8].

## Two types

- **Cyclical:** more layoffs and fewer hires in recessions, reversing in recoveries[^harford-ch8].
- **Structural:** always-present unemployment from wages held above clearing (Ford-style efficiency wages, minimum wages) and from benefits that make waiting for a preferred job preferable to taking the first offer; may be temporary (industries shrinking while new ones grow, requiring retraining) yet not helped much by demand stimulus[^harford-ch8].

> "There are basically two types of unemployment: the cyclical unemployment that comes and goes as recessions come and go, and more permanent structural unemployment."[^harford-ch8]

## The Beveridge curve

- Named for economist William Beveridge, architect of the UK postwar welfare state[^harford-ch8].
- Plots vacancies advertised against the unemployment rate: negative correlation, a curve from top-left (many vacancies, low unemployment — boom) to bottom-right (few vacancies, high unemployment — recession)[^harford-ch8].
- **Movement along:** recession or recovery moves the observed point along a given curve[^harford-ch8].
- **Shift of the curve:** structural change moves the entire curve. Example: paying extremely lavish benefits shifts it right — for any vacancy rate, unemployment is higher[^harford-ch8]. Both efficiency wages and benefits contribute to structural unemployment in this framework[^harford-ch8].

## US illustration 2008–2012

- Pre-recession <5% unemployment → 10% in autumn 2009: standard move along the curve[^harford-ch8].
- By 2012 vacancies recovered but unemployment barely fell below 8%, not back up the old curve but consistent with a new higher curve — suggesting much unemployment was structural rather than cyclical (Harford notes "a little early to tell"); if sustained, unemployment will remain higher at any level of demand until another structural shift lowers the curve[^harford-ch8].

## Cross-country levels of structural unemployment (good-times rates)

- Korea and Iceland ~3%, UK ~5%, Germany ~10%, Poland ~15%, EU average ~8.5%, OECD average ~6.5%[^harford-ch8]. Differences motivate asking why structural unemployment varies, not just how to solve recessions[^harford-ch8].

## Implications

- Two ways to fight unemployment: fight recessions to move to the top-left along the curve (more vacancies, fewer unemployed), and structural reforms to shift the curve down-left so for any vacancy level fewer are jobless; the two are not mutually exclusive[^harford-ch8].
- Structural unemployment, even when temporary, calls for different tools than demand-boosting stimulus[^harford-ch8].
