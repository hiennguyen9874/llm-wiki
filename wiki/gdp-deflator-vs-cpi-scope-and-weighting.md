# GDP Deflator versus CPI — Scope and Weighting Differences

The GDP deflator (nominal GDP / real GDP) and the CPI both gauge how quickly prices rise and usually tell a similar story, but they diverge when imported consumption prices or composition shifts matter because they differ in scope — all domestically produced versus all bought by consumers — and in weighting — currently produced bundle versus fixed basket[^mankiw-038-deflator].

## Definitions

- **GDP deflator** — ratio of nominal GDP to real GDP; nominal is current output valued at current prices, real is current output valued at base-year prices, so the deflator reflects the current price level relative to base-year prices[^mankiw-038-deflator].
- **CPI** — cost of a fixed basket of consumer goods and services relative to base-year cost, weighted by consumer spending[^mankiw-038-deflator].

Economists and policymakers monitor both to gauge price rises; Figure 2 (annual data since 1965, U.S. Department of Labor/Commerce) shows inflation as percentage change in each index — the two generally move together, divergence is the exception[^mankiw-038-deflator].

## Difference 1 — Scope: what prices are included

- GDP deflator reflects prices of **all goods and services produced domestically**[^mankiw-038-deflator].
- CPI reflects prices of **all goods and services bought by consumers** (including imports, excluding domestically produced goods not bought by consumers)[^mankiw-038-deflator].

Examples:

- Boeing airplane produced and sold to the Air Force: price rise shows up in GDP deflator (plane is part of GDP) but not in CPI (not in typical consumer's basket)[^mankiw-038-deflator].
- Volvo price rise: car made in Sweden, not part of U.S. GDP — not in deflator — but U.S. consumers buy Volvos, so it is in CPI basket[^mankiw-038-deflator].
- Imported consumption good price increase shows up in CPI but not GDP deflator[^mankiw-038-deflator].

Oil illustration — particularly important: U.S. produces some oil but much is imported; oil and products like gasoline and heating oil make up a much larger share of consumer spending than of GDP, so when oil price rises, CPI rises by much more than GDP deflator[^mankiw-038-deflator].

## Difference 2 — Weighting: how prices are aggregated

- CPI compares price of a **fixed basket** to price of the basket in the base year; BLS changes the basket only occasionally[^mankiw-038-deflator].
- GDP deflator compares price of **currently produced** goods and services to price of the same goods and services in the base year; the group used changes automatically over time as composition of GDP changes[^mankiw-038-deflator].

Implication: if all prices change proportionately, weighting doesn't matter; if prices of different goods change by varying amounts, weighting matters for overall inflation rate[^mankiw-038-deflator].

## Producer-price comparison

The **producer price index (PPI)** tracks prices of inputs bought by producers, such as raw commodities and electricity, rather than consumer purchases.[^krugman-ch7-042] The source presents the PPI as a potentially earlier signal of inflationary or deflationary pressure because commodity producers may change prices quickly. It also reports that CPI, PPI, and the GDP deflator usually move closely together, but the PPI fluctuates more, reflecting its greater exposure to energy and food price swings.[^krugman-ch7-042] This is a general empirical tendency, not a forecasting guarantee.

## Empirical pattern — when they diverge

- 1970s: both show high inflation[^mankiw-038-deflator].
- Late 1980s, 1990s, first decade of 2000s: both show low inflation[^mankiw-038-deflator].
- Exception: 1979 and 1980 — CPI inflation spiked up more than GDP deflator largely because oil prices more than doubled — explained by the two differences above, especially oil's import/consumer share[^mankiw-038-deflator].

## QuickQuiz tie-in (from same chapter)

- If a Pennsylvania gun manufacturer raises rifle price sold to the U.S. Army, its price hikes increase the GDP deflator but not the CPI (same logic as Boeing to Air Force)[^mankiw-038-deflator].
- Because consumers can sometimes substitute cheaper goods for those that have risen, the CPI overstates inflation (fixed-basket substitution bias) while GDP deflator automatically reflects substitution via changing production mix[^mankiw-038-deflator].
