# Social Security Funding, Saving, and Capital Accumulation

Workers contribute while employed and receive retirement benefits under either a fully funded or pay-as-you-go pension, but their macroeconomic effects differ. A fully funded system substitutes public saving for reduced private saving, whereas pay-as-you-go transfers current contributions to current retirees and can reduce total saving, capital accumulation, and long-run output; moving an established system toward full funding creates a transition cost because promised benefits remain due.[^blanchard-040]

## Two financing designs

- **Fully funded:** contributions are invested in financial assets and later finance the contributing workers' principal and return. Benefits depend on the assets' rate of return. Reduced personal retirement saving is offset, to a first approximation, by fund saving, leaving total saving and capital accumulation unchanged.[^blanchard-040]
- **Pay-as-you-go (PAYG):** current worker contributions finance current retiree benefits. Workers still save less privately in anticipation of benefits, but no offsetting fund saving occurs; total saving and capital accumulation consequently fall in the source's account. Benefits and effective returns depend on the worker-to-retiree ratio and contribution/benefit rules.[^blanchard-040]

Real systems can mix the two designs. The source characterizes the U.S. system as basically PAYG: its trust fund was much smaller than the value of promised future benefits. This is a source-era characterization, not a statement of the current program balance or financing status.[^blanchard-040]

## Aging and transition burden

Population aging worsens the PAYG financing trade-off: with more retirees relative to workers, maintaining benefits requires higher contributions, lower benefits, or both. The source's projected U.S. ratios and GDP shares are historical textbook projections and should not be treated as current forecasts.[^blanchard-040]

Full funding from a program's inception could leave a higher capital stock and future output. But an already-established PAYG system has outstanding benefit promises. Transitioning by investing new contributions while continuing benefits means current workers effectively pay once for their own funded retirement and again for current retirees' promised benefits. The source therefore concludes that any such transition would need to be gradual if its burden is not to fall disproportionately on one generation.[^blanchard-040]

Personal-account proposals do not automatically increase national saving: if benefits already promised under Social Security are debt-financed, increased private account saving is offset by lower public saving. Financing those promises with additional taxes can raise national saving, but makes the double-payment burden explicit.[^blanchard-040]
