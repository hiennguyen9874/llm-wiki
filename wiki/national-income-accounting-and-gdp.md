# National income accounting and GDP as market-value output

GDP is the centerpiece of national income accounting: the total market value of all goods and services produced in an economy in a year, where total income equals total production by construction[^harford-ch4]. In the early 2010s context cited, world GDP was about $70 trillion (~$10,000 per person), the US about $15 trillion, China >$7 trillion, Japan ~$6 trillion, and the EU $17–18 trillion together, with Brazil, Russia, Canada, India, Australia, Mexico and South Korea also at ~$1 trillion+[^harford-ch4].

## History of measurement

- For centuries governments collected economic data to tax wealth: the Census of Quirinius under Caesar Augustus, the Domesday Book (1086) under William the Conqueror, and in the 1660s William Petty's estimate of England's national income of £40 million/year — commonly reckoned the first national income accounts, developed after surveying Ireland for Cromwellian confiscations[^harford-ch4].
- Systematic modern measurement for stabilization rather than taxation dates to the 1930s Great Depression and prospect of war, under increased democratic accountability. The Roosevelt administration began collecting unemployment and other statistics to design relief and jobs programs, without prior reliable data[^harford-ch4].
- Simon Kuznets pioneered a logically consistent framework for adding up income/production and later won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics; his system underpins modern GDP[^harford-ch4].

## Definition and scope

- GDP measures willingness to pay, not aesthetic, practical, or moral worth. A Bible, *Fifty Shades of Grey*, and Harford's book count equally if they sell for the same price[^harford-ch4].
- Analogy: GDP is like mass — a brain (~3 lb) vs. three 1-lb bags of sugar can be compared on the same scale without implying equal value[^harford-ch4].
- GDP vs. GDP per capita (per person): aggregate GDP ranks large economies (US largest), while GDP per capita ranks small rich places like Qatar or Switzerland far above the US, Japan, Germany, and multiples of Brazil, India, China[^harford-ch4].

## GDP, GNP and GNI and the three calculation approaches

The three letters try to add up income, spending or production — in principle the same number because one person's spending is another's income and production is valued by what is spent on it[^harford-cult-gnp].

- **Three ways to calculate GDP:** sum all incomes (wages + property/share/bond income), sum all spending, or sum market value of all production[^harford-cult-gnp].
- **GDP vs. GNP/GNI:** GDP = market value produced inside a country's borders; GNP/GNI = income accruing to a country's citizens regardless of location[^harford-cult-gnp]. A Canadian-owned apartment or factory in Chicago adds to US GDP but Canadian GNI[^harford-cult-gnp]. If citizens owned assets only domestically the measures would coincide; open economies diverge — e.g., Ireland has high GDP from foreign-owned firms but lower GNI[^harford-cult-gnp]. GNI is preferred terminology because ownership is about income, though product and income are supposed to be interchangeable[^harford-cult-gnp].
- **International comparison:** GDP is typically converted at market exchange rates (reflecting export values); GNI tends to be converted at purchasing power parity (PPP), adjusting for domestic living costs[^harford-cult-gnp]. Hence Switzerland's GDP per capita appears ~60% above the US via exchange rates (banking, precision manufacturing), but GNI-PPP puts them roughly on par because fuel, food and housing are cheaper in the US[^harford-cult-gnp].

## What GDP omits or approximates — and technical fixes

Within a market-price, value-added framework, several categories are omitted or roughly estimated[^harford-cult-gnp]:

- **Imponderables vs. measurable poetry:** GDP counts poetry as money spent on poetry books minus printing/distribution costs; a perfect valuation is not worth pursuing vs. reading/writing poetry[^harford-cult-gnp].
- **Imputed rent:** Owner-occupied housing is estimated; otherwise two neighbours swapping houses and charging $10,000 rent each would spuriously raise GDP by $20,000 with no extra shelter consumed[^harford-cult-gnp].
- **Government and charity output:** valued at cost of inputs ($10bn spent = $10bn value assumed), ignoring possible divergence from willingness to pay[^harford-cult-gnp].
- **Household production:** unpaid child/elder care, home-grown vegetables, DIY repairs are ignored — a family paying for childcare raises GDP vs. parental care that does not[^harford-cult-gnp]. Classic illustration: "if a man marries his housekeeper, GDP falls" (sexist but illustrative); similarly a legal prostitute marrying a client would lower GDP only where prostitution is legal and counted[^harford-cult-gnp]. Kuznets wanted household production included to make GDP a better welfare measure but lost the argument for official statistics; the tension remains — measure market production well vs. comprehensiveness badly[^harford-cult-gnp]. Black/grey-market transactions (illicit drugs, counterfeit goods, cash-in-hand jobs) are also excluded because hidden from statisticians[^harford-cult-gnp].
- **Asset values and environment:** GDP records spending to rebuild the Empire State Building if King Kong knocks it down (with multiplier caveats if at capacity) without deducting the $10bn asset loss[^harford-cult-gnp]. Same for natural assets: Qatar's sale of four trillion cubic feet of gas adds to GDP with no footnote for the depleted stock; ozone, greenhouse gases, water quality, fish stocks are similarly omitted[^harford-cult-gnp]. A 1997 estimate of ecosystem services at 1–3× world GDP ($18tn) illustrates the difficulty — valuing sunlight/oxygen/water is conceptually dubious, though valuing specific ecosystem services for carbon-tax or wetland-vs-airport decisions is important even if hard[^harford-cult-gnp].
- **Technical improvements:** (1) subtract depreciation → net domestic product (NDP), increasingly relevant with rapid IT obsolescence but hard to measure; (2) better quality adjustment for services (e.g., distinguishing haircut/restaurant price rises from gentrification); (3) valuing financial services — Bank of England's Andrew Haldane noted UK banks contributed most to GDP growth in Q4 2008, right after Lehman's collapse, showing mismeasurement[^harford-cult-gnp].
