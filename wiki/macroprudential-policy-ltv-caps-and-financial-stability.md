# Macroprudential policy — LTV caps and financial stability

The crisis challenged the idea that stable inflation and output alone ensure stability: housing-price declines and financial-system risk can amplify each other into a severe macroeconomic downturn. Blanchard argues that policy rates are too economy-wide and blunt for such sectoral risks; **macroprudential tools** instead target borrowers, lenders, or intermediaries directly. Their use is supported as precautionary policy in this account, but their effects, interactions, and governance remain uncertain.[^blanchard-097][^blanchard-098]

## Why use targeted tools?

The source gives three reasons central banks hesitated to counter housing booms with interest-rate increases: fundamentals versus bubbles are hard to distinguish; a rate rise slows the whole economy; and policymakers expected later rate cuts to clean up a crash. The crisis led the source to favor "lean"—restraining a suspected buildup—over "clean" after it bursts, including for excessive bank leverage, even when the diagnosis is uncertain.[^blanchard-097]

Examples of targeted instruments include:

- **Borrowers:** a maximum loan-to-value (**LTV**) ratio limits mortgage size relative to house value; restrictions on household foreign-currency borrowing can limit exchange-rate exposure.[^blanchard-097]
- **Lenders/intermediaries:** minimum capital-to-assets or capital-to-risk-weighted-assets ratios constrain bank leverage; the source identifies time-varying adjustment of such requirements as unresolved.[^blanchard-097]
- **Cross-border finance:** capital-inflow controls can be taxes or direct limits, potentially differentiated by type of flow and sudden-stop risk.[^blanchard-097]

## LTV evidence: suggestive, not an effect estimate

For 21 countries with available data, the source compares maximum LTV allowed on new mortgages with nominal house-price growth from 2000 to 2007. The plotted LTV values range from 60% in Korea to 125% in the Netherlands; the fitted line slopes upward, and the chart labels its fitted relation $y=1.3x-46.7$.[^blanchard-098]

Countries with lower maximum LTVs, including Korea and Hong Kong, had smaller price increases in this descriptive comparison, while Spain and the United Kingdom had higher ratios and larger increases. But the relationship is visibly loose: Germany and Japan are below the fitted line, while countries at roughly 100% LTV have widely different price changes. It is therefore cross-sectional association, not evidence that changing an LTV cap causes a particular housing-price change.[^blanchard-098]

The source explicitly says other factors mattered and that even controlling for them it is difficult to identify the precise LTV effect confidently. It concludes that more must be learned before LTV regulation can be treated as a reliable macroprudential tool.[^blanchard-098]

## Interaction and governance questions

Very low policy rates may encourage risk-taking, so a central bank pursuing macroeconomic easing may need macroprudential tools to offset associated financial risk; the source says how to coordinate them is poorly understood.[^blanchard-098] It also identifies a governance trade-off: putting monetary and macroprudential tools under one central bank can improve coordination, but can concentrate too much authority in an independent institution. Its source-era examples assign both to the Bank of England in the United Kingdom and assign U.S. responsibility to a Treasury-led council in which the Fed has a major role.[^blanchard-098]
