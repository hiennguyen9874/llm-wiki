# Modern macroeconomics — construction, crises, and post-crisis reassessment

Blanchard portrays modern macroeconomics as iterative construction, crisis, and reconstruction: Keynes made aggregate demand and stabilization central; rational expectations forced models to make expectations and policy regimes explicit; a later synthesis combined microfoundations with nominal rigidities. The Great Recession then exposed that its standard models had not integrated financial intermediation, leverage, complexity, and liquidity adequately, prompting a major reassessment rather than, in this source's tentative view, a complete revolution.[^blanchard-099][^blanchard-100]

## Keynesian foundation and the neoclassical synthesis

Keynes's 1936 *General Theory*, written amid the Great Depression, argued that effective (aggregate) demand determines short-run output and that recovery can be slow. It supplied enduring components: consumption's relation to income and the multiplier, liquidity preference, expectations and animal spirits, and active fiscal policy in depressions.[^blanchard-099]

The postwar neoclassical synthesis formalized and extended these ideas. IS–LM supplied a tractable organizing framework despite initially omitting explicit expectations and wage-price adjustment. Subsequent work developed consumption, investment, money-demand, portfolio-choice, growth, and large macroeconometric models.[^blanchard-099]

The 1960s debate sharpened the policy questions. Friedman and Schwartz argued that monetary contraction driven by bank failures made the Great Depression worse and could have been countered by the Federal Reserve; the resulting consensus held that both fiscal and monetary policy affect activity. Friedman and Phelps also correctly challenged the presumed long-run Phillips-curve trade-off: attempts to hold unemployment below its natural rate would not yield a permanent inflation–unemployment exchange.[^blanchard-099]

## Rational expectations changed model and policy design

1970s stagflation and the rational-expectations critique jointly destabilized the earlier consensus. Lucas argued that estimated historical relationships are not reliable guides to a new policy regime because a policy change changes how people form expectations—the **Lucas critique**. Rational expectations also implied that anticipated monetary expansion could be absorbed in wages and prices rather than raise output, and recast policy as a strategic interaction rather than control of a passive system.[^blanchard-099]

The response was integration rather than wholesale abandonment. Rational expectations became a benchmark, while staggered wage and price setting supplied a mechanism through which slow adjustment and persistent output deviations can remain consistent with forward-looking behavior. Research on policy credibility, reputation, and commitment likewise made political and institutional constraints more explicit.[^blanchard-099]

## Toward the pre-crisis synthesis

Three research programs supplied different parts of the emerging framework:

- **Real business cycle (RBC) theory** made explicit microfoundations, rational expectations, and numerical solution central, and emphasized that some output movements reflect changes in potential output. The source notes objections to treating large recessions as technology regress and to ignoring monetary effects.[^blanchard-099]
- **New Keynesian research** explained nominal rigidity through staggered adjustment and small menu costs, and studied labor-market and credit-market imperfections. The latter recognized that bank lending can be constrained even for borrowers willing to pay the market rate.[^blanchard-099]
- **New growth theory** revisited increasing returns, technological progress, creative destruction, and institutions as sources of long-run growth.[^blanchard-099]

By the 2000s, the source describes a partial synthesis: models combined optimizing agents, rational expectations, technological shocks, and selected imperfections—including nominal rigidities and imperfect credit information. New Keynesian models developed by Woodford, Galí, and others informed inflation targeting and interest-rate rules; larger numerical descendants became central-bank **DSGE** workhorses. The source stresses that this was agreement on a framework, not a single settled model or list of indispensable frictions.[^blanchard-099]

## Crisis lesson: financial intermediation is macroeconomic

The crisis revealed a consequential gap. Research in finance and related fields had identified bank runs, liquidity shortfalls, limits to arbitrage, and behavioral departures from the representative rational-agent model. But standard macro models used by central banks had largely omitted the financial system's intermediation role and nearly excluded leverage, complexity, and liquidity; the available pieces were not integrated into a model of their interactions.[^blanchard-100]

The post-crisis research agenda therefore includes macrofinancial linkages, integration of financial frictions into larger models, macroprudential tools, and public-debt risks. This is not presented as finished knowledge: the source says the models should become richer but that another unanticipated shock remains likely.[^blanchard-100]

The crisis also qualified the assumption that an economy reliably returns to its natural level in the medium run. The source treats the U.S. liquidity-trap episode as showing how the normal response to weak output—lower interest rates—can fail. Its tentative consensus is conditional: normal adjustment may work for small shocks, but after exceptional shocks it may fail, policy room may be limited, and repair may take a long time.[^blanchard-100]
