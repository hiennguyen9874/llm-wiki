# Unconventional monetary policy — quantitative easing and reserve-rate operations

At the zero lower bound, policy-rate cuts stop, but other asset yields can remain positive because they include risk or term premia. Blanchard's account treats **quantitative/credit easing** as central-bank purchases of those assets, financed by money creation, to increase demand for them, compress their premia and lower corresponding borrowing rates. It can therefore still support activity, but works less directly and reliably than conventional rate policy.[^blanchard-097]

## Asset purchases at the lower bound

- A fall in private demand for an asset—for example, because of risk aversion—raises its premium. Central-bank purchases can increase demand and lower that premium; the transmission target is the relevant borrowing rate, not the already-zero policy rate.[^blanchard-097]
- In the source's chronology, **QE1** began in November 2008 with mortgage-backed-security purchases amid a dysfunctional market; **QE2** began in November 2010 with longer-term Treasury purchases intended to lower the term premium; **QE3** began in September 2012 with further mortgage-backed-security purchases intended to reduce mortgage costs.[^blanchard-097]
- The source reports wide agreement that QE1 materially limited premia in the impaired mortgage-security market. It reports that QE2 and QE3 lowered long-term Treasury term premia, but says their magnitude and broader effectiveness were more controversial.[^blanchard-097]

## Balance-sheet and operating-framework consequences

Asset purchases greatly enlarged central-bank balance sheets. The source reports that the U.S. monetary base rose from about $850 billion (6.6% of GDP) in September 2008 to about $4 trillion (22% of GDP) in 2015, mostly as bank reserves; its inspected chart shows both series rising sharply and later declining from their peaks.[^blanchard-097]

When the Fed raised its policy rate in late 2015 while retaining abundant reserves, the source says it paid interest on reserves so banks would be willing to hold them. Its source-era **corridor** description places the federal funds rate between the rate paid on reserves and the discount rate. The chart shows the funds rate moving closely with the reserve rate, though it was slightly below it because some institutions without reserve-account access would lend at a lower rate.[^blanchard-097]

This is a textbook account through early 2019, not a statement of current Fed implementation. The source presents the unresolved post-crisis choice as whether to retain a large balance sheet or return closer to a small-balance-sheet, mostly non-interest-paying-liability regime.[^blanchard-097]
