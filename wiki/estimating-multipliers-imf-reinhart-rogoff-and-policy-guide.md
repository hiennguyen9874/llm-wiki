# Estimating stimulus multipliers — IMF, Reinhart-Rogoff, openness, and a four-step fiscal guide

Measuring the fiscal multiplier is empirically fragile because stimulus coincides with many other shocks and lacks experimental variation; late-2012 IMF revisions from ~0.5 to 0.7–1.7, the spreadsheet and methodological challenges to Reinhart-Rogoff's 90% debt/GDP cutoff, and results on openness and fixed exchange rates all show that context — depth of recession, zero lower bound, trade leakage, and monetary regime — dominates the estimate, informing a practical four-step guide to using fiscal policy only after preparation and monetary easing[^harford-ch8].

## Why the world cannot cleanly estimate the multiplier

- Any complex economy has too much going on to isolate stimulus effects: must infer counterfactual without stimulus[^harford-ch8].
- U.S. smorgasbord 2008–2009: ~$100bn Bush tax rebate (2008) + ~$800bn Obama stimulus (early 2009), of which ~$300bn tax rebates/cuts, ~$100bn infrastructure not spent in 2009, ~$50bn aid to school districts offsetting local cuts (anti-anti-stimulus), plus one-month "cash for clunkers" (~$4,000 to scrap old cars for new efficient ones), plus very loose monetary policy (Fed printing money, cutting rates near zero, supporting banks/insurers) and weak export markets[^harford-ch8]. Similar confounding across U.K., Brazil, China, France, Greece, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Spain[^harford-ch8]. Conclusions from unemployment/growth paths must be tentative[^harford-ch8].

## Selected empirical claims

- Some credible post-U.S.-stimulus studies: multiplier around 2 for most successful bits (payments to low-income households and to state governments); other studies much more skeptical[^harford-ch8].
- Resources for the Future (environmental think tank, no particular partisan axe) on cash for clunkers: much effect was subsidizing purchases that would have happened anyway; Harford notes $4,000 saved might still have been spent elsewhere, so micro waste does not settle macro benefit[^harford-ch8].
- Ideal experiment not feasible: randomize world economies into large-stimulus vs nothing, then compare growth — even then trade spillovers contaminate controls; macroeconomic policy is as far as possible from a robust scientific experiment[^harford-ch8].

## IMF reassessment: 0.5 to 0.7–1.7

- IMF argued through the crisis that multipliers were around 0.5, then late 2012 announced the multiplier was at least 0.7 and perhaps as high as 1.7 — a big error stemming from looking at historical experience dominated by mild recessions[^harford-ch8].
- In ordinary recessions with slack limited, spending pushes up prices and interest rates; 2008 recession assumptions — weak demand, slack capacity, rock-bottom rates — were instead extreme and realistic[^harford-ch8].
- Political stakes: whether government borrows to boost or tightens belt (stimulus vs austerity) polarized politics; with multiplier 0.5, $1 spending cut shrinks economy $0.50 as private sector fills half the slack; with 1.7, the private sector shrinks too — cuts are far more damaging[^harford-ch8]. IMF admission meant it had understated the damage of spending cuts; its historical evidence was simply not relevant to the deeper slump[^harford-ch8].

## Reinhart and Rogoff and the 90% debt/GDP cutoff

- Carmen Reinhart and Ken Rogoff, "Growth in a Time of Debt" (2010): statistical correlations between growth and debt/GDP ratio (government borrowing relative to economy size); famous result that when debt/GDP rises above ~90%, growth tends to be substantially slower — cited by Paul Ryan, EU economics commissioner Olli Rehn, U.S. senators, journalists as reason to worry stimulus would push countries toward dangerous debt levels[^harford-ch8].
- Thomas Herndon (graduate student, University of Massachusetts Amherst) replication exercise: could not replicate results, obtained authors' actual spreadsheet[^harford-ch8].
  - Excel omission: some rows omitted, excluding data for Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark[^harford-ch8].
  - Other issues: inclusion of more recently available data changed results substantially; methodological dispute over weighting/averaging — more a matter of opinion[^harford-ch8].
  - Both sides overplayed: original correlations from wildly different countries/circumstances proved little, so errors disprove little; both pro-austerity and pro-stimulus sides milked respective findings[^harford-ch8].
- Harford's bottom line: high debt correlated with lower growth (unsurprising), but the sharp cutoff at 90% is imaginary; correlation does not show debt causes slow growth — slow growth causing debt is at least as plausible[^harford-ch8].

## Heterogeneity: openness and exchange-rate regime

- Ethan Ilzetzki, Enrique G. Mendoza and Carlos A. Végh study: multipliers larger in economies that do not trade internationally much — not only North Korea but also the United States, because domestic market looms large relative to exports/imports, so less leakage to French vineyards etc.[^harford-ch8].
- Illustrative trade totals (exports + imports as % of GDP): U.S. ~20–25%; many European economies ~50%; South Korea ~100%; Estonia >150%; Singapore and Hong Kong >300%[^harford-ch8]. Estonia's austerity debate tells us nothing about whether the United States should do stimulus or austerity[^harford-ch8].
- Multipliers larger with fixed exchange rates (e.g., eurozone members); fixed exchange rate is a very important, very sticky price — consistent with sticky-price account of why recessions persist[^harford-ch8].
- All such results are best guess, not cast-iron proof, like the IMF work[^harford-ch8].

## Four-step guide to effective fiscal policy in a crisis

**Step 1: Prepare when not in crisis.**
- Enter recession with low debt so lenders remain willing; few governments follow this — with irony that low/falling-debt Ireland and Spain struggled to borrow when banks were vulnerable, while more profligate U.S. and Japan had no trouble finding lenders[^harford-ch8].
- Maintain a shelf of vetted big public investment projects (due diligence done) to dust off quickly, avoiding dithering over airports vs sweepers vs chocolate coins; worthwhile sooner or later, best done when economy depressed; if misdiagnosed and no macro boost, the road/hospital/power station remains[^harford-ch8].

**Step 2: Use monetary policy first.**
- Cutting rates is simple, quick, reversible if inflation rises, better understood than fiscal stimulus, and typically run by independent technocratic central bankers less swayed by short-term politics; usually enough for short/shallow recessions[^harford-ch8].
- Fiscal advocates are usually wrong when recession is mild, rates well above zero, economy small/open with flexible exchange rate — or any subset — but in 2008+ rates were near zero, economies large and often fixed-rate, recession not mild, so stimulus was appropriate as an important recent case, not universal truth[^harford-ch8]. Conversely anti-stimulus voices are often right generally but were on Harford's reading wrong recently[^harford-ch8].

**Step 3: If recession looks long and deep, build from the shelf quickly.**
- Stimulus schemes often start so late the recession is over before foundations are laid; spending in a recovered economy merely fuels inflation and reduces efficiency[^harford-ch8].

**Step 4: Keep fiscal credibility on debt repayment.**
- Announcements matter: temporary sales-tax cut encourages spending now (money buys less later); on spending side, favour one-off investment (new high-speed railway, pothole repairs) that helps after recession and minimizes vested-interest persistence[^harford-ch8].
- Avoid creating persistent entitlements; governments perversely tend to cut investment in recessions because cutting pensions, civil service salaries and welfare is politically harder[^harford-ch8].

> Note: Harford flags this synthesis as Keynesian; a distinct classical critique (Keynes got it all wrong) is taken up next — beyond this chapter's coverage[^harford-ch8].
