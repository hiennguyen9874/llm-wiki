# Inflation targeting and the case for higher inflation (2% vs 3–4% and NGDP)

Central banks aim for *just enough* inflation — currently about 2% — not zero, because a small positive buffer eases real wage adjustment and guards against deflation; Harford argues 3–4% may be better, and explains why nominal GDP targeting is a tempting but confusing variant[^harford-ch4].

## Why not zero? Why not gold?

- A gold standard would largely prevent inflation unless a gold glut occurs, but it prevents the money supply from expanding when needed[^harford-ch4].
- Historical illustration: after Columbus, gold and silver from the Americas doubled European prices over the 16th century — about 0.7% a year, trivial by modern standards[^harford-ch4].
- Rule of 72: divide 72 by the annual inflation rate to get years for prices to double (72/0.7 ≈ 100 years; 72/2 ≈ 35 years; 72/4 ≈ 18 years)[^harford-ch4].

## Two reasons to want positive inflation

### 1. Real wage adjustment via money illusion and sticky wages

- When a sector needs lower real wages (falling productivity or foreign competition eroding output prices), nominal cuts are rarely feasible — people resist them[^harford-ch4].
- Recalls the [Sticky prices, nominal rigidity, and why money matters](sticky-prices-and-monetary-non-neutrality.md) professor who raged at a nominal pay cut but accepted a 3% nominal raise at 6% inflation — the same ~3% real cut — showing money illusion[^harford-ch4].
- With some inflation, employers can impose real cuts via below-inflation raises without nominal cuts; with zero inflation they cannot[^harford-ch4].

### 2. Buffer against deflation

- Monetary policy is imprecise and will overshoot/undershoot; targeting 2% means a 2-point undershoot still leaves ~0% rather than deflation; targeting 0% guarantees any undershoot is deflationary[^harford-ch4].
- Deflation is argued to be more damaging than moderate inflation (see [Deflation, the zero lower bound, and liquidity traps](deflation-zero-lower-bound-and-liquidity-trap.md))[^harford-ch4].

## Why printing trillions did not produce hyperinflation

- The U.S. Federal Reserve created > $2 trillion after 2007, at times $40bn a month (>$100 per U.S. resident monthly), mostly as bank reserves not cash[^harford-ch4].
- No linear link from money to prices: $100 given to a starving man is spent; $100 to an anxious 90-year-old with a decent pension may sit in a cookie jar and do nothing for demand or inflation[^harford-ch4].
- Since 2008 much new money has sat in equivalent cookie jars, propping up balance sheets without hyperinflation; inflation stayed near target[^harford-ch4].

## Is 4% inflation survivable?

- In normal times interest rates, wages and pensions broadly track inflation, so purchasing power is largely preserved (recent near-zero rates with positive inflation are an unusual crisis artifact)[^harford-ch4].
- Moderate inflation does not destroy money's three functions:
  - Medium of exchange: 4% does not require wheelbarrows[^harford-ch4].
  - Store of value: with a functioning financial system a 5–6% savings rate offsets 4% inflation; mattress/cookie-jar saving is poor but bank intermediation solves it[^harford-ch4].
  - Unit of account: Harford's West London wine-bar anecdote — charged almost £10 for two beers, unsure if bar was expensive or his memory of beer prices was fuzzy — shows slight fuzziness but not destruction; hyperinflation would destroy the unit role, 4–5% typically does not[^harford-ch4].
- Examples: India and China averaged ~5–8% inflation over 25 years yet grew strongly; China had >25% inflation in two bouts in the late 1980s, contributing to Tiananmen Square protests of 1989, but rapid growth erased economic consequences[^harford-ch4].

## Hyperinflation does not slide smoothly from 4%

- A 56-episode catalogue (57th is Iran late 2012) shows three-quarters clustered after extreme political stress: post-WWI central Europe (Weimar Germany most famous), WWII/immediately after (Hungary 1946 world record), and Eastern bloc as Soviet Union disintegrated (>half of all cases)[^harford-ch4].
- Remaining cases (Zimbabwe, sanction-struck Iran, revolutionary France) also involve exceptional crisis[^harford-ch4].
- Causality runs disaster → printing to cover war/civil-service pay when taxes cannot be collected → expectations and wage-price spiral → hyperinflation, not the reverse[^harford-ch4].
- Wage-price spirals can occur at moderate inflation without war (1970s wealthy countries reached double digits, sometimes >20% after oil shocks and loose policy) but central banks contained them well below 50% a month — not close to hyperinflation[^harford-ch4].

## Warning signs of too much money

- **Expectations embedded:** when people explicitly factor inflation into daily decisions and contracts, it raises transaction costs and makes disinflation harder; at ~25% a year almost everyone does so, at low single digits they do not[^harford-ch4].
- **Malinvestment at capacity:** when the economy is already at its supply limits (factories, hours, technology diffusion rate), new money has no sensible real investment and flows into asset bubbles — dot-com shares, Shanghai condominiums, subprime-backed bonds — that look profitable until they burst[^harford-ch4].
- Tricky in real time: under Alan Greenspan (Fed chair mid-1980s–mid-2000s) rates were cut at each scare, arguably fueling successive bubbles, yet inflation stayed moderate so looseness was contested even in hindsight[^harford-ch4].
- William McChesney Martin's punch-bowl metaphor: the Fed's job is to remove the bowl just as the party gets going, and monetary policy acts with a delay like alcohol — by the time drunkenness is visible, extra drinks are already in the stomach[^harford-ch4].

## Why 2%? The active debate

- Olivier Blanchard (IMF chief economist, 2010) floated 4%; supported across the spectrum by Greg Mankiw and Paul Krugman, among others[^harford-ch4].

### Arguments for higher target (3–4%)

- Makes deflation less likely — valuable because deflation is dangerous and hard to cure[^harford-ch4].
- Easier real wage adjustment when nominal cuts are sticky[^harford-ch4].
- Debts melt faster: surprise inflation transfers from creditors to debtors like a goddess forgiving debt; debtors are more likely to spend, aiding recovery after high-debt crises[^harford-ch4].

### Counter-arguments

- Higher underlying inflation magnifies sticky-price distortions: menu reprinted only every three years is 6% stale at 2% inflation vs. 12% at 4%[^harford-ch4].
- Debt-melting is unfair to creditors (e.g., pension savers) and raises future borrowing costs as lenders demand higher rates[^harford-ch4].
- Credibility cost: central banks earned anti-inflation credibility the hard way (e.g., Volcker years); moving the target may upset the applecart, though as a new policymaker the status-quo cost of staying at 2% must also be weighed[^harford-ch4].

### Harford's judgment

- Costs and benefits of 4% vs. 2% roughly balance on distortions vs. wage flexibility; no major drawbacks of 4% are easy to find, the clinching argument is avoidance of the zero-lower-bound trap next time — better not to enter it at all[^harford-ch4].

## Nominal GDP (NGDP) targeting as alternative

- Mechanics: if real growth averages 3% with 2% inflation, nominal GDP grows ~5%; target 5% NGDP instead of 2% inflation[^harford-ch4].
- Logic: central bank directly affects inflation but only indirectly affects real growth, so NGDP target is a moving inflation target — aim for more inflation when real growth is slow, tighten when fast; gives high-inflation benefits when needed but averages ~2%[^harford-ch4].
- Harford's caution: theoretically brilliant but practically too clever — public will not understand the moving implicit inflation target, making financial planning harder; credibility and simplicity favor a straightforward 3–4% inflation target instead[^harford-ch4].
