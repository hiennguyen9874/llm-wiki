# Global financial crisis — housing, securitization, and world transmission

In this source's account, the 2007–09 crisis began with falling U.S. house prices and poor-quality mortgages, but opaque, repeatedly repackaged mortgage securities spread uncertainty through banks; after Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, financial distress became a global recession through trade and financial channels.[^blanchard-tour-world]

## From housing decline to financial panic

U.S. house prices had doubled from 2000 before declining in 2007. Borrowers with large or poor-quality mortgages increasingly could not meet payments; when a home's value fell below its mortgage balance, default became more attractive.[^blanchard-tour-world]

Mortgage originators had bundled loans into securities and sold them onward, sometimes through further repackaging. The source argues that their complexity and opacity made their value hard to assess. Banks consequently became reluctant to lend to one another because they could not evaluate counterparties' assets; institutions then faced funding difficulty alongside uncertain asset values. Lehman's 15 September 2008 bankruptcy made the threat of broader bank failure acute.[^blanchard-tour-world]

## Demand collapse and international transmission

Falling house and equity prices, together with fear of a depression, led households to cut consumption and firms to cut investment. U.S. housing construction also fell sharply. The source reports that the Federal Reserve lowered its policy rate to near zero and the U.S. government cut taxes and increased spending, yet output contracted in late 2008 and through 2009.[^blanchard-tour-world]

The focus-box evidence isolates a consumption-confidence channel. In data indexed to 1 in 2008:Q1, total consumption fell before disposable income did, while durable-goods consumption fell much more sharply in late 2008. The source links this timing to fear after Lehman's 15 September 2008 bankruptcy: searches for “Great Depression” peaked in October 2008, and households delayed purchases such as cars and televisions despite comparatively small immediate changes in disposable income. This is evidence consistent with a fall in autonomous consumption, not evidence that expectations were the sole cause of the downturn.[^blanchard-011]

The crisis spread internationally through two linked channels:

1. **Trade:** reduced U.S. consumption and investment lowered U.S. imports, cutting foreign exporters' demand and output.[^blanchard-tour-world]
2. **Finance:** U.S. banks repatriated funds to meet domestic needs, straining banks abroad. In several European countries, large public debts and deficits then led investors to demand higher interest rates; fiscal contraction to reduce deficits weakened demand and output further.[^blanchard-tour-world]

The source labels the latter European deterioration the **Euro Crisis**. Its Figure 1-1 shows world growth turning slightly negative in 2009 and advanced-economy growth reaching about −3.4%, while emerging and developing economies stayed positive but grew 3.5 percentage points below their 2000–07 average. Its Figure 1-2 shows U.S., euro-area, and emerging-market equity indexes all falling to roughly half, or less, of their pre-crisis peaks by the end of 2008.[^blanchard-tour-world]

## Recovery, source-era scope, and limits

The source attributes the subsequent recovery to monetary and fiscal support and gradual financial-system repair. It presents the United States as largely recovered by the start of 2019, but the euro area as still facing high unemployment and weak growth, and emerging/developing-economy growth as below its pre-crisis pace.[^blanchard-tour-world]

These are the textbook's causal narrative and source-period observations, not current macroeconomic conditions or a complete attribution of the crisis and recovery.
