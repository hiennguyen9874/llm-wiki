# Macroeconomic aggregation, the paradox of thrift, and stabilization

Macroeconomics studies economy-wide performance, not merely the addition of individual-market outcomes: interconnected decisions can amplify each other, so individually prudent cuts in spending may collectively reduce output and employment. The chapter frames Keynesian monetary and fiscal policy as tools for countering such demand shortfalls, while treating the appropriate response as a policy question rather than an automatic implication.[^krugman-ch6-036]

## Aggregate questions require a distinct frame

Microeconomics examines decisions of individual households and firms, such as a particular salary, product price, or firm's location. Macroeconomics instead examines economy-wide employment, overall prices, total spending, growth, and international flows.[^krugman-ch6-036]

Basic microeconomic tools, including supply and demand, remain necessary, but the source cautions that applying them market by market and summing the results does not by itself explain the general price level or other aggregate outcomes.[^krugman-ch6-036]

## Feedback and the paradox of thrift

The source uses a traffic jam as an analogy: individually small braking responses can cascade into a large, unintended system-wide delay. Similarly, when households and firms fear hard times and reduce spending, lower sales can lead firms to cut jobs, reducing income and spending further.[^krugman-ch6-036]

This **paradox of thrift** is the claim that a collective attempt to save more during a demand-constrained downturn can leave families and businesses worse off, despite saving being prudent for any one actor. The reverse feedback can operate when optimism raises spending, hiring, and further spending.[^krugman-ch6-036]

## Keynesian stabilization claim

Before the 1930s, many economists treated unemployment as self-correcting without government intervention. The Great Depression prompted demand for a different explanation and response. The chapter characterizes Keynesian economics as diagnosing slumps as inadequate spending and supporting government action to raise overall spending through:

- **Monetary policy**: changing the quantity of money to affect interest rates and hence aggregate spending.
- **Fiscal policy**: changing government spending and taxes to affect aggregate spending.[^krugman-ch6-036]

The source contrasts early-1930s interest-rate increases, spending cuts, and tax increases—which it says deepened recession—with post-2008 rate cuts, temporary fiscal expansion, tax reductions, and bank support. It reports that most economists believe the latter active response helped avert a global catastrophe, while noting that the measures were controversial.[^krugman-ch6-036]

## Economy-wide co-movement in downturns

U.S. private-sector employment and industrial production rose substantially over 1985–2020 but not steadily. The source identifies simultaneous downturns around the early 1990s, early 2000s, late 2007, and 2020, and says the contractions were broad across sectors rather than confined to a few industries.[^krugman-ch6-036] This co-movement is evidence for treating recessions as an aggregate phenomenon.

## Closed-economy formulation

In Blanchard's short-run closed-economy model, fixed investment, government purchases, and taxes imply $I=S+(T-G)$ in goods-market equilibrium. A collective desire to save more is modeled as a fall in autonomous consumption; it lowers output enough that actual private saving is unchanged. This formalizes the paradox of thrift but is conditional on fixed investment and fiscal variables, so it does not carry unchanged to medium- or long-run saving and growth.[^blanchard-011] See [Goods-market equilibrium, Keynesian cross, and multiplier](goods-market-equilibrium-keynesian-cross-and-multiplier.md) for the model.
