# Should the Minimum Wage Be Raised to $15 — Targeting, Employment Effects, and the EITC Alternative

A $15 minimum wage — more than double the 2015 federal $7.25 and already adopted in some West Coast cities with Los Angeles considering it — is a blunt redistribution tool that poorly targets poor families, risks displacing the least-skilled through labor-labor substitution, and is dominated on targeting and employment by tax-based redistribution such as the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) and direct cash payments, according to David Neumark's 2015 assessment, complemented by BLS demographics showing minimum-wage workers are only ~2% of all workers and concentrated among young, female, less-educated, part-time, and leisure-hospitality workers[^mankiw-047].

## Context — the Fight for $15

Slogans like "Fight for 15; People Not Profits; One Job Should Be Enough" reflect worsening income inequality and persistent poverty driving national and state movements to raise the minimum wage[^mankiw-047]. Some West Coast cities had already voted to boost to $15 or more, more than double the federal standard, with Los Angeles then considering a similarly aggressive move[^mankiw-047]. Source is an LA Times May 15, 2015 op-ed by David Neumark, Chancellor's Professor of Economics at UC Irvine, reprinted in Mankiw[^mankiw-047].

## Why wages are low — market forces

Neumark argues underlying problems are technological changes that increased value of higher-skilled work and reduced value of lower-skilled work, and globalization that brought lower-skilled American workers into greater competition with counterparts abroad[^mankiw-047]. Simply requiring employers to pay $15 will not provide much ballast against these forces[^mankiw-047].

## Poor targeting of poverty

Minimum wages target low *wages* rather than low *family incomes* and are therefore ineffective at delivering benefits to poor or low-income families, with many benefits flowing to higher-income families[^mankiw-047]:

- Nearly a quarter of minimum-wage workers are teenagers who will eventually find better-paid jobs[^mankiw-047].
- Most poor families have no workers at all[^mankiw-047].
- For every $5 in higher wages that a higher minimum imposes on employers, only about $1 goes to poor families, whereas roughly twice as much goes to families with incomes above the median[^mankiw-047].

This parallels Mankiw's earlier note that less than a third of minimum-wage earners are in families below poverty — many are middle-class teenagers working part-time.

## Employment effects — least-skilled are hit hardest

### Survey of the literature

Not every one of the hundreds of studies finds negative employment effects, but Neumark compares that to existence of studies claiming humans have not contributed to climate change or that supply-side economics did not contribute to deficits — the comprehensive survey he conducted with William Wascher of the Federal Reserve System found two-thirds of studies point to negative employment effects, as do over 80% of the more credible studies[^mankiw-047].

### Labor-labor substitution

Economists use "labor-labor substitution" to describe employers responding to a higher minimum by replacing lowest-skilled workers with higher-skilled workers they are more willing to hire at the higher minimum[^mankiw-047]. Modest overall job loss may therefore mask much steeper loss among the least skilled[^mankiw-047].

Based on his research, Neumark expects a $15 minimum in Los Angeles to lead some teenagers currently focused on education to take part-time jobs at the new higher minimum and displace low-skilled workers from jobs they now hold — a bad outcome[^mankiw-047].

## Better solutions — redistribution through the tax system

Guaranteeing a minimally acceptable standard for those who work entails redistribution; minimum wage is one form but blunt[^mankiw-047]. Using the tax system is clearly better[^mankiw-047].

### Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)

The EITC targets low-income families very well; research establishes it provides generous government subsidies to their labor-market earnings and leads more people to work, which probably explains bipartisan support[^mankiw-047].

Some decry EITC as "corporate welfare" because the labor-market entry it encourages pushes down market wages, but that is precisely why it increases employment — if it did not lower wages, employers would not hire additional workers and those not hired would be more dependent on public programs[^mankiw-047].

Expansions proposed: make EITC more generous, including increasing it for those without children who are eligible only for minuscule payments[^mankiw-047].

### More radical — general cash support

More radically, consider whether all low-income families, irrespective of employment, should receive more general income support in the form of direct cash payments — a "public dividend" from the extraordinary productivity of the U.S. economy, which has permitted those at the top to earn dramatically increasing salaries while incomes at bottom stagnated[^mankiw-047].

### Financing comparison

Alternative policies would have to be financed by higher taxes, but that is a good thing: redistribution through taxes is paid for by those with highest incomes[^mankiw-047]. In contrast, higher minimum wages are paid for by those who happen to own businesses in low-wage industries and the consumers of those industries' products, who are more likely to be poor[^mankiw-047].

Neumark's political framing: progressives pushing $15 would do better channeling energy toward redistribution that harms least-skilled less and helps more; conservatives may be happier with well-designed redistribution than a high minimum floor, even though redistribution then provoked "socialism" cries — "it doesn't have to be"[^mankiw-047].

## Who earns the minimum wage — BLS demographics box

Mankiw's surrounding text adds BLS characterization of workers at or below the federal minimum (complements Neumark argument)[^mankiw-047]:

| Characteristic | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Overall incidence** | Among hourly paid workers, about 4% reported wages at or below prevailing federal minimum; thus directly affects about 2% of all workers[^mankiw-047] |
| **Gender** | 5% of women paid by hour at/below minimum vs 3% of men[^mankiw-047] |
| **Age** | ~15% of employed teenagers (16–19) paid by hour at/below minimum vs 3% of hourly workers age 25+[^mankiw-047] |
| **Education (16+)** | ~7% without high-school diploma vs ~4% with high-school diploma (no college) vs ~2% with college degree[^mankiw-047] |
| **Hours** | 10% of part-time workers (<35 hrs/week) at/below minimum vs 2% of full-time[^mankiw-047] |
| **Industry** | Highest proportion leisure and hospitality 18%; over half of all at/below-minimum workers employed there, primarily in food services and drinking places; tips supplement hourly wages[^mankiw-047] |
| **Time trend** | Share of hourly workers at/below minimum trended down 13% in 1979 (regular collection began) to 2% in 2006, then rose to 4% in 2014 partly due to legislated increase $5.15 (2006) to $7.25 (2014)[^mankiw-047] |
